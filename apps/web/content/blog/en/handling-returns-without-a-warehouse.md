There's a padded envelope by your front door that arrived on Tuesday. Inside is a dress, no note, no order number, and you have three customers who mentioned returning something in the last fortnight. You don't know whose it is, whether you've already refunded it, or where to put it.

Returns from a flat are a process problem, not a space problem. Three steps fix most of it: the buyer messages you before sending anything, you reply with where to send it and what to write on it, and you refund within a stated number of days of it arriving. Before any of that, do the arithmetic on whether you want the item back at all, because on a $46 dress with $9 of return postage and twenty minutes of your time, sometimes the cheapest return is the one you don't ask for.

Then keep one shelf. Not a room. One shelf, with three sections.

## Decide whether you want it back

This is the calculation almost nobody does, and it changes the whole process.

The cost of getting an item back is the return postage, the time to receive, open, inspect, clean and re-photograph it, plus the chance that it comes back unsellable. The value of getting it back is what you can honestly resell it for.

| Item | Resale value if it comes back | Cost of the return | Ask for it back? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $46 vintage dress | $46, it's one of a kind | $9 postage, 20 minutes | Yes, always |
| $12 pair of earrings | $0, hygiene | $6 postage, 10 minutes | No, refund and let them keep it |
| $28 candle, damaged | $0 | $8 postage | No, and don't make them post a broken thing |
| $95 jacket | $80 after a clean | $12 postage, 40 minutes | Yes |
| $9 lip balm | $0, opened | $5 postage | No |

The "no" rows are not generosity, they're arithmetic. Paying to receive an item you'll throw away is spending money to make yourself feel like a proper business.

The sentence that goes with a no-return refund matters, because it's easy to make it sound like you don't want the customer either:

> Don't post it back, it'd cost more than it's worth and it's no use to me once it's opened. Refund's gone through today. Keep it or pass it on to someone who'll use it.

One warning. If you always say keep it, a small number of people notice, and word travels in the places where your buyers talk to each other. Keep the no-return rule tied to the item type and its value, not to who's asking, and you'll be fine.

## The three-step process

**Step one: they message first.** This is the single line that prevents most return chaos, and it belongs in your policy, your confirmation message and the slip in the box.

> Message me before you send anything back so I know it's coming.

Without it you get anonymous parcels, parcels sent to an address you moved out of, and parcels that sit at a collection point until they go back to the sender.

**Step two: you reply with the instructions**, in one message, every time:

> Sorry it's not right. Send it to: [name], [address]. Pop a note inside with your name and what you'd like, refund or exchange. Cheapest tracked service is fine, and send me the tracking number when you've posted it. Once it lands I'll refund you within 3 working days.

Four things in there do real work. The address in full, so they don't have to find it. The note inside, which is what stops the anonymous-envelope problem. Tracked, so neither of you is guessing. And a refund promise with a number in it, which stops the "have you got it yet?" messages.

**Step three: refund on receipt, within the window you stated.** Not on inspection, not next week, not when you get round to it. Set a calendar reminder if you need one.

## Where things get posted, when you live there

Your home address is going to be on the return instructions. Three ways sellers handle that:

- **Use it anyway.** Most small sellers do. It's the simplest option and buyers who return things are rarely a threat.
- **A parcel locker, PO box or a shop-based collection point**, if your country has them. Costs a little, keeps your address private, and adds a trip for you.
- **A friend's shop or a family business**, which is common and works well, provided the person there knows what to do with a parcel that arrives.

Whatever you choose, write it identically everywhere. A return sent to an old address is a return you'll be arguing about in three weeks.

## What to do the moment a return arrives

Photograph it before you open it, then photograph the contents next to the packaging. Ten seconds, and it's your entire evidence base if there's a dispute about the condition it arrived in.

Then match it to a customer. This is why the note-inside rule exists. If there's no note, check the sender's name on the label against your recent orders, and if you still can't tell, post one line publicly or message the people who said they were returning something: "A green dress arrived today with no note, is it yours?"

Then grade it, out loud, into one of three:

1. **Back on sale as new.** Unworn, tags on, packaging intact.
2. **Sellable as seconds or sample.** A mark, a missing tag, opened but unused. Price it lower and say why in the listing.
3. **Write-off.** Worn, damaged, or hygiene-blocked.

Write the grade next to the order in whatever list you keep. Grading later never happens.

## One shelf, three sections

You don't need storage. You need three labelled areas, and they can be three shoeboxes on the same shelf.

- **Incoming**, for returns that have arrived but aren't dealt with yet. Nothing lives here longer than a week.
- **Back to stock**, for items waiting to be cleaned, re-tagged and re-listed.
- **Write-off**, which you empty once a month and photograph before you do, because those photos are what tell you which product is quietly costing you money.

The failure mode of returns in a flat is not lack of space. It's an unlabelled pile that becomes a permanent piece of furniture because deciding about it is harder than walking past it.

## Who pays for what, and how to say it

Split it into two cases and treat them completely differently. Almost every return argument is a disagreement about which case you're in.

**Your fault.** Damaged, faulty, wrong item, not as described. You pay everything, including the return postage, and you say so immediately. Ideally you don't ask for it back at all.

**Their change of mind.** They pay the return postage. This is normal, it's what most small sellers do, and it isn't unfriendly as long as it was written down before they bought.

Then say which case you think it is, early and without an accusation:

> Just so I get this right, is it damaged or not what was described, or is it fine and just not right for you? Both are fine, I handle them a bit differently.

Where sellers get into trouble is offering free returns to be nice, and then resenting it at the fourth one. The policy question, including the five answers your policy has to give, is in [writing a refund policy people trust](/en/blog/writing-a-refund-policy-people-trust), and most of the returns you'll never have to process are prevented by the work in [setting expectations before the sale](/en/blog/setting-expectations-before-the-sale).

## Refunding, rail by rail

The money going back is where a smooth return becomes a mess, because it works differently on every rail you take.

- **Card.** Refund in your own Stripe account. Tell the buyer it may take a few days to appear on their statement, because otherwise they'll message you twice while it's in flight. Check your own Stripe fee schedule for what does and doesn't come back to you on a refund, before you promise anything free on a low-value order.
- **Bank transfer.** You send it back manually, to the account it came from. Screenshot it. Never send a refund to a different account, however good the explanation, because that request is a well-worn scam shape.
- **Cash on delivery.** There's no rail to reverse. You'll need bank details from the buyer, and you should ask for them in the same conversation as the original order rather than starting a fresh thread with a stranger.

The full mechanics, including partial refunds and what to do when the buyer has closed the account, are in [refunding on a manual payment rail](/en/blog/refunding-on-a-manual-payment-rail).

## The rules you have to look up

Many countries give buyers who bought at a distance a right to cancel within a set period, on top of whatever you offer, and often with rules about who pays return postage and how quickly you must refund. Some categories are excluded, typically made-to-order goods, perishables and digital files once downloaded.

I'm not putting a number of days in this article, because it differs by country and by what you sell, and a confidently wrong number is worse than none. Search your own country plus "consumer contracts" or "distance selling" or "online returns", read the government page rather than a blog, and write your policy on top of it. Your policy can be more generous than the law. It can't be less.

## The return that never arrives

It happens. They posted it, tracking shows nothing, and you're now arbitrating between a customer who wants their money and a courier who won't talk to you because you're not the sender.

The rule to set in advance, in your instructions: the return is the buyer's responsibility until it reaches you, and that's why you ask for tracked postage. Say it kindly and say it up front, not on day nine of a missing return.

In practice, if tracking shows it was collected and then vanished, refund it. The customer did what you asked and the loss is between you and the postal system. If there's no tracking at all because they sent it the cheapest way after you asked them not to, that's the conversation the instruction line exists to make possible.

## Track the reason, not just the count

One column in your order list: reason for return. Six words maximum.

After thirty returns you'll have something more useful than any analytics dashboard, because the reasons cluster. Size, colour, arrived damaged, changed mind, late so no longer needed, ordered by mistake.

Size and colour are fixable with sentences, and the fix is measurements on the listing and an honest note about how the colour photographs. If sizing is your top reason, [how to handle sizes and variants](/en/blog/how-to-handle-sizes-and-variants) is where to start.

Damage is fixable with packing. Late is fixable with an honest dispatch time. Changed mind is mostly not fixable and mostly fine, and if that's your biggest bucket, you're doing well.

## The buyer who returns everything

Track a return rate per customer as well as overall. Most sellers never notice this because the numbers are small, but the pattern is unmistakable when it appears: one person, four orders, three returns, always after the weekend.

You're allowed to stop serving that person. Do it without drama and without an accusation:

> I've refunded this one. I've noticed most of what you've ordered has come back, and honestly I don't think what I make is right for you, so I'd rather not take further orders. No hard feelings.

That's a rare message and you should not enjoy writing it. But a serial returner in a one-person shop isn't a cost of doing business, they're an unpaid part-time job.

## Worked example: Rachel, thrifted dresses, Columbus

Rachel sells thrifted and mended dresses from her apartment in Columbus, Ohio. Average price $46, around 55 orders a month, mostly through Instagram, roughly half on card and half by bank transfer. Every item is one of a kind, which makes returns unusually valuable to her, since a returned dress is a dress she can sell again.

Her return rate was about 14%, which is normal for second-hand clothing and terrible for a person with one shelf. Eight parcels a month coming back, and at her worst point four of them were sitting unopened because she didn't know which order each belonged to.

What she changed, over one weekend:

1. **"Message me before you send it back" in bold** in the confirmation and printed on the slip in the box.
2. **A single saved reply** with her address, the note-inside instruction and the three-working-day refund promise.
3. **Three shoeboxes** on one shelf: incoming, back to stock, write-off.
4. **Measurements on every listing**, in inches, measured flat: chest, waist, length, plus the model's height in the photo.
5. **A reason column** in her order sheet.

The measurements did the most. Her return rate went from about 14% to about 8% in three months, and her reason column showed why: size was 11 of her first 19 returns and then almost vanished.

The thing she added that isn't obvious, and that she's slightly sheepish about: she asks for a photo of the item next to its packaging before she approves a return. It's framed as "just so I can check it's travelled okay", and it isn't an accusation of anyone. What it does is change what people send back. Her worn-once returns dropped noticeably in the month after she started asking, and she's never once had to challenge a photo.

Her arithmetic on the ones she doesn't want back: anything under $20 that's been opened, she refunds and tells them to keep it. At about two of those a month it costs her roughly $30, against maybe $12 of postage saved and forty minutes she gets back. She calls it about break-even on money and clearly positive on Saturdays.

## What Sailo does here, and what it doesn't

The order arriving written down, with the item, options, address and total, is what makes matching an anonymous parcel to a customer possible at all. That's the part that helps.

The rest is manual and there's no way around it. There's no return label to generate, no tracking of a return, nothing that tells you a parcel is on its way back. And the refund itself never runs through Sailo on a manual rail, because Sailo never touches that money: a bank transfer refund is you, in your banking app, typing the account number in by hand. On card, the charge landed in your own Stripe account, so the refund happens in Stripe. There's no native app for any of it, either.

Which is another argument for the one-shelf, three-box system. The process has to live somewhere physical, because it isn't going to live in software.

If returns are pushing you towards charging for delivery differently, the arithmetic for that is in [how to work out delivery charges](/en/blog/how-to-work-out-delivery-charges).

## Set up the shelf and the saved reply

Two jobs, about twenty minutes total.

Label three boxes: incoming, back to stock, write-off. Put them on one shelf where you'll walk past them.

Then write the returns instruction into your saved replies, with your address in it, the note-inside line and a real number of days for the refund. Add "message me before you send anything back" to your order confirmation while you're there.

The next return will arrive with a name in it, and you'll know what to do with it in about ninety seconds.
