A brand asked for your rate for "a few product shots", you said $400 because that's what you charged the last person, and three weeks later the images are on a bus shelter. You got $400. The bus shelter cost them thousands.

Three things carry almost all the money in a photography business, and none of them are the shooting. A deposit that holds the date and is genuinely non-refundable. A usage licence written in one plain sentence, because "a few product shots" for a social feed and the same shots for out-of-home advertising are different products at different prices. And a delivery method with an expiry date on it, so the wedding you shot in 2026 isn't a support request in 2031.

Get those three right and the rest of it is turning up with charged batteries.

## The deposit holds the date, and it is not refundable

A booking that isn't paid for isn't a booking. It's a note in your calendar that prevents you accepting other work.

Standard practice, and it's standard because it works:

- **Portrait or product sessions:** a flat deposit, somewhere between $75 and $150, or 30% of the fee.
- **Weddings and big events:** 50% to hold the date, balance due before or on the day.
- **Multi-day commercial work:** 50% up front, and for a new client with no history, all of it up front is a reasonable ask that professionals make regularly.

The deposit is non-refundable because its job is to compensate you for the date you turned away. Say that in exactly those words, once, in the booking confirmation: "The deposit holds your date. It's non-refundable because I stop taking other bookings for that day."

What to do when someone cancels three weeks out and asks nicely: transfer the deposit to another date within a stated window, usually 90 days. You keep the client, you keep the money, and you look reasonable. What not to do: refund it because you feel bad, because you'll do that four times and then have a February with no income.

For weather-dependent outdoor work, name the rule in advance. "If it's raining hard at the shoot time we'll move it, once, at no cost." Then define hard, because everyone's definition differs and yours should be the one written down. [Taking a deposit before you start](/en/blog/taking-a-deposit-before-you-start) has the wording for the awkward part, which is asking for money from someone who hasn't seen your work in person yet.

## Usage rights are the actual product for commercial clients

This is where new photographers lose the most money, and they lose it quietly, because nothing goes wrong at the time.

A shoot fee pays for your day. A licence pays for what the client does with the pictures afterwards. Consumer clients don't need this: a family who wants photos of their kids should get broad personal use and no complications. Business clients do need it, and they expect to be asked, because their agencies ask them.

Write the licence as one line at the bottom of the quote:

> Licence: organic social media and website use, worldwide, 12 months from delivery. Paid advertising, print and out-of-home are not included and can be quoted separately.

Four variables, and you can price against each of them:

| Variable | Narrow | Broad |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where | Their own social and website | All media including paid ads and print |
| How long | 12 months | Perpetual |
| Territory | One country | Worldwide |
| Exclusivity | You can license similar work elsewhere | Exclusive to them in their category |

A shoot licensed for organic social for a year might be $600. The same shoot with perpetual all-media rights and category exclusivity might be $2,400, for identical work on an identical day. That's not a trick. It's what the images are worth to a business that's going to put money behind them, and it's why the question to ask before quoting is always the same one.

**Ask where the images will be used before you give a number.** Not after. "Where are these going to run, and for how long?" is a normal, professional question and the answer changes the quote by a factor of four.

Two related habits.

Don't say "you own the photos" if you mean "you can post them". Ownership and licence are different, and using the words loosely is how you end up with no basis to charge for the second campaign.

And get written permission from anyone recognisable in an image a client will advertise with. Commercial clients and their legal teams will ask you for it, and chasing a model who was at a shoot eight months ago is miserable. Get it signed on the day, on your phone, before anyone leaves.

## The invisible hours are where the rate really lives

An eight-hour wedding is not eight hours of work.

| Stage | Hours |
| --- | --- |
| Enquiry, calls, quoting, contract | 2 |
| Pre-shoot planning, timeline, location scout | 3 |
| Travel and setup | 2 |
| Shooting | 8 |
| Backup, ingest, culling 2,400 frames to 600 | 4 |
| Editing 600 images | 12 |
| Gallery build, delivery, follow-up, album chase | 3 |
| **Total** | **34** |

A $2,000 wedding at 34 hours is $59 an hour before gear depreciation, insurance, software, travel, and tax. That's a perfectly reasonable number for a second-year photographer and a terrible one for someone with a $9,000 kit and ten years of experience, and the point of writing it down is that most people have never done the sum.

Do the same for whatever you shoot most. A $375 family session is typically 45 minutes of shooting and about four hours of everything else. That's $83 an hour, which sounds fine until you find you can only fit six of them into an October weekend because of daylight.

The two things that move that number: culling faster and editing less. A preset that gets you 80% of the way, applied on ingest, saves more time than any other single change. And a hard number in the deliverable, "25 edited images", stops the slow creep towards editing everything.

## Delivering the gallery, and the message you get in five years

Deliver through a gallery with a download option, a PIN or password, and an expiry date. Then tell the client the expiry date twice: once in the delivery message, once in a reminder a week before it lapses.

Why the expiry matters. Without one, you're the permanent free backup service for every client you've ever had, and you will get a message in 2031 from someone whose laptop died asking for their wedding photos. Decide now what that costs. Mine would be: free for 12 months, $60 for a retrieval after that, if I still have them.

What you keep, and where:

- **Delivered JPEGs**, kept for at least two years, on two drives in two places.
- **RAWs**, kept for 12 months minimum, longer for weddings.
- **A folder per job** with the contract, the invoice, the licence line, and any releases.

Three copies, two kinds of storage, one off-site, is the old rule and it survives because hard drives fail without warning. Photographers who lose a wedding lose the client, the reviews and often the will to keep going.

On the client-facing side, two things prevent almost every complaint.

**Send three sneak peeks within 48 hours.** Not the gallery. Three good frames, edited, so they can post them. The single most common complaint about photographers is silence after the shoot, and three images fixes it for the whole three weeks.

**Promise longer than you need.** Say three weeks, deliver in ten days. Nobody has ever been annoyed by early.

Large files, tight deadlines and clients on bad connections are their own small headache, and [delivering large files to buyers](/en/blog/delivering-large-files-to-buyers) covers the practical side, including why sending a 4 GB folder over a chat app fails at the worst possible moment.

## Worked example: Tomás, Chicago, family sessions and small commercial

Tomás does two things. Family and portrait sessions at $375, and small commercial work for local businesses, mostly food and product, at a $650 half day.

The family side: $375 for a 45-minute session, 25 edited images, delivered in ten days, $100 deposit to book. October is the whole year in miniature. He shoots 22 sessions in five weekends, which is $8,250, and spends about 90 hours on them all in. The rest of the year he averages four a month.

The commercial side is where the licence question earns its keep. His $650 half day covers up to 15 finished images, licensed for organic social and web for 12 months. A restaurant that wanted the same images for a print menu, delivery app listings and paid social got quoted $1,150. They paid it without blinking, because they were spending more than that on the ad placement.

His costs: about $2,400 a year in software, insurance and drives, a lens fund he treats as a business expense, and roughly $180 a month on the car for travel he mostly doesn't bill for. He should bill for it. Everyone should bill for it.

The change that made the most difference to his year had nothing to do with photography. He added a $95 print credit to every family session and roughly a third of clients spend it, usually spending more than the credit. That's about $2,600 a year for work he'd already done.

The mistake he'd undo: in his second year he shot a product range for a skincare brand for $400 flat, no licence discussed, files handed over as a zip. The images ran for three years, including a trade stand and a magazine ad. He didn't do anything wrong, and he also didn't get paid for any of it, because nobody had written down what $400 bought.

In Nairobi, Sandra shoots events and corporate portraits at KSh 25,000 a half day, gets paid to an M-Pesa till number that clients see the registered business name on before they confirm, and takes 50% up front for anything with a date attached. Same three mechanics, different rails entirely.

## Booking, quoting and getting paid

For a photographer, the shop page is mostly a booking page with a duration, a location, a date picker and a notice period on it. Set the notice period to at least 48 hours for sessions and a week for anything you need to plan, so nobody can book you for tomorrow morning while you're on a job.

Two ways people structure the money:

**Deposit as its own item.** List the deposit at $100 as a bookable thing with the date picker on it, and invoice the balance separately before delivery. Simple, clear, and the client can see exactly what they're paying for.

**Full payment up front for small sessions.** Under about $200, just take the whole thing. The admin of chasing a balance is worth more than the flexibility.

On bank transfer, cash, WhatsApp, Instagram DM, Telegram, email and phone orders, Sailo never touches the money and takes nothing. For commercial clients who need an invoice and pay by transfer on 30-day terms, cards are irrelevant. For consumer sessions where someone books at 10pm on a Sunday, cards are most of why they book at all.

Business clients are a different animal to consumers and they'll want a proper invoice with your details on it, sometimes a purchase order number, and they'll pay when their finance run happens rather than when you'd like. [Getting paid by a business not a person](/en/blog/getting-paid-by-a-business-not-a-person) covers what to put on the invoice so it doesn't sit in someone's inbox for six weeks.

The honest limitation for photographers specifically: there's no native app. You'll be checking a browser tab, which is fine at a desk and less fine when you're standing in a field at a wedding and want to know whether the Tuesday enquiry paid their deposit. Set up email notifications and check them between things rather than expecting a push alert. And on manual rails, Sailo can't tell you a bank transfer arrived. Only your bank can, so the deposit isn't confirmed until you've looked.

## Year two, when it goes wrong

**A client asks for "all the photos, even the unedited ones".** The answer is no, and the reason is that unedited frames aren't your work. Put the number in the contract. "25 edited images delivered; unselected frames are not supplied."

**The date moves.** Deposit transfers once, within 90 days, subject to availability. Written down before it happens.

**Someone haggles after seeing the gallery.** This is why the balance is due before delivery. Once they've seen the images you've got nothing left to hold, and they know it.

**A second shooter cancels the week of a wedding.** Have two names you can call. Pay them properly and they'll answer.

**A card, a battery or a body dies mid-event.** Two bodies, dual card slots where you have them, and a backup you actually own rather than one you'd borrow. This is the one part of the job where redundancy isn't optional, because there is no reshoot for a wedding.

**A brand wants to pay in exposure.** They're offering you a thing that doesn't exist in exchange for a thing that does. Decline warmly and keep the door open, because some of them come back with a budget the following year.

## What to do next

Write your licence line today. One sentence, four variables, saved somewhere you can paste it. Then put it at the bottom of your next commercial quote and see whether anyone objects, because they won't.

Add the question "where will these be used and for how long?" to your enquiry form, above the budget question. It changes what you charge more than any price rise you'd have the nerve to make.

Then go and check your backups. Actually open the drive, actually confirm last month's job is on both copies. The day you need that to be true is the day you can't do anything about it.
