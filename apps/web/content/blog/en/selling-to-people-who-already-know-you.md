Your aunt wants a cake for Saturday. She's told you the flavour, the number of people, and the time she'll collect it, and she has not mentioned money once.

You now have about six seconds to decide something you should have decided three months ago.

Here's the whole answer. Pick your friends-and-family policy once, write it as a single sentence, and say that sentence before you agree to the order rather than after. It can be full price, a fixed friends price, or a gift. All three work. What doesn't work is deciding in the moment, per person, based on how awkward you feel, because that's how you end up doing a $65 cake for free and quietly resenting someone who genuinely thought it was a present.

Saying the price before you say yes is the entire trick. The order of those two sentences decides whether you get paid.

## Why this is the hardest selling you'll do

A stranger buying from you is a clean transaction. They want a thing, you have it, there's a price, and if they don't like the price they leave and nobody's Christmas is affected.

With people who know you, four things are tangled together at once:

- The money.
- The relationship, which existed before the business and will outlast it.
- Their sense of what they've done for you over the years.
- Your own uncertainty about whether the price is even fair, which they can smell.

That fourth one does more damage than the other three. Sellers who are confident their price is right almost never have this problem, because they say the number in a normal voice and it lands as a normal fact. If you're not sure your price is right, the conversation goes soft, and soft is where discounts happen by accident. [How to price what you make](/en/blog/how-to-price-what-you-make) is worth doing properly before you do any of this, because you cannot hold a price you don't believe.

## Three different requests that look like one

They need different answers, so tell them apart.

**The assumed freebie.** They never mention money because it hasn't occurred to them that this is a business. Common with parents, older relatives, and anyone who watched you start it as a hobby. Not malice. Genuinely not malice.

**The mates-rates ask.** "You'll do me a deal though, right?" They know it's a business. They're asking for a discount, out loud, which is much easier to deal with than the first one.

**The delayed payer.** They agree to the price and then don't pay, for weeks, because there is no consequence and you will never send a stranger's chasing message to your cousin. This is the most expensive of the three and the one nobody warns you about.

## Decide the policy once

Write one sentence. Any of these is a defensible policy:

> "Friends and family pay the same as everyone else. I'd rather give people things than discount them."

> "Family gets 20% off, once a year, and I need a week's notice."

> "Anyone in my house is free. Everyone else is full price."

> "I don't do free work, but I do give things away when I decide to, not when I'm asked."

Pick one and use it for a year. The consistency matters more than which one you choose, because the moment your policy varies by person, the people who get less will find out, and the ones who got more will keep asking.

The version I'd argue for is full price with occasional unasked-for gifts. It keeps the pricing intact, it means nobody's calculating, and a gift you chose to give is worth far more socially than a discount somebody extracted. A 20% discount is a transaction. A free extra jar with your sister's order because you felt like it is a story she tells.

## Say it before you say yes

The whole thing turns on sequence.

Bad sequence: "Of course, Saturday's fine, I'll do the vanilla with the berries." Then, three days later, at handover, trying to work out how to mention $65.

Good sequence, said in the same breath as agreeing:

> Saturday's fine. That size in vanilla is $65, and I'd need $30 up front to hold the date. Want me to put you in?

That's not cold. Read it again. It's the same warmth, with the number in it, and it takes the awkwardness out of the transaction by putting it at the front where it costs one sentence instead of at the end where it costs a week of dread.

If they're surprised by the number, you find out now, when nothing's been baked.

## What to say to the direct ask

When someone asks for a deal outright, the kind answer is a fast, cheerful, specific no with one real thing attached.

> Honestly I keep the price the same for everyone, it's the only way I don't go mad working out who gets what. But I'll throw in the extra six cupcakes, they're already made.

Three things happening there. The refusal is framed as a rule rather than a judgement of them. It's given a reason that's about your admin rather than their worth. And it ends with a gift, which lets them leave the conversation having got something.

What to avoid: explaining your costs. "Well the ingredients alone are $22 and then there's my time..." invites a negotiation about whether your time is worth that. Your price is your price. It doesn't need a receipt.

If you do decide to discount, do it deliberately and know what it costs you, because a 20% cut on a $65 cake with $28 of ingredients removes about a third of the profit, not a fifth. [How to run a discount without losing money](/en/blog/how-to-run-a-discount-without-losing-money) does that arithmetic properly.

## Take a deposit, especially from people you love

A deposit does three things at once with friends and family, and it's the single most useful mechanism in this whole article.

It converts a vague favour into a transaction, quietly, without a conversation about it. It filters the people who were never really going to buy, and there are more of those among people who know you than among strangers. And it means that if they cancel on Thursday, you're not out the ingredients.

Ask for it in the same message as the price. Not as a separate awkward follow-up. [Taking a deposit before you start](/en/blog/taking-a-deposit-before-you-start) covers the mechanics, and with family the wording matters more than the percentage. "I'd need $30 to hold the date" is better than "I require a 50% deposit", because holding a date is an obvious thing that has to be held.

## When they ask in front of other people

The worst case. Family gathering, someone says "she'll do you one cheap", and now you're negotiating in public with an audience that's on their side.

Do not settle it there. Say one line and move it:

> Let's sort it properly on WhatsApp tomorrow, I've got the prices on my phone.

Then send the price in writing the next morning, in your normal wording. The public setting is what makes you concede. Removing the audience removes the pressure, and text gives you the chance to say the number without your voice doing something apologetic.

## Dee's cakes, in Houston

Dee makes birthday cakes out of her kitchen in Houston. An 8-inch two-layer is $65, a 10-inch is $95, delivery within about ten miles is $15, and she takes maybe twelve orders a month.

Her first year, she added it up: nine cakes given free or heavily discounted to friends and family, an average of $58 of value each, roughly $520 gone. About $230 of that was ingredients she'd paid cash for.

Worse than the money was the sequencing problem. Free cakes came with the same deadlines as paid ones, and twice she pushed a paying customer's collection time to accommodate one.

What she changed, at the start of year two:

| Situation | Old response | New response |
|---|---|---|
| Family birthday | Free, silently | Free, but only for her mother, sister and grandmother, announced once at a family dinner |
| Friend's event | "Just cover the ingredients" | Full price, plus free delivery |
| Friend-of-a-friend | Awkward guessing | Full price, deposit, same as anyone |
| Any of them, late notice | Squeezed in | "I'm full that week" is a complete sentence |

She sent one message to about fifteen people in January: "Making this the year I actually run this properly, so I'm charging everyone the same from now on. Nothing personal, and you'll all still get cake."

Two people were slightly frosty for a month. Eleven of the fifteen have since bought a cake at full price, and four of them have referred somebody.

The detail worth stealing is the announcement. Doing it once, to everyone, in advance, meant no individual ever had to be refused. Nobody had to be the person who got told no, because the rule arrived before the request did. Turning down an individual ask is ten times harder than publishing a policy, which is also most of what [when to say no to a customer](/en/blog/when-to-say-no-to-a-customer) is about.

## The delayed payer, and the only fix

Your friend agrees to $65, takes the cake, and says "I'll send it over tonight." Three weeks later you still haven't been paid, and you have now rehearsed the chasing message forty times without sending it.

The fix isn't a better chasing message. It's not letting the situation exist. Payment before handover, for everyone, with no exceptions and no interpretation. That rule protects the friendship far better than flexibility does, because the one thing guaranteed to damage a relationship is you resenting them for three weeks about $65 they forgot.

If it's already happened, send one message, once, light and specific, and then let it go:

> Hey, sorry to be that person, the cake was $65, could you send it over when you get a sec? No rush.

If they don't pay after that, write it off in your own head and change the rule for next time. Chasing twice will cost you more than $65 is worth.

## The part that's genuinely good

Almost everything above is defensive, so it's worth saying the other half plainly: people who already know you are your best early customers and it isn't close.

They buy faster, they forgive the packaging, they tell people, and they'll answer honestly when you ask what they'd change. Your first ten orders will mostly come from this group and that's not a weakness, that's how nearly every small shop starts. [Getting your first ten orders](/en/blog/getting-your-first-ten-orders) is essentially a method for asking them properly.

The only thing you need to protect is the price, because a warm circle that has learned you're cheap is a warm circle that can never become a customer base.

## One honest note on tools

None of this gets easier because you've got a shop link. Sending someone `sailo.store/yourname` instead of quoting a price by voice does help, in that the number is written down and impersonal, and impersonal is exactly what you want here.

But nothing confirms a payment for you on the manual rails. If your cousin says the transfer's sent, only your bank can tell you it arrived, and you'll be the one checking. Card payments, where the money confirms itself, carry {{fee_range}}% of the goods, which is 33 cents on a $65 cake. At twelve orders a month that subscription is the real number to weigh, not the third of a dollar.

## Write the sentence tonight

Not a policy document. One sentence, in your own words, that you could say out loud to your aunt without your voice going up at the end.

Write it in your notes app. Then, the next time somebody who knows you asks for something, say the price in the same message as the yes. That's it. That single change fixes most of this, and you'll know within two orders whether your sentence is the right one.
