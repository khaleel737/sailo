You've seen shops with photos that look expensive and assumed they own a camera. Most of them don't. A ₹12,000 phone from four years ago takes a better product photo than a ₹60,000 camera pointed at a badly lit table, and the gap between your photos and theirs is almost never the hardware.

Three things are: the light you're shooting in, the settings your phone turned on without asking, and what you do to the picture afterwards. Fix those in that order. The settings part takes about ninety seconds and it's the one nobody does.

Start here, before anything else. Wipe the lens with a cloth. Phone lenses live in pockets and against faces, and a smeared lens produces a soft, low-contrast photo that looks exactly like a bad camera. This is not a joke fix. It's the single most common reason a phone photo looks hazy.

## The three settings to turn off

Modern phones are tuned to make faces and scenery look good on a small screen. Everything they do to achieve that works against a product photo.

**AI scene detection, or whatever your phone calls it.** On many Android phones it's "AI camera", "Scene optimiser" or "AI scene enhancer". It recognises what it thinks you're photographing and pushes the colours accordingly. Point it at a green plant and the green gets greener. Point it at food and everything goes warm and saturated. That's a colour shift you didn't ask for on a product whose colour you're contractually promising. Turn it off in the camera settings and leave it off.

**Beauty mode and skin smoothing.** Obvious for selfies, less obvious for products, but it's often on for the rear camera too on phones sold in India, and it softens texture. Texture is what makes fabric look like fabric and leather look like leather. Off.

**Portrait mode, for product shots.** It blurs the background artificially by guessing where the edges of your subject are, and it guesses badly on anything with a complicated outline: jewellery, plants, handles, fringe, lace. A blurred halo around half a macramé hanger is worse than a sharp background. Portrait mode is fine for a lifestyle shot; it's wrong for the photo a buyer uses to decide.

Then turn two things on:

**The grid.** Camera settings, "Grid lines" or "Composition guides". It's how you keep the horizon level and the product centred without thinking about it, and a crooked photo is the most common giveaway on an otherwise decent shot.

**The highest resolution the camera offers**, if yours defaults to something lower to save space. You want the extra pixels because you're going to crop.

## Which lens, and how close to stand

Phones with multiple lenses default to the main one, usually labelled 1x, and that's the one you want. Two rules follow from that.

**Never use the ultra-wide (0.5x) for a product.** It distorts, badly. Anything near the edge of the frame stretches, and anything close to the lens looks enormous relative to the rest. It's a lens for rooms, not objects.

**Never use digital zoom.** If your phone offers 2x and it's a real lens, fine. If pinching to zoom gives you 3.7x, that's a crop the phone is doing for you, and doing worse than you'd do afterwards. Stand closer instead.

There's a limit on standing closer, and it's the one that catches everyone photographing small things. Most phone main cameras can't focus closer than about 8 to 10cm. Push in past that and the image goes soft in a way no editing fixes. If your product is small, back off to arm's length, take the photo, and crop in afterwards on a sharp file. A crop from a sharp photo beats a full frame that missed focus, every time.

## Lock focus and exposure, then don't move

This is the technique that separates a controlled photo from a lucky one, and hardly any small seller uses it.

Tap and hold on the part of the product you most care about being sharp. The phone locks focus and exposure there, usually with an "AE/AF Lock" or a padlock indicator. Now the camera stops re-deciding every time your hand moves or a cloud passes.

While it's locked, most phones let you slide a finger up or down to adjust brightness. Slide it down slightly. Phones consistently overexpose light-coloured products against light backgrounds, and a photo that's very slightly dark is recoverable while a photo with blown-out white is not. Once a highlight is pure white, there is no detail underneath it to bring back.

Lock it, set brightness, then shoot the whole set without unlocking. Every photo in the group will match, which is the thing that makes a catalogue look deliberate.

## Holding the phone still

Blur is the second most common flaw in phone product photos, after light, and it's usually not the phone's fault.

- Two hands. Elbows tucked into your ribs.
- Breathe out, then press.
- Use the volume button as the shutter, or the 3-second timer, so the press itself doesn't shake the phone.
- In dim light, prop the phone against a stack of books rather than trying harder to hold it steady. A stack of books is a tripod.

A ₹400 phone tripod with a clamp is the only piece of equipment on this page worth buying, and only if you shoot regularly. It pays for itself the first time you shoot forty items without your arms getting tired.

## The light, which is still the whole thing

Everything above is worth about 20% of the result. The window is the other 80%.

Daylight from the side, no other light on in the room, product about half a metre from the glass. Overcast is better than bright sun. A north-facing window is better than a south-facing one. If direct sun is coming through, tape a white dupatta or a sheet of butter paper over the glass and it becomes soft light.

Two India-specific notes that matter more than they sound.

**Tube lights are the enemy.** A white CFL or LED tube ceiling light plus daylight from a window gives you two different colour temperatures in one photo, and the phone's white balance can only pick one. You get a photo that's cold on one side and green on the other. Switch the tube off, even if the room feels dark. Especially if the room feels dark.

**Shoot in the morning during summer, not the afternoon.** In most of North India between April and June the afternoon light is harsh and the room is unbearable. 9am to 11am is soft, workable, and you'll actually do it.

## Editing: four adjustments, in this order

Edit in your phone's built-in photo editor. It's enough. Paid apps mostly give you sliders you shouldn't be touching.

1. **Crop and straighten.** Product fills most of the frame, edges square with the gridlines. Do this first, because everything else looks different once the frame changes.
2. **White balance or "warmth".** If you put a sheet of white paper in the first frame of the session, adjust until that paper is neutral white, then apply the same shift to every photo in the set.
3. **Exposure, slightly up.** Only if it needs it. Stop as soon as any white area loses its detail.
4. **Shadows, slightly lifted.** This opens up the dark side of the product without flattening it. A small amount.

Then stop.

The slider that costs money is saturation, and it costs money in a specific and predictable way. You nudge it up because the photo looks a bit flat on a small screen, the buyer receives a kurta that's noticeably duller than the picture, and either you refund them or you keep a buyer who won't order again. On a cash-on-delivery order, a colour mismatch often means a refusal at the door, which is the worst outcome available: you've paid the courier both ways and the product has been in a van for a week.

Leave saturation alone. If the colour genuinely doesn't reproduce, write it in the description: "the real shade is deeper than it looks on most screens".

## File size, and why it matters here more

A modern phone photo can be 4 to 8MB. Twenty products at six photos each is a lot of megabytes to push through a phone connection, and it's a lot for your buyer's phone to pull down at the other end.

Before you upload, resize. Most phones let you share a photo at a smaller size, or you can use any free resizer. Around 1500 pixels on the long edge is plenty for a shop page and it cuts the file to a few hundred kilobytes.

This isn't housekeeping. A buyer on a patchy 4G connection in a tier-two city waiting for six 6MB images to load is a buyer who leaves before they see your prices. Shop pages get judged in the first two seconds and most of that is image weight. ## Worked example: macramé hangers in Pune, ₹650

Priya makes macramé plant hangers in Pune and sells them at ₹650, around 45 a month, through Instagram and a WhatsApp group of about 300 people.

Her photos were taken at night on her balcony under a tube light, holding the hanger up with one hand and photographing with the other. Blurry, yellow-green, and the cotton looked grey rather than cream.

What she changed, in an hour:

- Moved to the living room window, 10am, tube light off.
- Turned off AI scene mode and portrait mode.
- Hung the macramé on a nail in a white wall, two feet from the wall so the shadow stayed soft.
- Propped the phone on a stack of books at hanger height, used the 3-second timer.
- Tap-and-hold to lock on the knot detail, slid brightness down two notches.
- Shot six: full hanger, hanger with a pot in it, close on the knots, the loop at the top, one with her hand holding it for scale, and the three sizes together.

Cost: nothing. One nail and some books.

The photograph that moved her sales was the one with a pot in it. Nearly every question she'd been getting was "which size pot fits", and once the photo showed a standard 6-inch terracotta pot sitting in the hanger with the pot size written underneath, those messages mostly stopped and her orders per week went up without any change in reach. She'd been answering that question fifteen times a week and treating it as normal.

She takes payment by UPI. Buyers scan or send to her UPI ID, it costs her nothing, needs no gateway and settles instantly, which is why she has no interest at all in card payments. Sailo has no UPI rail; the complete list is card, WhatsApp, Telegram, Instagram, email, phone, bank transfer and cash on delivery. What she does is put her UPI ID into the bank transfer instructions field with a note on what reference to use, and confirm each payment against her own UPI app. Sailo handles the catalogue and the order. Her phone handles the money. That's the honest shape of it and it suits her fine.

## What phone photos will not do

They won't produce a genuinely white background with no shadow, the sort you see on large marketplace listings. That needs a lightbox or a lot of editing, and for a handmade shop it's usually the wrong look anyway. A soft shadow says "real object photographed in a real room", and for a maker that's an asset.

They won't fix a product that photographs badly because of what it is. Very shiny metal and clear glass are genuinely hard, and the fix is technique rather than equipment: shoot at an angle where the reflection is the window rather than your face.

And they won't compensate for shooting at the wrong time of day. No setting recovers a photo taken at 9pm under a tube light. If the only free hour you have is at night, the answer isn't a better phone, it's shooting on Sunday morning instead.

## The honest limitation

There's no Sailo app. Everything you do goes through a mobile browser, so uploading a full catalogue of images is a browser upload on a phone connection, and it is exactly as slow as that sounds. Do it on wifi, in one sitting, with resized files.

And plan for the product count before you shoot. The free plan holds 20 products. If you photograph 35 things over a weekend and then find you can only list 20 without moving to Pro at $9.99 a month, that's an afternoon you don't get back. Decide what's going in the shop, then photograph that.

## What to do next

Open your camera settings right now and find the AI or scene enhancement toggle. Turn it off. Turn the grid on. That's ninety seconds and it improves every photo you take from here.

Then take one product to the best window in your home tomorrow morning, tap and hold to lock the exposure, and shoot the same item you last photographed at night. Put the two side by side. That comparison is what convinces you to redo the rest, and nothing written here will do it as effectively.
