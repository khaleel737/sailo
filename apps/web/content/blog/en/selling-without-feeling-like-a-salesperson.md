You made the thing. It's been finished for three weeks. It's still not for sale, because every version of the post you draft reads like a used car advert, and you'd rather it sat there than have people think you've changed.

Learning how to sell without being pushy is mostly about being clearer, not softer. Almost everything that reads as pushy is vagueness plus repetition: a hint, then a tease, then a story, then another hint, and no price anywhere. Say what it is, who it's for, what it costs, and what happens if the answer is no. Once, plainly. Then stop talking and let the silence sit there, which is the part that actually takes nerve.

Here's a number to start from. Amira sells perfume oil in Dubai. For eleven months she replied "DM for price" and fielded around 40 enquiries a week, converting about six. She put AED 65, AED 120 and AED 220 on a pinned post in one afternoon. Enquiries dropped to 22 a week. Orders went to fourteen.

Fewer conversations, more money, and the conversations she does have are with people who already know what it costs.

## What actually feels pushy

Be precise about this, because "I don't want to be salesy" is doing a lot of work in most people's heads and it usually means five different things.

Genuinely pushy, in the way people mean:

- **Asking someone twice after they've said no.** Once is an offer. Twice is pressure.
- **Manufactured urgency.** "Only 2 left" when there are forty. People find out.
- **Selling into a conversation somebody started for another reason.** Someone messages about their mother's operation and you mention your course.
- **Hiding the price so they have to ask.** More on this in a second, because it's the one nobody thinks of as pushy.
- **Making the no expensive.** Guilt, sulking, or the version where you go quiet with someone who's been friendly for a year.

Not pushy, despite feeling like it:

- Posting the price.
- Posting it again next week, because 90% of your followers didn't see it.
- Telling one specific person you made a thing and thought of them.
- Following up once, three days later, on a conversation they left mid-sentence.
- Saying, out loud, that this is what you do for money now.

Nearly everyone reading this is doing zero things from the first list and none of the things from the second, and has concluded they're bad at selling.

## Withholding the price is the rude thing

"DM for price" is the most common piece of small-seller behaviour in the world, and it's a tax on your buyer.

Think about what it asks. The buyer has to open a private conversation with a stranger, admit interest, wait an unknown number of hours, and then be told a number in a context where saying "oh, too expensive" now feels personal. They can't just quietly decide it's not for them. They have to reject you to a face.

So most of them don't message at all. And of the ones who do, some are only asking because it's the only way to find out, and now you've got a conversation you both have to escape from politely.

A public price does the rejecting for you, silently, with no feelings involved. It is the single kindest thing on this list.

> A price on the page lets somebody decide it's not for them without ever having to tell you.

## The four sentences

Every offer you'll ever make fits into four sentences. Write them badly first, then fix them.

1. **What it is.** Physical description, no adjectives. "A 12ml roll-on perfume oil." Not "a luxurious sensory experience."
2. **Who it's for, and by implication who it isn't.** "For people who want oud without wearing a full bottle of it to the office."
3. **What it costs and how it arrives.** "AED 120. Delivered in Dubai within two days, or posted anywhere in the UAE."
4. **The exit.** "If it's not your thing, no problem at all."

That fourth sentence is what makes the whole thing sayable. It's not a softener, it's information: it tells the reader that a no is allowed and costs nothing, which is exactly what somebody nervous about being sold to needs to hear.

Most people write sentence one and three and then wrap them in four paragraphs of throat-clearing about how they don't usually do this. Cut the throat-clearing. It reads as apology, and apology makes buyers uneasy, because if you're not sure about it, why should they be.

## Selling in a DM without the ick

DMs are where most small-seller money actually moves, especially in the Gulf, where Instagram is the shop window and WhatsApp is the till. The rules are different from a post because it's one person and they can tell.

**Answer the question they asked first.** Fully, before anything else. If they asked whether it's suitable for sensitive skin, answer that, don't pivot to the offer.

**Give the price unprompted the moment it's relevant.** Not at the end. Somebody who has to ask twice is being managed.

**Say one thing about fit, not three about features.** "The 6ml is probably the one to start with, it's AED 65 and it'll last you about two months."

**Then stop.** Do not send a follow-up in the same breath. Do not send a voice note. Do not send the payment details before they've said yes, which is a thing people do out of helpfulness and which reads as a hand held out.

**Follow up once, and make it easy to close.** Three days later: "No pressure at all, just checking whether you wanted me to put one aside or leave it." That gives them a scripted way to say no, and about a third of people take the yes instead simply because they'd forgotten.

## Worked example: Amira, Dubai

Amira blends perfume oils in a spare room in Al Quoz. About 2,700 followers, most of them in the UAE, some in Saudi and Kuwait.

**Before.** Photos of bottles, no prices. "DM for price" in the caption. Roughly 40 DMs a week, of which maybe six became orders. She spent about an hour a day on messages, most of them the same message. She dreaded opening the app.

**What she changed in one afternoon.**

| Change | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Pinned post with prices | 6ml AED 65, 12ml AED 120, set of three AED 220 |
| Delivery stated | Dubai next day, rest of UAE 2 to 3 days, flat AED 20 |
| One link in bio | The three products, prices, and a WhatsApp order button |
| A stock line | "In stock" or "back on the 14th", updated weekly |
| An exit line in her replies | "Totally fine if it's not the one" |

**After, six weeks in.** 22 enquiries a week. Fourteen orders. Average order AED 118. That's roughly AED 1,650 a week against AED 720 before, and her time in the inbox halved.

**The thing she didn't expect.** Four regular customers who used to message every few weeks now just order without saying anything. She thought she'd lost them. She'd actually removed the only obstacle between them and buying, which was having to start a conversation every time.

**And the awkward bit.** Amira takes bank transfer for anything over AED 200 and cash on delivery below it. Sailo can't see her bank account. Nothing can tell her a transfer arrived except her bank, so about twice a week she has to message somebody and ask whether they've sent it.

## The message you'll dread most

It isn't the price. It's the one four days later asking whether the transfer went through.

The only way to make that message painless is to have promised it in advance. At the point of sale, Amira says one line: "I'll confirm as soon as it lands, and if I haven't heard by Thursday I'll give you a nudge in case it bounced."

Now the nudge is a thing she said she'd do, not an accusation. The buyer is expecting it. And "in case it bounced" gives them an explanation that isn't "you didn't pay", which matters, because sometimes they genuinely did and the reference was wrong.

The wording for the harder version of this, where somebody insists they've paid and you can't find it, is in [what to say when a buyer says they paid](/en/blog/what-to-say-when-a-buyer-says-they-paid). It's the most common awkward conversation in manual selling and it's entirely scriptable.

## Repetition is not pushiness

The single biggest cause of "I posted it and nobody bought" is posting it once.

Feeds don't show everything to everyone. A post that reaches 40% of your followers is doing well. So the person who's been meaning to buy from you for a month has, statistically, probably not seen the post where you mentioned it. From inside your head you've talked about nothing else. From theirs you've mentioned it once, in March.

The trick is variety, not volume. Say the same offer eight different ways: the making of it, a question someone asked, a photo of it in use, the reason you made it, what it isn't for. Nobody experiences that as repetition. Eight identical "still available!" posts, they do.

How often is a real question with a real answer, and it's in [how often should you post about your product](/en/blog/how-often-should-you-post-about-your-product).

## Saying no is part of selling

The fastest way to stop feeling like a salesperson is to turn somebody down.

"Honestly, I don't think this is right for you. What you want is X, and I don't make it." Say that once and something changes permanently, in you more than in them. You stop being a person trying to convert everyone and start being a person with a specific thing that suits specific people.

It also has a practical effect. Buyers who were fenced-sitting move, because you've just proved that when you say something is right for someone, you mean it.

Related: decide in advance who you don't sell to. The person who wants it in three days when you need seven. The person who's asked eleven questions and hasn't mentioned money. Saying no early is not lost revenue, it's the refund you didn't have to give. There's a whole practice around this in [building trust before money moves](/en/blog/building-trust-before-money-moves).

## When it still isn't working

If you've done all this and nobody's buying, the problem has probably moved. It's rarely the tone at that point.

It's usually price, timing, proof, or a mismatch between the people who follow you and the thing you're selling. Those are diagnosable in a way that "I need to be more confident" isn't. [Why your audience does not buy](/en/blog/why-your-audience-does-not-buy) goes through the causes in order of how common they are, and the most common one is not what most people guess.

And if the discomfort is really about whether an audience your size can support any of this, that question has an arithmetic answer rather than an emotional one: [turning an audience into income](/en/blog/turning-an-audience-into-income) works through it.

## What to do this week

Write the four sentences. What it is, who it's for, what it costs and how it arrives, and the exit. Aim for under 60 words total.

Post them, with the price visible, and don't add a paragraph explaining why you're posting them.

Then send it to five specific people who have a specific reason to want it, one message at a time, each one saying why you thought of them. Include the exit line in every one.

Five is enough. If none of the five bites, you've learned something about the offer, not about your personality.

And when one of them says no, which some of them will, answer it in one line and stop. "No problem, I'll stop mentioning it" is the whole reply. No discount, no second pitch, no explaining the value again. The discount is the part that actually makes you feel like a salesperson, because it tells you and them that the first price was a negotiating position rather than what the thing costs. People who say no this month buy in March surprisingly often, and the thing that keeps that door open is having taken the no gracefully the first time.
