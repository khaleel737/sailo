You've taught the same thing eleven times. The same eight questions, the same three places people get stuck, the same worksheet you keep re-sending. Turning it into a course is obviously the move. Then you look at course platforms, and there's a monthly fee for software you'd be using at maybe 5% of its capability, before you know whether anyone will buy the thing at all.

You don't need it yet. A set of video files, a workbook, and one booked call is a course. Sell it for ₱2,500, or £75, or whatever your market carries, and find out if people want it. The software becomes worth paying for at a specific and knowable point, and that point is later than you think.

What follows is how to build the no-platform version so it doesn't feel cheap, and how to recognise the day you should leave.

## What a course is once you remove the software

Three things, and only three.

**Material in an order.** Videos, usually. Sometimes just written material with screenshots, which is faster to make, easier to update and better for anything involving software.

**Something to do.** A workbook, a template, a checklist, an exercise per module. This is the part people skip and it's the part that decides whether anyone finishes. A course with no homework is a playlist.

**A way to ask a question.** An email address you answer. A WhatsApp number. A booked call. Anything where a stuck person can reach a human.

Every feature a course platform adds sits on top of those three. Progress bars, quizzes, drip schedules, certificates, discussion boards. Useful, eventually. None of them is the course.

## Build the zip so it doesn't embarrass you

The default no-platform version is a zip file the buyer downloads. It works, and there are five details that separate a zip that feels like a product from one that feels like someone emptied a folder at you.

**Number your folders with two digits.** `01-start-here`, `02-setting-up`, `03-your-first-week`. Not `1-`, `2-`, `3-`. With single digits, `10-` sorts before `2-` on every operating system there is, and the first support message you get will be from someone who watched module 10 after module 1, found it incoherent, and quietly decided your course is bad. Two digits. Always.

**Put a `00-start-here.pdf` at the top level.** One page. What order to do things in, roughly how long each module takes, how to reach you, and the version and date. This is the file that stops the "I don't know where to begin" email.

**Export video as H.264 MP4 at 1080p.** Not 4K, not ProRes, not whatever your editor's default "high quality" setting is. A 20-minute 1080p lesson at a sensible bitrate lands somewhere around 250 to 400MB. Eight of those is three gigabytes, and three gigabytes is a download that fails halfway through on mobile data in Cebu, Lagos or anywhere else people mostly use phones. If your lessons are screen recordings, 720p is genuinely fine and roughly halves the size.

**Aim for a total under 1.5GB.** Above 2GB you should be actively cutting. Under 500MB is a delight.

**Zip it in a way that doesn't confuse people.** Zipping a folder on a Mac adds a hidden `__MACOSX` folder that shows up as junk when a Windows user unzips it. Mention it in the start-here file, one line, and nobody panics. Also: use `.zip`, never `.rar`, because half your buyers will not have anything that opens a `.rar` and the ones who do will have to go and find it.

## The version that works better in most of the world

Don't ship the videos at all. Ship a PDF.

Upload each lesson as an unlisted video on YouTube or Vimeo. Then sell a PDF, maybe 3MB, that contains the module list, the workbook, and a link to each video. The buyer downloads a tiny file in two seconds and streams the videos at whatever quality their connection can handle, on a phone, on a bus, with the player they already know.

The tradeoff is real and worth stating plainly: unlisted links get shared, and there's nothing you can do about that. But a 3GB zip also gets shared, and it gets shared as a whole folder rather than a link, which is worse. The realistic view of all of this is in [what you can and can't do about people sharing your paid files](/en/blog/stopping-people-sharing-your-paid-files), and the summary is that you're choosing between speed bumps, not between locked and unlocked.

For anyone selling into markets where mobile data is metered, this version wins on every practical measure. It also means you can fix a lesson without reissuing the product, because the link stays the same and you just replace the video.

## The booking half is what makes it worth paying for

A ₱2,500 course is a file. A ₱2,500 course plus a 45-minute call is a different product entirely, and you can charge two or three times as much for it, because the call is the thing nobody can copy.

This is where selling files and selling time stop being separate businesses. The course does the teaching at scale. The call does the part that has to be personal: looking at their actual situation and telling them what to do about it. If you sell both, [how to sell digital products and services online](/en/blog/how-to-sell-files-and-time-online) explains why the two halves need almost opposite handling.

Three rules for adding a call.

**Cap it.** Ten a month, not unlimited. Put the number on the page. A booking that eats your Tuesday is the fastest way to make a course business feel like a job you hate.

**Set the notice period to 24 hours** so nobody can book you for 7am tomorrow while you're asleep, and set the duration to the real length including your prep. The rest of the settings that protect your week are in [how to take bookings online for a small business](/en/blog/how-to-take-bookings-for-a-service).

**Sell it as a tier, not an upsell.** Course ₱2,500. Course plus one call ₱6,000. Both on the same page, at the same moment, because the comparison is doing the work. A meaningful share of buyers take the higher one, and the ones who don't now think ₱2,500 looks like a bargain.

## What you genuinely don't get

Say this to yourself out loud before you start, because these are the things people discover in month three and get angry about.

- **No progress tracking.** You will not know who watched what, or who stopped at module 2.
- **No resume-where-you-left-off.** The buyer manages their own place in the material.
- **No quizzes, no assessment, no certificate.** If your buyer needs a certificate for an employer, a licensing body, or a visa application, stop reading and go and pay for a real platform. This is not a limitation you can work around.
- **No drip.** You cannot release module 3 on day 14. Everything ships at once. For some courses that's better. For anything where pacing is the pedagogy, it's fatal.
- **No cohort, no forum, no student community.** You can run a group chat, and a group chat is not nothing, but you're running it.
- **No per-student analytics, no completion rates, no automated reminders.** You'll be doing that from your own phone or not at all.

Sailo will sell a course. It's a shop, not a learning platform, and it does not pretend to be one.

## The free plan will run out before you expect

One specific trap. Sailo's free plan caps you at {{free_products}} products. If you decide to sell your course as twelve separate module products so people can buy them individually, you've just spent more than half your catalogue on one course and you've got eight slots left for everything else you'll ever sell.

Sell the course as one product. Two or three at most, if you want tiers. Modules-as-products is a structure that makes sense on a platform built for courses and no sense at all on a shop, where every module is a line item competing for attention on your own page.

If you outgrow {{free_products}} products, Pro is ${{pro_monthly}} a month for {{pro_products}} of them. That's a fine problem to have and a cheap one to fix, but it's better to not walk into it in week one.

## Delivery, and the lag nobody mentions

On Sailo, digital files unlock on payment confirmation, and you can set a download limit and an expiry. Five downloads and 30 days is the sensible pair for a course, because people re-download courses far more than they re-download a one-page template.

The thing to be honest about: if you're taking payment by bank transfer, or into a GCash or M-Pesa number you've written into your payment instructions, then the payment confirmation is you. Sailo can't see your bank account and can't tell you the money arrived. Someone who buys your course at midnight gets it when you check your phone in the morning.

For a ₱2,500 purchase, that lag is more dangerous than it is for a ₱200 one, because the buyer has spent real money on a stranger and has nine hours to think about it. So say it on the page, above the button, in plain words: "Access is released once I've confirmed your payment. Usually within a few hours, always by 10am." Then keep to it. Card payments make it instant, and they need the Business plan at ${{business_monthly}} a month plus a connected Stripe account, with a {{fee_range}}% commission on the goods. At ₱2,500 a sale, that's a much easier trade than it is at ₱200.

## Worked example: Joy in Cebu, ₱2,500

She's a bookkeeping tutor with six years of teaching small business owners how to stop dreading their books. Eight lessons, none longer than 18 minutes, all screen recordings at 720p because they're mostly spreadsheets. Total zip: 640MB. She ships it as a zip and also as a PDF with unlisted Vimeo links, because a third of her buyers are on prepaid data.

Two tiers on the page. The course at ₱2,500. The course plus a 45-minute review call, where she goes through their actual accounts with them, at ₱6,000. She caps the calls at eight a month and says so in the description. Roughly one buyer in five takes the higher tier, and that fifth buyer contributes more revenue than the other four together.

Payment is bank transfer with her GCash details in the payment instructions. She confirms twice a day, at 9am and 8pm, and the page says so. Download limit five, expiry 30 days.

She has no idea how many people finish the course. That bothers her and there's no way around it without different software. What she does have is a workbook completion question in her follow-up email, which is a manual, partial, human substitute for an analytics dashboard, and it's enough for now.

Her pricing started at ₱1,200 and moved to ₱2,500 in month three, which roughly doubled revenue on slightly fewer sales. If you're stuck on the number, [pricing a digital product for the first time](/en/blog/pricing-a-digital-product-for-the-first-time) covers why the low first price is usually the wrong one.

## When to leave, stated plainly

Move to a real course platform when any two of these become true:

1. **You need drip.** The material only works released over time and people are getting overwhelmed by getting it all at once.
2. **A buyer needs a certificate** for an employer, a professional body, or anything official. One platform decision, made instantly.
3. **You're selling to organisations** that need multiple seats, per-seat invoicing, or an admin who can see who completed what.
4. **You're spending more than two hours a week on manual admin**: sending links, answering "where's module 4", resetting downloads, chasing payments. Two hours a week is a month a year.
5. **You have more than about a hundred active students at once** and the same five questions arrive every week with nowhere searchable to put the answers.
6. **You genuinely need to know who finished.** If your next product decision depends on completion data, you have to buy the thing that produces it.

Course platforms charge a monthly fee and some of them also take a cut of revenue, which changes the maths considerably at volume. Open their own pricing page in the week you decide rather than trusting a number from a blog, including this one. Prices move and comparison articles rot.

Until then, the platform is a cost against a business you haven't proved exists.

## Build version one this month

Record the eight lessons you've already taught eleven times, badly, at 720p, in one weekend. Write the workbook. Name the folders with two digits. Write the one-page start-here file with a version date on it.

Put it up at a price that makes you slightly uncomfortable, add the tier with a call in it at two and a half times that, cap the calls, and set the notice period to 24 hours. Then sell it to the eleven people who already asked you to teach them, and let them tell you what module nine should be.
