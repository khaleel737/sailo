Three people have typed "mine" under the same photo within ninety seconds of each other. Two of them have already DMed you. One of them has already sent the money.

You now have to disappoint somebody, and whatever you do next, they will tell people about it.

Selling in Instagram comments works, and it works better than almost anything else for reach, because every claim is engagement and every reply you leave is more of it. It breaks because a comment section is not a ledger. The fix is three things: publish the rule before you post, set a holding time short enough to matter, and write every claim down somewhere that isn't Instagram, within a minute of it arriving. Most sellers get the first two roughly right and skip the third, and the third is the one that saves you at 40 items a week.

## Why comment-claiming works at all

The mechanics are worth understanding because they explain both the appeal and the trap.

A comment is a stronger engagement signal than a like. A reply is stronger again. A conversation with multiple replies pushes the post to more people, and those people arrive to find a post where strangers are visibly competing for something, which is the oldest sales pressure there is and it still works.

So a post with 60 comments of "sold", "mine", "is this still available" outperforms a post with 200 likes, by a lot. You get distribution you didn't pay for, and urgency you didn't have to manufacture, because it's real. There genuinely is only one of that jacket.

Nothing in the rest of this article is an argument against doing it. It's an argument for doing it with a system, because the exact thing that makes it work, speed and competition, is what makes it fall apart.

## Publish the rule before you post

The rule has to exist before the first claim, and it has to be visible. In the caption, not in your bio, not in a highlight, and not in your head.

A rule that works looks like this:

> First comment wins. I reply with a link, you have 20 minutes to complete the order or it goes to the next person. Please don't DM to hold, I can't reserve anything.

Four things that does. It names the tiebreaker. It names the deadline. It tells people what "winning" gets them, which is a link, not a jacket. And it pre-refuses the DM-to-hold request, which is the single biggest source of arguments, because in the buyer's mind a DM is a claim and in your mind it isn't.

Pin the rule as your own first comment on every selling post. It takes five seconds, it survives people who skip the caption, and when there's a dispute you can point at it instead of explaining yourself.

## The double-claim problem

Two people claim the same item. This is not an edge case, it's Tuesday.

The hard part is that "first" is genuinely ambiguous, and the ambiguity is the platform's fault, not yours.

- Instagram doesn't reliably show comments oldest-first. Sorting shifts as engagement builds, so the list you're reading isn't the list in the order it was written.
- Notifications arrive out of order and get batched.
- A DM about the item can land before a comment about the item.
- Someone comments "mine" on your story reply and a different person comments on the post, and both of them are right that they said it first, in different places.

So the claim that loses is usually not the one that arrived second. It's the one you replied to second, which is a completely different thing, and the buyer can tell.

What actually reduces the damage:

**Screenshot before you reply.** Capture the comment thread the moment claims start arriving. Once you begin replying, the ordering shifts and your evidence is gone.

**One place counts, and say which.** "Comments only" is a defensible rule. "Comments or DMs, whichever I see first" is not a rule, it's a coin flip that you're going to be blamed for.

**Decide fast and say it out loud.** Reply to the winner and to the person who missed, in public, in the same minute. "Sorry Wanjiku, Grace was 30 seconds ahead, I'll post two more of these Thursday." Public and quick reads as fair. Silence reads as favouritism.

**Have something to offer the loser.** A first-refusal list is free and it works. People who missed once and got a heads-up next time are some of the most loyal customers you'll get.

## Holding time is the number that decides everything

Every seller sets this too long. Twenty-four hours feels generous. It isn't generous, it's expensive.

Think about what a hold actually costs. While one person decides, the item is invisible to everyone else, the post's momentum is dying, and the four other people who wanted it have gone to sleep or bought elsewhere. A 24-hour hold means you sell one item a day. A 20-minute hold means you can sell the same item three times in an evening if the first two don't complete.

Rough guide, by what you sell:

| What you sell | Holding time that works | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One-of-one clothing | 15 to 20 minutes | The buyer is on their phone right now, that's why they commented |
| Food, same-week delivery | 1 hour | People check with the household |
| Higher-value items, over about KSh 5,000 | 2 hours | Money often has to be moved between accounts first |
| Anything you have more than one of | No hold needed | Nobody's competing, so there's nothing to hold |

Start the clock when you reply, not when they commented. Say the time in your reply, with the actual clock time in it: "yours until 8:40pm" beats "20 minutes", because the buyer doesn't know when you replied.

And then actually enforce it. A holding time you don't enforce trains your regulars to ignore it, and the person who suffers is the fast, decisive buyer you most want to keep.

## Write the claim down within a minute

This is the step people skip and it's the whole difference between a busy Friday and a disaster.

The moment you reply to a claim, record it. Not later, not at the end of the post, within a minute, because in an hour you will not remember whether the striped shirt went to the account with the flower in the name or the other one.

The minimum record, in a notebook, a spreadsheet, a note on your phone, anywhere that isn't the comment section:

1. The item, by whatever code you use in the caption. Number your items. "Item 14" is findable; "the blue one" is not.
2. The buyer's handle, copied not retyped.
3. The time you replied, so the hold has a start.
4. The status. Claimed, link sent, paid, sent. Four states, no more.

Numbering your items pays back faster than anything else here. Once every photo has a number in the corner, claims become "14 mine" instead of "the second one from the left in the third photo", and your ledger and your comments finally agree with each other.

Once claims are written down, the order itself should live somewhere structured too, which is the same argument made about chat threads in [turning Instagram DMs into orders](/en/blog/turning-instagram-dms-into-orders).

## Grace in Nairobi: 40 items a week

Grace sells thrifted clothing from Nairobi. Pieces run KSh 800 to KSh 3,500. She posts a batch of {{free_products}} items on Tuesday evening and another on Friday, and the good ones are claimed inside four minutes.

Her worst week involved a KSh 2,200 leather jacket claimed by two women eleven seconds apart. She'd replied to the second one because Instagram showed it first. The first claimant had already sent the money to her till. She refunded, apologised, and lost both of them, because the one who got the jacket felt the whole thing was uncomfortable and the one who didn't felt cheated.

What she does now:

Every item gets a number, printed on the photo. Claims are comments only, stated in the caption and pinned as her first comment. She screenshots the comments before replying to any of them. Her hold is 20 minutes and she says the actual time in the reply.

Her reply to a winning claim is one line and a link to that item's page, where the price and the delivery options for Nairobi versus upcountry are already set. The buyer completes the order there, so the address arrives typed by them rather than dictated to her, and payment goes to her M-Pesa till. The till helps more than she expected: buyers see her registered business name on their phone before they confirm, which for a stranger buying a KSh 2,200 jacket from an Instagram account is most of the reassurance they need.

Her ledger is a spreadsheet with four columns and she fills it in while the post is still live.

The measurable change was the second sale. Items that don't complete inside 20 minutes now get reposted to the next person the same evening, and about one in six items sells to its second claimant. On 40 items a week, that's six or seven sales she used to lose to a hold she never enforced.

## Where it becomes an accounting problem

Comment selling doesn't break gradually. It breaks on the night you have a good post.

The failure looks like this. Forty claims in ten minutes. You reply to as many as you can. Some of the replies go to the wrong comment because the list moved under your thumb. Six people send money without a link. Three send the wrong amount. Two send money for an item that was already claimed. You wake up to nine payment notifications and no way to match five of them to a person.

Now you're doing forensic accounting on a Wednesday morning, and the honest answer to "did I actually make money on that post" is that you don't know.

The specific things that stop scaling:

- **Comments have no status.** You cannot mark one as paid. So the only place an order exists is your memory, until you write it down.
- **Money arrives without context.** A till or a transfer notification tells you an amount and often a name that doesn't match the handle.
- **Refunds are manual and they're social.** Every double-sale costs you the item, the fee, and some reputation.
- **You cannot go back.** There's no undo on a post that got away from you.

The threshold is lower than for DM selling, usually around 20 to 25 claimed items a week, because the claims arrive in bursts rather than spread out. What to say when the money and the order don't line up is its own problem, covered in [what to say when a buyer says they paid](/en/blog/what-to-say-when-a-buyer-says-they-paid).

## What Sailo doesn't do here

Being direct about it: Sailo does not watch your comments. There's no comment-claiming feature, no bot that replies "sold" for you, no automatic hold. Claims stay a manual, human job. What Sailo gives you is the thing you send after the claim, which is a link to a numbered item with its price, options and delivery already on it, so the buyer types their own address and the order arrives assembled instead of dictated across six comments.

## Number your items before the next post

Do these three before you post again, in this order.

Put a number on every photo. Write the rule in the caption and pin it as your first comment. Open a spreadsheet with four columns: item, handle, time replied, status.

Then post, and fill in the spreadsheet while the claims are arriving rather than afterwards. The first time you do it it'll feel like it's slowing you down. By the third post it's the reason you can take Saturday off.
