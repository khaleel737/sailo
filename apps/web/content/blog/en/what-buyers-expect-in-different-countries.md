The first order you get from another country teaches you that half of what you thought was normal was just local.

Three things vary enormously between markets: how people expect to pay, how fast they expect the parcel, and what they think happens if they don't like it. Three things don't vary at all: a price they can read without asking, a date they can hold you to, and a reply. Get the first three right for the market you're selling into and you convert. Get the last three right and you keep the customer.

Concretely: a UK buyer has 14 days from receiving the item to tell you they're cancelling, and another 14 to send it back, and you have 14 days from getting it back to refund them, under the rules on gov.uk read in August 2026. A US buyer has no equivalent general right for an online purchase. Same product, same seller, two completely different afterwards.

## The payment default is the biggest difference

If you only check one thing before selling into a market, check what people there actually use.

| Market | What most buyers reach for | What it means for you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| US | Card and card-backed wallets | They expect a checkout, and they expect it to work first time |
| UK | Card, plus bank transfer between people who know each other | A card button is close to table stakes for a stranger |
| Nigeria | Instant bank transfer | Free to run, and you confirm it yourself, daily |
| Kenya | Mobile money till number | The buyer sees your registered business name before confirming |
| Ghana | Mobile money, increasingly to a merchant shortcode | Same trust signal as Kenya, same manual confirmation |
| South Africa | EFT and PayShap, and card online | Prepay is normal, which is good for your cash flow |
| India | UPI to a plain ID, plus a lot of cash on delivery | UPI needs no gateway and no setup fee at all |
| Pakistan | Cash on delivery, plus Raast and wallets | First orders are usually COD unless you give a reason not to |
| Indonesia, Philippines, Vietnam | Cash on delivery, then wallets and QR | COD is the default expectation, not a fallback |
| Gulf | Instagram to WhatsApp, then card or cash at the door | The storefront and the checkout are two different apps |

The pattern worth noticing is that COD-heavy markets are not markets where people lack cards. They're markets where a buyer who gets scammed has no realistic way to get their money back, so they hold onto it until the box exists. That's a recourse problem, not a technology problem, and [why cash on delivery is popular in some markets and not others](/en/blog/why-cash-on-delivery-dominates-some-markets) works through what actually shifts it.

## Delivery windows, and what "fast" means where

Buyers don't compare you to other small sellers. They compare you to the fastest thing they've experienced, and that varies wildly.

A buyer in a large US or UK city has been trained by next-day and same-day retail. Three to five working days from an independent seller is accepted, but only if you said three to five working days at the point of sale. Say nothing and they will assume next week and be annoyed on Tuesday.

A buyer in Lagos, Nairobi, Accra or Dubai expects same-day or next-day inside their own city, because a dispatch rider can do it, and expects several days for anything crossing a state or emirate line.

A buyer in Jakarta or Manila expects the courier's published window and will track it obsessively. Island shipping is understood to take longer and nobody is offended by it.

A buyer anywhere ordering from another country expects a range, not a date, and will accept a wide one. What they will not accept is silence.

There's a US rule worth knowing if you sell there. Under the FTC's Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule at 16 CFR 435.2, if you don't state a shipping time, you're required to ship within 30 days of a properly completed order, and if you can't, you must offer the buyer a choice between consenting to the delay and cancelling for a prompt refund. Stating your own realistic window on the page is both easier and better business than relying on a default.

The practical version of all this is in [delivery times you can actually promise](/en/blog/delivery-times-you-can-actually-promise), and the rule of thumb is to quote the window you hit nine times out of ten, not the one you hit on a good day.

## Returns come in three different regimes

This is where sellers get caught, because the rules aren't the same shape in different places, not just different lengths.

**Statutory cooling-off.** The UK and the EU give distance buyers a right to change their mind, with no reason required. In the UK that's 14 days to notify, 14 more to return, and 14 for you to refund, and you generally have to refund standard delivery too. This exists whether or not your policy mentions it. Writing a policy that promises less doesn't override it.

**No general right, but strong convention.** In the US there's no broad federal cooling-off period for a purchase made entirely online. The FTC's Cooling-Off Rule at 16 CFR Part 429 covers sales made away from a seller's normal place of business, which is doorstep and trade-show selling, not e-commerce. But American buyers have been trained by large retailers to expect free returns, and a small seller who offers none looks worse than the law requires them to be. State law and defect rights still apply.

**Negotiated, case by case.** Across much of Africa, South Asia and Southeast Asia, returns for a small seller are a conversation rather than a process. What replaces the policy is inspection at the door. That's exactly what cash on delivery is: the buyer's return window, compressed into the thirty seconds the rider is standing there.

The practical consequence is that your returns policy has to be written for the strictest market you sell into, not the average one. If you ship to the UK at all, write to that standard.

If you're drafting one, [writing a refund policy people trust](/en/blog/writing-a-refund-policy-people-trust) has a structure that reads like a person wrote it.

## Worked example: Chinelo, beaded bags, Enugu

Chinelo makes beaded bags in Enugu and sells them at ₦28,000. Around 30 a month, almost all inside Nigeria, mostly through Instagram.

Then a Nigerian woman in Manchester saw a reel and wanted two.

The naive move is to open a converter, convert ₦28,000, and quote that. Chinelo didn't, and this is the part worth copying. She built a separate UK price from the ground up: her cost, her time, international shipping quoted properly for the actual weight, the packaging that survives a longer trip, and a margin she'd be happy with in pounds. She landed on £46 including shipping. That number has nothing to do with any exchange rate she looked at that morning, and it doesn't need to move when the rate does.

Then she changed three other things:

- She quoted a window of 10 to 16 working days, not a date, and she said so before payment rather than after.
- She wrote a returns line that matched UK expectations rather than Enugu ones: 14 days to change their mind, buyer pays return shipping, refund within 14 days of it arriving back.
- She took payment by bank transfer to a UK account belonging to a relative, with a reference, because a card button was not available to her.

That last point is the one that trips people up, so let's be direct about it.

## The rail you can offer is not always the rail they expect

Sailo's complete list of payment rails is card, WhatsApp, Telegram, Instagram, email, phone, bank transfer and cash on delivery. There is no UPI rail, no M-Pesa rail, no GCash rail, no mobile money rail of any kind. A seller can put a till number, a UPI ID or a MoMo shortcode into the bank transfer instructions field, which is free text, and confirm the payment themselves. That's a workaround, and it's a normal way to work, and it is not an integration.

The card rail runs on Stripe and only Stripe. Checking Stripe's own global availability page in August 2026: Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana and South Africa sit in an extended network served by Paystack rather than as launched Stripe countries, and Sailo doesn't support Paystack. India and Indonesia are listed as preview, which means invite rather than sign-up. Pakistan and the Philippines don't appear at all.

So if you're selling from any of those places into a market where buyers expect a card checkout, that mismatch is real and no plan upgrade solves it. The workable answer is usually the one Chinelo used: bank transfer with a clear reference, quoted properly, with the trust built before the money moves rather than after.

And card costs money even where it works. On eight international orders a month that subscription is a much bigger number than the percentage.

## What doesn't change anywhere

Three things, and they're the cheapest things on this list.

**A price on the page.** Every market on earth has buyers who leave rather than ask. A shop that answers "how much" with "DM for price" reads the same everywhere, which is as a shop that prices by how rich you look.

**A date, not a word.** The one thing that translates in every language is a specific day. Not "soon", not "shipping shortly", an actual Thursday. Sellers underestimate how much anxiety a date removes and how much a vague reassurance creates.

**A reply.** Speed of first response does more for conversion in chat-led markets than almost anything you can change about the product, and it matters more, not less, across a time zone. If you can't answer within a few hours, say on the page when you do answer.

## The five things to check before your first foreign order

Do these in order. It takes about half an hour.

1. **What do people there actually pay with?** Not what's available. What they reach for. If you can't offer it, say clearly on the page what you can offer, before they get invested.
2. **What does shipping actually cost for this item's real weight and box size?** Not an estimate. A quote. Volumetric weight will catch you once, because a big light box costs more than a small heavy one.
3. **What are the buyer's rights in their country, and do you meet them?** Write the strictest version into your policy.
4. **Who pays import duty and tax at the other end?** Decide, say so on the page, and never let a buyer find out from the courier. A parcel held at customs for a fee the buyer didn't expect is a refund and a bad review at the same time.
5. **What window can you promise nine times out of ten?** Quote that one, not your best case.

For the operational side of the first one, [selling across borders for the first time](/en/blog/selling-across-borders-for-the-first-time) covers customs paperwork, duties and what goes wrong on the way out.

## What to do next

Pick the one country outside your own that already sends you the most enquiries. You probably know which without checking.

Write four lines for that country and put them on your page: what you accept as payment, what shipping costs, what window you're promising, and who pays duty. Four lines. Nothing else.

Then send yourself a test parcel to a friend there and time it. Whatever number comes back is the window you quote from now on, and it will almost certainly be different from the one the courier's website told you.
