You've got forty pieces to photograph, no model, no budget for one, and no intention of being in the pictures yourself. That's most people selling clothes online, and it's a solvable problem.

Three methods cover almost everything: flat lay for anything soft that lies well, a hanger against a wall for anything with structure, and a stuffed or pinned shape for anything that needs a body to make sense. Add one more photograph nobody thinks of, a tape measure lying across the garment at the chest, and you'll cut your "will this fit me" messages roughly in half. A ₱350 dress in Cebu doesn't justify a photo shoot. It justifies twenty minutes of iron, hanger and window.

## Steam it first. Everything else comes second

This is the part sellers skip and it's the part that decides whether the photo looks like a shop or a wardrobe.

Creases photograph much worse than they look in person, because a camera flattens depth and a crease becomes a hard grey line with no context. A £30 blazer with fold marks across the front reads as second-hand even when it's new.

Ten minutes with a steamer, or a hot iron and a cloth, or just hanging the garment in a steamy bathroom for a quarter of an hour, does more for a clothing photo than any camera setting. If you sell second-hand, this is doubly true: [selling thrift and vintage clothes](/en/blog/selling-thrift-and-vintage-clothes) lives or dies on whether the piece looks cared-for, and steam is the cheapest way to buy that impression.

Then check, in this order, before you take a single frame:

- Collar sitting flat and symmetrical.
- Buttons done up to where they'd normally be worn.
- Zips fully up.
- Sleeves the same length as each other.
- Loose threads pulled off. Lint rolled.
- Label tucked in, unless the brand is the selling point, in which case it's a separate photo.

## Method one: flat lay

Best for: t-shirts, knitwear, anything soft, kids' clothes, sets and outfits shot together.

Lay the garment on a flat surface, shoot from directly above, and get the whole thing in frame with a little space around it. The two things that make a flat lay look professional are that the camera is genuinely overhead, not at 70 degrees, and that the garment is arranged rather than dropped.

Arranging means: shoulders square, sleeves placed deliberately (either straight down or with a single soft bend at the elbow, the same on both sides), and the body of the garment smoothed outward from the centre so it lies wide rather than bunched.

Getting truly overhead is the practical difficulty. Standing over it and leaning produces a trapezoid, and the trapezoid is what makes home flat lays look like home flat lays. Fixes, cheapest first:

1. Put the garment on the floor and stand on a chair.
2. Turn the phone's grid on and line the garment's edges up with the gridlines.
3. Use the phone's built-in level if it has one, or the crop tool's straighten function afterwards.

Surface: a plain sheet, a wooden floor, a large sheet of paper. Not a bed, because a bed sags under the garment and the shadow gives it away. Not a patterned rug.

The colour rule is the same as for any product. Light garment on a mid-toned surface, dark garment on a light one. White on white is a shape nobody can see. There's a fuller version of that logic in [what a good product photo background looks like](/en/blog/what-a-good-product-photo-background-looks-like).

## Method two: the hanger

Best for: dresses, shirts, jackets, coats, anything with a shoulder line that matters.

A wooden hanger on a plain wall, shot straight on from about chest height, is a legitimate and extremely fast method. It's what a lot of resale sellers use for everything, and buyers read it as honest rather than cheap.

Details that separate a good hanger shot from a bad one:

- **Use a proper hanger.** A thin wire hanger creates a peak in the shoulder that will not exist on a person, and it makes the shoulders look narrow. Wooden or thick plastic, matched to the garment's weight.
- **Hide the hook if you can.** A small nail or hook in the wall, with the garment hung so only the shoulders show, looks far cleaner than a hanger dangling from a door frame.
- **Get the garment away from the wall.** If it's touching, you get a hard shadow behind it. Two or three centimetres of gap softens that considerably.
- **Shoot at the garment's mid-height, not down at it.** Down-angled shots shorten everything and make a dress look like it would fit a child.

The weakness of the hanger method is that it can't show how something falls on a body. Trousers on a hanger tell you almost nothing. Which is why there's a third method.

## Method three: stuffed, pinned or worn-and-cropped

Best for: trousers, structured jackets, anything that collapses on a hanger, and anything where drape is the selling point.

**Stuffing.** Fill the garment with tissue paper, a rolled towel or a cushion until it holds the shape it would hold on a body. Don't overstuff; you want the silhouette, not a balloon. This is how a lot of secondhand denim gets photographed and it works well for anything with a defined shape.

**Pinning.** Hang the garment, then pull the fabric taut at the back and pin it out of sight. A shirt pinned at the back sits like it's being worn. Three pins, thirty seconds, and the difference is large.

**The invisible mannequin.** Wear the garment, photograph it in front of a plain wall, then crop your head out, or take two shots (one worn, one of the garment's inside collar) and combine them. Plenty of sellers do a simpler version: shoot yourself from the neck down. This isn't hiding anything, and buyers don't mind. A body in the clothes is worth a lot for drape and scale even when it's an anonymous one.

**A dress form**, if you sell enough to justify one. A basic adjustable form is a genuine upgrade, and it's the only item on this whole list that costs real money.

## The photograph nobody takes: the tape measure

Sizing is the number one cause of clothing returns, and returns are what kills a small clothing shop, because you pay postage twice and get a garment back that's been worn once.

Labels lie. A UK 12 from one brand fits like a 10 from another, and second-hand pieces from the 1990s are sized differently again. The fix is measurements, in centimetres, taken flat, and a photograph that proves them.

Lay the garment flat and shoot a tape measure across:

- Chest or bust, armpit to armpit.
- Length, shoulder seam to hem.
- Waist, flat across.
- Sleeve, shoulder seam to cuff.
- Inside leg, for anything with legs.

Say clearly that measurements are taken flat and should be doubled for the full circumference, because half your buyers will assume one and half the other. Then write them into the listing text too, since a buyer comparing against a garment they already own wants numbers they can copy, not a photo they have to squint at. That belongs in the numbers block described in [writing product descriptions that sell](/en/blog/writing-product-descriptions-that-sell).

This one photograph does more work than any styling decision on this page.

## Colour, and the returns it causes

Fabric colour is the second biggest cause of clothing returns and it's almost entirely self-inflicted.

The sequence that causes it: photograph a navy dress, notice it looks a bit flat on your screen, push the saturation and brightness up, publish. Buyer receives a dress that is visibly darker and duller than the picture. Buyer is disappointed even though the dress is exactly as described.

Shoot next to a window with every other light off, put a sheet of white paper in the first frame of the session, correct the white balance so the paper reads white, and then leave the saturation slider alone entirely. If a colour is unusual, describe it in words as well: "rust, closer to terracotta than to orange".

Where a colour genuinely can't be reproduced, say so. "Photographed in daylight. The real green is slightly deeper than it looks on most phone screens" costs you nothing and buys you the buyer who would otherwise have returned it.

## Worked example: ukay-ukay in Cebu, ₱350 a piece

Joy sells second-hand dresses in Cebu at ₱250 to ₱450, averaging ₱350, about 70 pieces a month through Facebook and Instagram, with cash on delivery for most orders.

Her problem wasn't photography quality. It was volume: 70 unique items a month, every one different, no repeat listings, and she was spending most of a day a week on photos.

What she settled on, per piece:

1. Hanger shot on a white wall, straight on. The hero.
2. Flat lay with the tape measure across the chest.
3. One close-up of the fabric or a detail (button, print, hem).
4. One close-up of any flaw. A mark, a repair, a thinning patch.

Four photographs. About three minutes a piece once she had the wall marked, the hanger hook in place and the light figured out, so around three and a half hours a month rather than four days.

The fourth photograph is the one that changed her business. Photographing the flaw and pricing it in cut her refused cash-on-delivery parcels noticeably, because the buyer who would have refused at the door had already seen the mark and either accepted it or never ordered. On COD, a refusal costs you the courier's fee both ways and gets you a garment back that's been in a van for four days. Preventing the refusal is worth far more than the sale you talked someone into.

She writes the flaw into the listing too: "small mark on the left hem, about 1cm, shown in photo 4". Buyers trust the whole listing more because of it, including the pieces with no flaw photo at all.

## Shooting a rail of forty in one session

The efficiency argument matters more than any single technique, because clothing sellers photograph more items than any other category.

Set up once, and then run the whole rail through the same spot. Hook in the wall, tape mark on the floor for where you stand, the same window at the same time of day. Steam five pieces while you shoot the previous five, so the steamer is never idle and neither are you.

Shoot in the same order every time, and name the files as you go if your phone lets you. Four shots per piece, forty pieces, and the folder makes sense afterwards because everything is in groups of four.

Do the whole rail before you edit any of it. Batch editing with the same white balance correction across a session is both faster and more consistent, and consistency across the grid is most of what makes a small shop look like a real one.

## The honest limitation

None of this stops every size return. A tape measure photograph reduces them; it doesn't eliminate them, and anyone selling clothing online eventually eats a return on a garment that was described accurately.

On the Sailo side, worth being straight about: there's no native app, so all of this uploading happens through a mobile browser. Forty pieces at four photos each is 160 images, and that's a long, dull upload on a phone connection. Do it on wifi, sitting down, in one session, and expect it to take longer than the shoot did.

## What to do next

Put a hook in a wall today. One hook, in the plainest wall nearest your best window, at roughly chest height. That single fifteen-minute job is what turns clothing photography from an event into a habit, because the setup is what you're actually avoiding.

Then photograph one piece four ways: hanger, flat lay with tape measure, fabric detail, flaw. Compare it to what's currently on your listing and decide whether it's worth doing to the other thirty-nine.

Once the photos are consistent, the next thing that leaks money on a clothing shop is options. One dress in four sizes is one product, not four, and getting that structure right stops the double-sold-item problem before it starts: [how to handle sizes and variants](/en/blog/how-to-handle-sizes-and-variants) is the next twenty minutes of work.
