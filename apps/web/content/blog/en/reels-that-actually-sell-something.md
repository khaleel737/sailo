Twenty-six thousand views, 340 saves, eleven new followers and not one order. You have made that reel. Most sellers have made it several times and concluded that Instagram is for reach, not money.

The reel was probably fine. The first second was not, and the caption was doing a job it cannot do.

Three things decide whether a reel sells. **The first second** has to contain the product, not a title card or your face saying "so". **The middle** has to show the thing being used, worn, poured, opened, worn out, rather than sitting on a table looking expensive. And **the price has to be on screen**, because the caption is not the sale: a caption is not tappable on Instagram, it truncates at roughly 125 characters in the feed, and most of the people who buy from you will never open it. ## The first second, and the half-second you should delete

A stranger scrolling gives you less time than any advice article admits. They are not deciding whether to buy. They are deciding whether to keep watching, and they make that decision before your first word finishes.

So the product is in frame at frame one. Not after the hook. Not after the logo. Not after two seconds of you walking towards the camera. If you sell perfume oil, the first thing on screen is a wrist and a roller. If you sell cakes, it is a knife going in.

Then cut the first half-second off in the edit. Almost every phone video starts with a fractional wobble as your finger leaves the record button, and that wobble sits exactly where the decision happens. Trim it. It costs four seconds per video and it is the single cheapest improvement available to you.

Three openings that reliably fail:

- **A title card.** Text on a plain background is a request for patience from someone who has none.
- **Your face, mid-sentence, product off-screen.** Fine if people know you. Fatal if they do not.
- **A slow pan across a table.** It is beautiful and it is a scroll.

## Show the product being used, not the product existing

This is the one that separates sellers who post well from sellers who sell.

A photo shows what a thing looks like. A video shows what it *does*, and what a thing does is the only reason anyone buys it. A static shot of a candle is a photograph with a frame rate. A hand lighting it, the wick catching, the light changing on a wall, that is a reason.

Concretely, per category:

| What you sell | What has to be on screen |
| --- | --- |
| Perfume, oils | The roller on skin, the wrist rubbed together, the bottle in a bag |
| Food | The cut, the pull, the steam, someone eating it |
| Clothes | Walking, sitting, the fabric moving, a real body |
| Jewellery | On a hand at arm's length, in daylight, moving |
| A service | The before and the after, in one continuous shot |
| Digital files | A finger scrolling the actual pages, not a mockup |

The version of this that sellers avoid is the one that works best: your own hands, your own kitchen or workroom, unstaged. Not because "authenticity" sells, which is a word that has been drained of meaning, but because a stranger is trying to work out whether a real person will actually post their order, and a real room answers that faster than a studio does.

The wider craft of filming a product properly, lighting and all, is in [how to shoot a product video](/en/blog/how-to-shoot-a-product-video). This article is about the selling part.

## Why the caption is not the sale

Four reasons, and every one of them is structural rather than a matter of writing better captions.

1. **It is not tappable.** An Instagram caption cannot contain a working link. You can type a URL and it renders as text. Nobody is retyping a URL from a video.
2. **It truncates.** Roughly 125 characters in the feed before "more". Anything after that is read by people who already decided.
3. **It arrives late.** The viewer watched, felt something, and started scrolling. The caption is below the decision.
4. **Reels reach strangers.** Half your viewers on a good reel have never seen you before, and a stranger does not read captions from accounts they do not know.

So the caption is for search and for the already-convinced. Write the first line as a plain, literal description in the words a buyer would type. "Oud roller oil, 12ml, AED 120, ships across the UAE" is worth more than any clever line, because Instagram's search does get used for products and the first line is most of what it has to match.

Everything you need the viewer to actually know goes on screen, in the video, and out of your mouth.

> If your reel needs the caption to make sense, it is not a reel. It is a caption with a video attached.

## The price on screen, and the objection to it

Sellers resist this. The objection is always the same: putting the price on the video means people who cannot afford it scroll past, and you lose reach.

That is exactly what it means, and it is what you want. A viewer who scrolls past your AED 120 perfume because it is AED 120 was never going to buy it, and their view was worth nothing to you. The viewer who stays is now already past the biggest objection before they have tapped anything. You have pre-qualified your own audience for free.

The cost of hiding it is a DM tab full of "how much?" and a conversion rate you cannot fix, because the price question is a friction point that happens after the desire has already started cooling.

Put it in the middle of the frame, not the bottom. The bottom of a reel is under the caption, the username and a row of buttons, and text you put there is perfectly legible in your editor and invisible in the feed. Same for the right edge, which sits under the like and share column.

Say the number out loud too. A muted viewer reads it; an unmuted one hears it; and a viewer who does neither still has it in the on-screen text for the two seconds it is up.

## Sends are the signal, and they are earned differently

Instagram's own people have said publicly that sends, someone forwarding your reel to a friend in a DM, is one of the signals weighted most heavily. Whether the exact weighting is what anyone outside the company thinks it is, the behaviour underneath is real: a reel that people send to a specific person spreads, and a reel that people merely enjoy does not.

Which changes what you film. A reel is sendable when it is about a person the sender knows. "This is the one for your sister who is always cold." "This is the birthday cake." "This is the thing you were looking for."

You cannot manufacture that, but you can stop blocking it. Reels that are entirely about you, your journey, your milestone, are not sendable, because there is nobody to send them to.

## Comment-to-DM, and where it quietly breaks

The standard mechanic right now is "comment SIZE and I'll send the link", answered by a tool that DMs everyone who comments. It works. It works well enough that it has become the default across Instagram selling, and there are two problems with it that nobody mentions until it has cost them.

First, Instagram cannot prefill a DM. When the buyer arrives in your inbox, the message is blank, and you are now six messages from an order: what colour, what size, where do I send it, what is the total, how do I pay. Every one of those is a place to lose them. This is genuinely worse than WhatsApp or Telegram, where a link can arrive with the order already typed, and it is worth knowing before you build your whole process on DMs. What to do about it is in [turning Instagram DMs into orders](/en/blog/turning-instagram-dms-into-orders).

Second, it only works while you are awake. A reel that climbs at two in the morning produces a hundred comments and no replies, and by the time you wake up the moment has passed for most of them. A link in your bio does not sleep. Use the comment mechanic on top of a bio link, never instead of one.

## Trial reels and testing without annoying your audience

Instagram has been rolling out a way to publish a reel to non-followers only, hold it for a day or two, look at how it performed, and then either push it to your followers or quietly bin it. Availability has been gated behind a follower count and the exact behaviour has moved, so check whether the option appears when you upload rather than planning around it.

If you have it, the use is not "test everything". It is one specific thing: testing a **new product's first reel** without spending your existing audience's goodwill on something that might not land. If it does numbers with strangers, it will do numbers with followers. If it does not, you have learned something for the cost of an upload.

## Layla in Dubai, AED 120 a roller

Layla blends oud-based perfume oils in Dubai. A 12ml roller at AED 120, a 6ml at AED 65, and a three-roller set at AED 300. Almost all of her orders end up on WhatsApp, which is normal across the Gulf: Instagram is the shop window and WhatsApp is the till.

Her reels used to open with a slow shot of three bottles on a marble tray, her logo fading in, then her hand reaching for one at about four seconds. Good views, lots of saves, very few orders. Her caption carried the price, the sizes and the shipping, and her DMs carried a permanent backlog of people asking about the price, the sizes and the shipping.

Four changes, over about three weeks:

1. **Frame one became a wrist and a roller**, already moving. The bottles on marble got moved to the end.
2. **AED 120 went on screen** at two seconds, in the middle of the frame, and she started saying it out loud.
3. **The first caption line** became "Oud roller oil, 12ml, AED 120, ships across the UAE" instead of a line about intention and evenings.
4. **Her bio link stopped being a menu** and became the shop page itself, with the three products, the prices and a WhatsApp order button that opens with the item and total already written.

Her view counts went *down*, noticeably, on the reels with the price on them. Her orders went from single figures a month to somewhere in the thirties. She describes it as having stopped selling to people who were never buying, which is an uncomfortable thing to admit about most of your audience and a useful thing to know.

The set at AED 300 was the surprise. It barely sold when it was a line in a caption. It sells now because it is a product on a page, next to the others, where people can see it exists.

## When a reel is the wrong format

Say the awkward thing: not everything should be a reel.

If your audience already knows you and you are announcing a restock, a Story does that better and costs you eight seconds. Stories reach the people who already trust you, carry a tappable link sticker, and disappear before they can date-stamp your shop as quiet. The whole case is in [selling through Instagram Stories](/en/blog/selling-through-instagram-stories).

If your product needs explaining rather than showing, a carousel or a long caption on a feed post will outperform a reel, because reading is a better format for reasons than fifteen seconds of video is.

And if you are making one reel a week and it is taking you three hours, that is a bad trade against three hours of replying to people who already asked to buy something. Reach is not the constraint for most small sellers. Fulfilment and follow-up are.

## Do this with your next reel

1. Trim the first half-second. Every time, before anything else.
2. Move the product into frame one, and move the pretty establishing shot to the end.
3. Put the price on screen in the middle of the frame at around two seconds, and say it.
4. Rewrite the first caption line as a flat, literal sentence with the price in it.
5. Check your bio link is a shop and not a menu. [What to put in your bio link](/en/blog/what-to-put-in-your-bio-link) covers the page itself.

Post four reels that way and compare the order count, not the view count. If views fall and orders rise, it is working, and you should keep going even though the graph looks wrong.
