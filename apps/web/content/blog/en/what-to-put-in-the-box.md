You're in a stationery shop holding branded tissue paper, a roll of ribbon and a pack of stickers, doing sums in your head about whether this is an investment or a treat for yourself.

Four things earn their place in a parcel: the product, protected; a note with the buyer's name on it; the information they'll need after they open it; and one clear reason to come back. Everything else is optional, and a fair amount of it is money you'll never see again. On a ₹650 candle, ₹40 of extras is about 6% of the price, which is roughly what a whole month of your ad spend does. On a ₹6,000 order it's nothing and you should absolutely do it.

The size of the order decides this, not what you saw someone unbox on TikTok.

## The four that earn their place

**1. The product, packed so it survives.** Obvious, and still the thing people trade away for prettiness. Tissue paper is not padding. Protect first, decorate second, and the full method is in [packaging that survives the courier](/en/blog/packaging-that-survives-the-courier).

**2. A note with their name on it.** Not a card that says "thank you for supporting a small business". A note that says their name and one specific thing.

**3. The information they need after opening.** How to use it, how to store it, how long it lasts, what's in it, and what to do if something's wrong. This is the piece that quietly prevents messages and refunds, and it's the one most sellers skip in favour of the pretty stuff.

**4. One reason to come back**, in one line, with something to act on.

If your parcel has those four, it's a good parcel. Everything after this is either how to do them well or a list of things you can skip.

## The note

Handwritten beats printed, but only if it's specific. A handwritten card with a generic message is just a printed card with worse handwriting.

Bad:

> Thank you so much for your order! It means the world to a small business like mine. Please tag me in your photos!

Good:

> Priya, thanks for this. The oud one is my favourite of the batch and it took three tries to get right, so I hope you like it. Any problems, message me on WhatsApp, I'm the one who answers. Divya

Look at what the second one has that the first doesn't: her name, a detail about the actual item, an admission of effort, and a way to reach a human. It's four lines and it takes 25 seconds.

The note that gets photographed is the one with the buyer's name and a detail only you could have known: which scent they picked, that they mentioned it was for their sister, that they ordered on the day you were baking at 5am. The generic thank-you card gets read once and goes in the bin, and it turns up in nobody's photos, ever.

Two practical notes on notes. Write them at the point of packing, when the order is in front of you, not in a batch of fifty at the weekend, because the specific detail is the whole value and you won't remember it later. And if you're doing 200 parcels a month, handwriting each one is not sustainable, so use a printed card with a handwritten name and one line added. That keeps 80% of the effect for 20% of the effort.

## The care card

This one earns its place financially, which the note only sort of does.

A care card answers the questions that would otherwise arrive as messages, and it prevents the returns that come from misuse. What goes on it depends on what you sell, but the shape is the same:

| You sell | The card says |
| --- | --- |
| Candles | Trim the wick to 5mm, first burn 2 to 3 hours, never leave unattended, burn time you can expect |
| Skincare | Full ingredients, patch test advice, how to store it, use within X months of opening, batch number |
| Clothing | Wash cold, dry flat, do not tumble, and the actual measurements of the item they bought |
| Food | Ingredients, allergens, keep refrigerated or not, best before, how to reheat |
| Plants | Light, water frequency, what the first two weeks will look like, what a dropped leaf means |
| Electronics or second-hand | What's included, what condition it's in, how to test it, your warranty promise |
| Jewellery | Metal, whether to remove for showering, how to clean it, how to store to stop tarnish |

Add one line on the bottom of every version:

> If anything's wrong with it, message me within 48 hours with a photo and I'll sort it. I'd rather fix it than have you disappointed.

That sentence turns a person who was about to leave a two-star review into a person who messages you. It's the single highest-value line of print in the whole parcel.

The plant one is worth calling out because it's the clearest example of what a care card actually does: a customer who knows the plant will drop two leaves in week one doesn't message you in week one saying the plant is dying.

## The line that gets you a second order

One line, with one action. Not a leaflet.

> Reordering is easiest here: sailo.store/divyacandles. If you tell me it's your second order I'll put something extra in.

Or the review version:

> If it's good, a review helps me more than anything else. If it isn't, message me first and let me fix it.

Note the order in that second one. Asking for the review and offering the fix in the same breath is what keeps the bad ones out of public, and it's honest rather than manipulative, because you're genuinely offering to fix it.

What about a discount code for next time? It works, with two caveats. It has to be big enough to notice, and small enough that you're not training people to wait for discounts. And it works far better with a deadline on it, because an open-ended code gets put in a drawer.

The systematic version of all this, including when to follow up outside the box, is in [how to get repeat buyers](/en/blog/how-to-get-repeat-buyers), and the review side is in [how to collect reviews that convince](/en/blog/how-to-collect-reviews-that-convince).

## What's a waste of money

Blunt list, and there'll be one on it you love.

**Branded tissue paper on low-value orders.** On a ₹6,000 order it makes the parcel feel considered. On a ₹300 order you've spent 8% of the price on paper the buyer will bin in four seconds.

**Stickers, unless people actually want your sticker.** Nobody puts a sticker on a laptop because a soap company sent them one. If your brand is something people identify with, stickers are great. If you're not sure, you're not.

**Ribbon and raffia.** Cost, weight, and it gets cut off and thrown away. The exception is genuinely gift-oriented products, where the buyer is handing the parcel straight to someone else.

**Confetti, glitter and anything loose.** It gets everywhere, some people hate it, and in a few markets it will annoy the person who has to sweep their floor because of a purchase they made.

**A printed catalogue of everything you make.** It's out of date the week you print it, and a link does the job better. Print the link, not the catalogue.

**Free samples of things nobody's asked for.** These work in one narrow case: a sample of something adjacent to what they bought, that costs you almost nothing, with a line explaining what it is. A random sachet with no explanation is landfill with your name on it.

**A thank-you card with no name on it.** It's the most common insert and the least effective. If you can't personalise it, at least make it useful by putting the care instructions on the back.

**Anything that makes the parcel heavier without adding value.** Every gram is postage, on every parcel, forever. A 15-gram card is nothing. A 60-gram booklet across 200 parcels a month might push some of them into a heavier band, and that costs more than the booklet.

## The things that aren't marketing but have to be there

These aren't optional and they're not decoration.

- **A slip identifying the order**, with the buyer's name and what's in the parcel. Especially for multi-item orders.
- **A second copy of your address inside the box.** If the outer label gets destroyed, this is the only thing between your parcel and a warehouse of unclaimed post. Costs nothing, almost nobody does it.
- **Return and contact instructions**, including "message me before you send anything back", which is the line that prevents anonymous parcels arriving at your door.
- **A receipt or invoice if the buyer needs one**, and particularly if they're a business buying from you.
- **Ingredients, allergens or batch information** where your category requires it. Cosmetics, food and anything for children commonly carry labelling requirements that vary by country and are worth looking up properly rather than copying from another seller's packaging, since plenty of small sellers are quietly non-compliant and you'd be copying their mistake.

One case to handle deliberately: gifts. If someone says the order is a present going straight to the recipient, leave the price off. A gift with an invoice showing what it cost is a small disaster, and it happens constantly because sellers automate the invoice and forget the case.

## What changes in a cash on delivery market

If most of your orders are cash on delivery, the parcel gets opened at the door in front of the buyer in a lot of cases, and they decide whether to hand over the money based on the first ten seconds.

That changes what matters. The parcel needs to open neatly rather than into a nest of crumpled paper. The item needs to be visible and obviously the thing that was ordered. And the note is doing a slightly different job, because the person reading it hasn't paid yet.

It's also an argument against loose glitter and against anything that makes the contents ambiguous. The buyer looking at your parcel on their doorstep with a rider waiting is making a fast decision, and confusion goes against you.

## Weight, cost, and the sum that settles it

Work out what your extras cost per parcel, then multiply.

| Insert | Rough cost each | Worth it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Handwritten note on a plain card | Very little, plus 25 seconds | Almost always |
| Printed care card | A few rupees or cents | Almost always, and it prevents messages |
| Branded tissue | Moderate | Order value is high, or the product is a gift |
| Sticker | Small | Your buyers actually identify with your brand |
| Sample sachet | Small, plus the product | It's adjacent to what they bought and explained |
| Ribbon | Moderate | Gifting products only |
| Printed catalogue | High | Rarely, and a link replaces it |

Then the test: if the extras cost more than about 5% of a typical order, they need to be doing measurable work. Either people mention them, photograph them, or reorder more often. If none of those are happening, that money buys more as a slightly better product or a lower delivery charge.

Every second you spend adding extras is also packing time, which has its own cost, and the way to keep that from eating your evenings is in [packing orders faster](/en/blog/packing-orders-faster).

## The photograph economy, honestly

People photograph parcels that look considered. Not expensive, considered. A neat parcel with a personal note gets photographed more than an elaborate one with generic branding.

If you want the photos, make them easy to give you: put your handle on the card, ask once, and say what you'll do with it. "Tag me if you post it and I'll share it" is a fair trade. Then actually share it, because that's the part that makes the next person bother. What to do with the photos you get is in [using customer photos to sell](/en/blog/using-customer-photos-to-sell).

What doesn't work is asking for a tag on a card in a parcel that took ninety seconds to throw together. The ask has to be earned by the parcel it's in.

## Worked example: Divya, candles, Bengaluru

Divya pours soy candles in Bengaluru, ₹650 for a standard jar and ₹1,700 for a set of three, around 90 orders a month, mostly through Instagram, paid by UPI with maybe a fifth on cash on delivery.

Her inserts started at ₹52 a parcel: branded tissue at ₹18, a printed thank-you card at ₹8, a sticker at ₹6, ribbon at ₹5, and a care card at ₹4, plus a sample sachet at ₹11. Across 90 parcels that's ₹4,680 a month, on revenue of about ₹68,000. Just under 7%.

She cut it deliberately over two months and watched what happened.

Out went the ribbon, the sticker and the branded tissue on single-jar orders. Kept the tissue for sets of three and anything over ₹1,500. Kept the care card, made it better, and added the "message me within 48 hours" line to the bottom.

Then she changed the note from a printed card to a plain card with the buyer's name and one handwritten line about their specific scent.

New cost: about ₹19 a parcel on single jars, ₹41 on gift sets. Roughly ₹2,100 a month, saving ₹2,500.

Reorders went up rather than down. She can't prove the note caused it, and says so. What she can point at is that customer photos of her parcels roughly doubled, and every single one of them shows the handwritten note, which the printed card never appeared in once.

The care card change was the measurable one. She added "trim the wick to 5mm before every burn" in larger type after realising that four of her five "it's tunnelling" complaints were wick length. Those complaints went to about one every two months.

One thing she'd tell anyone: she printed 500 cards with her shop link on them in her first month and then changed her handle six weeks later. 430 cards in a drawer. Print 100 at a time until you're sure nothing's changing.

## What Sailo does here, and what it doesn't

Nothing in the box comes from Sailo. There's no packing slip to print, no insert template, no card design, and no native app to do any of it from. The parcel is entirely your project.

Where the shop is relevant is the one line you print: a card that sends someone to sailo.store/yourname is a working reorder route with prices on it, which is more useful than a card sending them to a DM where they have to ask what things cost.

The honest limitation is the one Divya hit, and it's worth taking seriously before you order 500 of anything. Printed material outlives the link on it. If you change your handle, move platforms, or stop paying for a plan, the cards in your drawer point at nothing. Two defences: print in batches of about 100, and always put something on the card that you own outright as well, your phone number, your email or your own domain if you have one. The link is the convenience; the way to reach you is the thing that has to survive.

The other limit worth knowing: the free plan caps at 20 products, so a printed card that promises "everything I make is in the shop" needs your shop to actually be able to hold everything you make.

## Print one care card this week

Take your best seller and write the card for it: how to use it, how to store it, how long it lasts, and the line about messaging you within 48 hours if anything's wrong.

Print 50, not 500.

Then, on the next order that goes out, write the buyer's name at the top of a plain card with one specific line about their item. It'll take you 25 seconds and it's the only insert in this entire article that people photograph.
