Someone in a comment asked how much for the cardigan, you said $95 because that felt like a lot, and then you sat down and worked out it took you thirty-five hours. That's $2.71 an hour before yarn. This is the moment nearly every crochet seller has, usually about four months in, and it's the reason most of them stop.

The answer isn't a better price on the cardigan. There isn't one, at least not one your buyers will pay. The answer is that crochet has an hours problem no other craft has, and the sellers who make money at it either pick items with low hours and high repeatability, or they sell the pattern instead of the object, or they charge properly to a small number of people who understand what they're buying. Usually two of the three.

Here's the part that makes it structural rather than a pricing mistake.

## No machine crochets, and that is the whole problem

Knitting can be machined. Domestic knitting machines exist, industrial ones make most of the jumpers in the world, and a knitter who buys a mid-range machine can produce a plain panel in a fraction of the hand time.

Crochet can't be. There's no machine that makes true crochet fabric at scale. Every crocheted item on earth, including the ones on a fast fashion site for $22, was made by a pair of hands. That's a lovely fact and a brutal one, because it means your competition on price is a person being paid very little somewhere, and your only real defence is not competing on that.

So the numbers look like this, and they don't move much:

| Item | Realistic making time | Yarn cost | Price to earn $20/hr | Price the market pays |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chunky beanie, bulky yarn | 75 to 90 min | $9 | $38 | $40 to $55 |
| Scrunchie or ear warmer | 15 to 25 min | $2 | $10 | $12 to $18 |
| 20 cm amigurumi plush | 5 to 7 hours | $6 | $126 | $45 to $75 |
| Baby blanket, DK | 18 to 25 hours | $28 | $458 | $80 to $150 |
| Granny square cardigan | 30 to 45 hours | $72 | $772 | $120 to $220 |
| Written PDF pattern | 12 to 20 hours once | $0 after | Sell 40 at $7 | $5 to $9 |

Look down the last two columns. The beanie and the scrunchie are the only rows where the honest price and the market price overlap. The blanket and the cardigan are off by a factor of three to five, and no amount of better photography closes a gap that size.

That table is the article. The rest is what to do about it.

## Why materials-times-three bankrupts you specifically

Most craft pricing advice says materials times two, or times three if you're feeling brave. For a candle maker that's roughly fine, because the wax is a real share of the cost and the labour per unit is minutes.

For crochet it's catastrophic, because your material cost is almost unrelated to your time. A $9 ball of bulky yarn becomes a beanie in 80 minutes. A $28 pile of DK becomes a blanket in 22 hours. Materials times three prices the beanie at $27 and the blanket at $84, which works out at $13 an hour for the beanie and $2.55 for the blanket. Same rule, same maker, and one is five times worse than the other.

The rule you want is time first, materials second:

```
Making time × your hourly rate      A
+ Yarn and notions                  B
+ Finishing time (ends, blocking)   C
+ Admin time (photo, list, pack)    D
+ Failure and frogging allowance    E
= Cost
Cost ÷ (1 − margin)                 = Price
```

Two lines in there that people leave out and shouldn't.

**Finishing.** Weaving in ends on a granny square garment is not a rounding error. A cardigan of forty-two motifs has upward of two hundred ends, and that's three to four hours on its own. Blocking a lace shawl is an evening plus a drying day. Time it once and you'll never forget it again.

**Frogging.** You will pull back a sleeve. Everyone does. Allow 5% of your making time for it and stop treating it as a personal failing.

The full seven-cost method, including overheads and the fee on the money, is worked through in [how to price what you make](/en/blog/how-to-price-what-you-make). The crochet-specific part is that line A dominates so heavily that getting it slightly wrong swamps everything else.

## The single biggest lever is yarn weight

If you take one operational thing from this, take this one.

Yarn weight changes your hourly rate more than skill does. A beanie in bulky yarn on a 9 mm hook is about 80 minutes. The same beanie in DK on a 4 mm hook is three and a half hours, because you're making roughly four times as many stitches to cover the same area. The bulky beanie sells for $45. The DK beanie sells for $45.

That's not a small optimisation. It's a 2.6x difference in what you earn per hour for a product the buyer values identically, and often prefers, because a chunky hat looks handmade in a way a fine one doesn't.

The same logic runs through everything:

- Blankets in super bulky, on a 12 mm hook, are a viable product. Blankets in DK are a hobby.
- Amigurumi is the exception, because size is the point and fine yarn is what makes the face work. Amigurumi's escape is the pattern, not the yarn.
- Anything with drape, cardigans, shawls, tops, wants finer yarn to look right. That's exactly why those items don't work as a made-to-sell line.

Batching is the second lever and it's smaller but free. Make six beanies in a session rather than one. Weave all the ends at the end, in front of the television, as a separate job. Photograph six at once. Denise below cut her admin from twelve minutes a hat to four by listing in batches.

## Sell the pattern, not just the object

A pattern is the one product in crochet where the hours stop being a ceiling.

Writing a good one takes real work. Fifteen to twenty hours for a garment pattern, honestly, including grading it to five sizes, getting it tested by two or three other crocheters, taking step photos and laying it out. Then it costs you nothing to sell it again.

Forty sales at $7 is $280 for a fixed twenty hours, which is $14 an hour. Four hundred sales is $2,800, which is $140 an hour. The distribution is brutally uneven and most patterns sell in the low dozens, but the ones that work fund the ones that don't, and none of them require you to make another cardigan.

What separates a pattern that sells from one that doesn't:

- **Graded to at least five sizes**, with finished measurements, not just "S M L". A hand-made garment pattern that only comes in one size is a photograph, not a pattern.
- **Gauge in a stated stitch**, over 10 cm, with the hook you actually used.
- **Yardage per size**, so people can buy the right amount. Getting this wrong is the single most common complaint and it costs someone real money.
- **Tested by someone who isn't you.** You cannot proofread your own instructions. You know what you meant.
- **Both US and UK terms**, or a very loud note about which you've used. A UK double crochet is a US single crochet, and someone will make a whole sleeve in the wrong stitch before they notice.

Selling a PDF is mechanically simple and mostly a question of delivery and payment, both of which are covered in [how to sell PDFs without a website](/en/blog/how-to-sell-pdfs-without-a-website).

Worth saying plainly: patterns get shared. In sewing and crochet communities they get shared in Facebook groups within a week of release. Putting the buyer's email on the PDF and setting a download limit reduces it, and [stopping people sharing your paid files](/en/blog/stopping-people-sharing-your-paid-files) covers what actually helps. You will not stop it entirely, and pricing at $7 rather than $27 is part of why most designers don't try.

## Sizing is where the money leaks out

A crocheted garment has no size label from a factory. It has whatever you made, and the buyer has whatever they imagine "medium" means.

Take three measurements before you start anything fitted, and take them from the buyer rather than from a size chart:

1. **Bust or chest**, around the fullest part, tape snug not tight.
2. **Length from the shoulder seam** to where they want the hem to sit.
3. **Sleeve length** from shoulder point to wrist.

Then tell them the finished measurements of what you'll make, including the ease. "This will measure 108 cm around the chest, which is 10 cm of positive ease on your 98 cm measurement, so it'll sit loose like the photo." That one sentence prevents most of the arguments, because "it doesn't fit" almost always means "it doesn't fit how I pictured", and a number in centimetres beats a picture.

For anything not fitted, publish flat measurements in the listing. Hat circumference unstretched and stretched. Blanket dimensions. Plush height sitting, not standing, because sitting is how they photograph and standing is how they measure.

Put the same three numbers in every listing, in the same order, so that a buyer comparing two of your hats is comparing like with like rather than guessing.

Two more failure modes that only show up once you've shipped a few.

**Dye lots.** Buy all the yarn for a project at once and check the lot number on every ball. Running out with two motifs to go and buying a replacement ball six weeks later gives you a visible stripe in a colour that looks identical in the shop and doesn't in daylight. There is no fix after the fact. You remake it.

**Washing.** Someone will put your wool cardigan in a machine at 40 degrees and it will come back child-sized, and they will genuinely believe you sold them a faulty item. Two defences. Put a care card in the box with the words "hand wash cold, dry flat" on it, and put the same words in the listing above the fold. And for anything for a baby, a man, or a gift where you don't know the recipient, use superwash or a good acrylic. A blanket for a newborn that can't go in a machine is a blanket that gets used twice.

## Worked example: Denise, chunky beanies, Baltimore

Denise crocheted for eleven years before she sold anything. Her first six months of selling were cardigans and blankets, and she made about $340 total, which she worked out afterwards was $4.10 an hour.

She now sells one product. A chunky ribbed beanie in a merino and acrylic blend, seven colours, $48.

Her numbers per hat: $9.60 of yarn, 80 minutes of making, 6 minutes of weaving and blocking, 4 minutes of admin because she lists and photographs in batches of six. That's 90 minutes at a $24 hourly rate, so $36 of labour, plus $9.60 yarn, plus $1.40 for a card, a tag and a poly mailer. Cost $47. She sells at $48, which is not enough, and she knows it.

So she did two things. She raised to $55 in September and lost roughly one buyer in nine, which meant more money for less work. And she added a pom-pom option at $8 that takes her four minutes, which about a third of buyers take. Her effective revenue per hat is now $57.60 for 94 minutes, which is $36 an hour of making time with materials already out.

She makes 30 to 40 hats between September and December and almost none the rest of the year. That's the honest shape of the business. In spring she sells a $7 pattern for the same hat, which did 214 sales last year and took her sixteen hours to write.

The interesting part is what she stopped. She still gets asked for cardigans about twice a month. She quotes $420, in a friendly message, with the hours written out. About one person a year says yes, and that person is delighted, and Denise enjoys making it. Everyone else says thanks and buys a hat.

Across in Cebu, Rhea sells amigurumi at ₱650 for a 15 cm plush, five to six hours each, ₱180 of yarn. She's making around ₱80 an hour, which she's clear-eyed about, and her actual business is a ₱400 pattern bundle and a small paid community. The objects are the marketing.

## Getting paid without giving away the margin

Most crochet orders start as a message. Someone sees a hat on a story, asks if the green one's still there, and you're now in a conversation that has to end with money.

For made-to-order work, take the money before you start. Not a deposit, the whole thing, for anything under about $80. A custom hat in someone's college colours cannot be sold to anyone else, and a maker who spends two evenings on a piece and then gets ghosted has lost the yarn and the time. For bigger commissions, half up front and half before it ships. [Getting paid for a custom order](/en/blog/getting-paid-for-a-custom-order) has the wording that makes that feel normal rather than suspicious.

On the rails themselves: bank transfer, cash, WhatsApp, Instagram DM, Telegram, email and phone orders cost you nothing through Sailo, because Sailo never touches that money. Card is {{fee_range}}% of the goods after discount, excluding delivery and tax, which on a $48 hat is 24 cents, and card needs a Stripe account cleared for charges.

The honest limitation, and it bites pattern sellers specifically. On manual rails you confirm payment yourself. That's fine for a hat you're going to post on Thursday. It's genuinely bad for a $7 PDF, because someone in another time zone pays at 1am and sits waiting for a file until you wake up, and half of them will have messaged you twice by then. If patterns become a real part of your income, that's the moment card starts paying for itself, and not before. Sailo can't tell you a transfer landed either. Only your bank can.

## What to do next

Time yourself making one of whatever you make most, at a normal pace, with a stopwatch. Not your fastest. Include the ends. Almost every crochet seller underestimates their own making time by about a third, and that single number is the missing input in every price on your page.

Then take the table at the top of this article and fill in your own row. If the honest price is more than about 30% above what your market pays, that item is a gift, not a product, and you should either make it in a heavier yarn, write it up as a pattern, or keep making it for people you love and sell something else.

Pick the one item where your hours and the market actually meet. Make six of them in a batch this week. Photograph them together against a plain wall using the flat-lay method in [photographing clothes without a model](/en/blog/photographing-clothes-without-a-model), put them on one link with the flat measurements in the description, and post it once.
