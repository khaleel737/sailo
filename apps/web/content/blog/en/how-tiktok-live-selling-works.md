You watched someone sell forty pieces of preloved clothing in two hours, on a phone, from their living room, and you thought: I could do that.

You probably could. The selling is the easy half. The half nobody films is what happens between the stream ending and the parcels going out, and that's where live sellers either build a business or quietly burn out.

Live selling on TikTok works like this: you go live, hold up items one at a time, say the price out loud, and viewers claim them in the comments. Then you convert those claims into real orders with addresses and payment, by hand, afterwards. To go live at all you generally need around 1,000 followers and to be 18 or over, though the threshold varies by region. Everything else in this article is about the second half, because that's the part that decides whether you can do it twice a week for a year.

## What you need before your first live

The access requirements first, since they stop people at the door.

TikTok has long gated LIVE behind a follower count, commonly 1,000, and it can differ by region. You must be 18 or older to host, and 18 or older to send or receive gifts, with some countries setting it higher. Your account needs to be in good standing. If you meet all of that and the LIVE button still isn't there, it's usually a rollout or a region thing rather than something you did.

The physical setup matters more than people expect, and it's cheap:

- **A phone on a tripod at chest height.** Not held. Three hours of holding a phone is not a plan.
- **Charging while you stream.** A phone streaming video for two hours gets hot and drains fast, and a hot phone drops the stream. Plugged in, always.
- **Wired internet or the strongest signal in the house.** Streaming needs upload speed, which is the half of your connection you never think about. A dropped stream doesn't pause your audience, it disperses them.
- **A second device for comments.** You cannot read a fast comment stream on the same screen you're using to show product.
- **Light in front of you, not behind.** A window at midday, or a ring light. Backlight makes you a silhouette selling shadows.
- **Items numbered and laid out in reach.** Write numbers on tags. "Item 14" is faster than "the blue one, no, the other blue one."

## The claim problem, which is the whole game

Every live seller runs on the same mechanic, and it's the reason live selling is chaotic.

A viewer types "mine" or "mine 14" in the comments. You say sold. You move on. Twenty minutes later you have no idea who claimed item 14, because the comment scrolled away four hundred comments ago.

The comment stream moves faster than you can read, and it moves fastest exactly when things are going well. So decide your rule before you go live, and say it out loud every twenty minutes:

> First "mine" gets it. If you don't pay within the hour after the live, it goes to the next person. No reservations.

That single sentence, repeated, prevents most of what goes wrong. It sets the claim rule, it sets a deadline, and it tells the second claimant they have a real chance, which keeps them watching.

Three ways sellers actually capture claims, in increasing order of how well they hold up:

1. **Screenshot as you go.** You or a helper screenshots the comment when someone claims. Fine up to about 30 viewers, useless above it.
2. **A helper typing claims into a note.** Item number, username, time. This is what most working live sellers do, and it's why live selling is usually two people.
3. **Recording the live and reviewing after.** The safety net for disputes. Save every stream and keep it for a week.

The two-person thing is worth stating plainly, because guides skip it. One person sells and never looks at the comments. One person reads comments, calls out claims and names, and keeps the list. Solo live selling works at small scale and breaks the moment you get busy, which is a cruel design: it fails exactly when it's working.

## The rhythm that keeps people watching

Live selling has a tempo, and it's faster than feels natural.

For each item, roughly 60 to 90 seconds: hold it up, say what it is, say the size or the detail people will ask about, say the price, say the claim word, count down from five, sold. Move on.

What kills a live is dead air while you look for something, and the second thing that kills it is answering one person's question for four minutes while a hundred others wait.

A few things that hold a room:

- **Say the price every time, for every item, even if it's on screen.** People arrive constantly. The person who joined 20 seconds ago has no idea what's happening.
- **Re-introduce yourself every ten minutes.** Name, what you sell, how delivery works, how to pay. It feels ridiculous and it's the single highest-value repetition in the stream.
- **Keep one desirable item back for later.** Announce it early, show it at the end. It's the only reason someone stays for hour three.
- **Announce the live in advance.** A post the day before and a story an hour before doubles who's there at the start, and the start is when TikTok decides whether to send you more people.

Same day, same time, every week. Live selling is a habit business. People plan their evening around it, and they can't do that if you're unpredictable.

## Joy in Davao: two lives a week, ₱250 average

Joy sells preloved clothing in Davao. Bale-opening lives on Wednesday and Sunday evenings, roughly three hours each, average piece around ₱250, some pieces at ₱120 and a few branded ones at ₱600.

A typical Sunday live gets her 60 to 80 claims. That's the number that sounds like a business and isn't yet.

Here's what her actual Sunday night looks like after she stops streaming at 10pm.

She and her sister go through the claims list: 71 claims, item numbers and usernames. They post the list, then message each claimant with the item, the price, the delivery options and the total. Some are in Davao and will meet up. Most are provincial and want cash on delivery through a courier, and a handful pay GCash straight away.

By Monday morning, of the 71 claims, about 52 have confirmed with an address. By Monday night, 44 are packed. The other 27 are a mixture of people who changed their mind, people who never replied, and two who claimed six items each and went silent.

That ratio is the thing nobody tells beginners. Roughly six or seven in ten claims turn into a shipped parcel, and if you plan your stock and your evening around 71 you will be wrong every single week.

What changed it for her, in order of how much each helped:

1. **Saying the payment deadline out loud during the live.** Claims dropped slightly. Paid orders went up.
2. **Putting the whole live's stock on a page with prices.** She now says "everything from tonight is on joysukay dot sailo dot store" and posts the link in a pinned comment. People who claimed can fill in their own address and delivery choice instead of her typing five messages each.
3. **A ₱50 down payment for anyone claiming more than three items.** The six-item claimers stopped.

The page didn't sell anything by itself. What it did was move the address collection off her thumbs and onto the buyer's, which cut her Monday from about four hours to about ninety minutes.

## Cash on delivery is normal here, and it changes your maths

Across the Philippines and Indonesia, cash on delivery isn't a fallback for nervous buyers. It's the default, and a live seller who refuses it is refusing most of the room.

What that means practically: you pack, you pay the courier, and you find out whether you were paid when the parcel is accepted or refused. A refused parcel costs you shipping twice and the item comes back creased.

Two habits that cut refusals, both boring:

- **Message the buyer the day before delivery.** Not to be polite. A buyer who confirms they'll be home refuses far less often than one who's surprised by a rider.
- **Never ship to someone who claimed six items and paid for none.** You already know how that ends.

## Gifts, and why they're not your business model

TikTok LIVE has gifts: viewers send virtual items that convert to money. It's real, and hosts do earn from it.

It is not a plan for a seller. Gift income is concentrated among people who stream for many hours a day and build a room around entertainment rather than product. If you're selling ₱250 dresses for three hours twice a week, your income is the dresses. Treat gifts as a nice surprise and never as a reason to stretch a stream to five hours.

The related discipline: TikTok expects commercial content to be declared, and the rules around promotional streams have tightened over time. Check what your region's settings ask for before you stream a selling session, rather than discovering it through an enforcement action.

## What goes wrong, in order of how often

From most common to least:

1. **Claims you can't match to a person.** The comment scrolled. Fixed by a helper and a written list.
2. **Two people claiming the same item.** Fixed by saying the rule out loud, repeatedly.
3. **The stream dropping at the busiest moment.** Fixed by internet and a charged, cool phone.
4. **Selling something you already sold.** Fixed by physically moving sold items into a separate box, immediately, on camera.
5. **Running out of energy at hour two.** Fixed by shorter lives, twice a week, rather than one marathon.
6. **A price you regret.** Fixed by writing your floor price on the tag before you go live, where the camera can't see it.

Number four is the one that costs you a customer rather than an hour. A physical box for sold items, and nothing goes back in the pile. It's low-tech and it works better than any list.

## What Sailo does not do here

Plainly: Sailo has nothing to do with your live. There's no overlay, no comment capture, no way for a "mine" in a TikTok chat to become an order by itself. Nobody's product does that reliably across platforms, and anyone promising it should be asked to demonstrate it on a stream with 400 comments a minute.

What a shop page does is hold the prices and take the order after the fact, so you're sending one link instead of typing five messages per claimant.

## Before your first live

1. Check whether the LIVE button exists on your account. If not, that's your first month's goal, not a strategy problem.
2. Number twenty items and write the prices on the tags where the camera can't see.
3. Find one person to read comments. A sibling, a friend, anyone.
4. Write your claim rule as one sentence and practise saying it.
5. Announce a time, and go live at that time next week too.

Do three lives before you judge any of it. The first one is bad for everyone, the second one is where you find out what your claim-to-paid ratio actually is, and the third is the first honest data you'll have.
