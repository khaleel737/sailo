Three people have told you to just get Shopify. Nobody has told you why, and the monthly fee looks like a lot next to the eleven things you currently sell.

Short answer: you need Shopify when your problem is operations, not distribution. If your evenings are going on stock counts, shipping rules, variant spreadsheets and reconciling what sold where, Shopify is the right answer and a link in bio is a toy. If your evenings are going on making things and posting about them, and your orders arrive one at a time from people who already follow you, Shopify is a monthly bill for machinery you won't switch on.

On 6 August 2026, Shopify's own pricing page showed me Basic at €19/month, Grow at €56, Advanced at €289 and Plus starting from €2,100, with a promotional 3 days free then €1/month for 3 months. Prices localise, so mine came in euros; the Indian page the same day showed Basic at ₹1,499/month billed yearly, or ₹1,994 billed monthly. Check your own region's page before you budget, because the number you'll pay is not the number in this paragraph.

## What Shopify is actually selling you

Not a website. You can get a page with products on it for nothing.

Shopify sells you a single source of truth about inventory, and everything that depends on it. One item, one stock number, decremented whether it sold on your site, at a market stall through the point of sale, on Instagram, or through a marketplace integration. That number is the thing. Everything else in the product is built on top of it: shipping rates that depend on weight and destination, tax rules that depend on where the buyer lives, discount codes with usage limits, staff accounts with permissions, an app ecosystem for the twenty things your specific trade needs.

If you have never once oversold an item, that machinery is solving a problem you don't have.

## Five signals that Shopify is your answer

Count how many are true. Three or more and stop reading comparison articles.

**You have more than about 150 SKUs.** Not products, SKUs. A t-shirt in five sizes and four colours is twenty SKUs and twenty stock numbers. Managing that by hand is where evenings go to die.

**You sell in more than one place at the same time.** A market stall on Saturdays, a website all week, a marketplace listing, maybe a wholesale account. The moment two channels can sell the same physical object, you need one system that knows how many are left, and you need it before the Saturday you sell the last one twice.

**Your shipping is a rule, not a number.** If postage varies by weight, by zone, by whether the order is over a threshold, or by whether the buyer picked a courier, you want a shipping engine. Typing a delivery price into each order works until it doesn't, and it stops working at about the same volume that makes it painful to fix.

**Somebody else touches the orders.** A packer, a part-time assistant, a warehouse. You need accounts with permissions, an order status everyone can see, and a picking list. That's an operations product, not a link.

**Your accountant has opinions.** VAT, sales tax by state, GST, invoices with sequential numbers, integrations into accounting software. Once tax compliance is a real obligation rather than a thing you'll sort out later, the platform that handles it saves you more than it costs.

## The arithmetic, honestly

Take Shopify Basic at €19/month. That's €228 a year, plus card processing at 1.7% + €0.25 per online transaction on that tier, per the same page on 6 August 2026.

Against what? Against your time, mostly, and against the cost of the mistakes you're currently making.

If Shopify's inventory sync prevents four oversells a year, and each oversell costs you a refund, an apology, and a customer who doesn't come back, that's worth more than €228 on its own. If it saves you two hours a week reconciling stock, that's 100 hours a year, and you can price your own hour.

Now the other direction, which vendors of both kinds tend to skip. If you sell 15 items a month at €30 each, that's €450 of revenue. A €19 monthly fee is 4.2% of it before anyone has processed a card. You'd be paying a marketplace-sized commission for a system whose main feature you don't use, because 15 orders a month of 11 products does not need an inventory engine.

The crossover isn't a revenue number. It's a complexity number. Revenue only tells you whether you can afford it.

## The fee nobody reads until they've committed

Shopify's pricing page on 6 August 2026 listed an additional transaction fee for using a payment gateway other than Shopify Payments: 2% on Basic, 1% on Grow, 0.6% on Advanced and 0.2% on Plus.

That matters enormously outside the countries where Shopify Payments is available. If you're in a market where you must use a local gateway, you pay that gateway's rate and then you pay Shopify 2% on top, on Basic. On €5,000 a month of sales, that extra 2% is €100 a month, which is more than five times the plan fee. Sellers discover this after migrating, which is the worst possible time.

Check whether Shopify Payments is live in your country before you compare anything else. If it isn't, add the third-party fee to the plan fee and compare that total.

## The bill is never just the plan

Budget for four line items, not one. This is the part that makes a €19 plan cost €90 a month by March.

**Apps.** The platform is deliberately thin and the app store is deliberately deep. Reviews, subscriptions, bundles, a decent product filter, back-in-stock alerts, a proper accounting sync. Each is a few dollars to a few tens of dollars a month, none of them feel like a decision at the time, and they compound. Write down the ones you'd install on day one and add their monthly cost to the plan fee before you compare anything.

**A theme, or somebody's afternoon.** Free themes are real and they work. They also all look like each other, and the moment you want something specific you're either learning Liquid or paying someone who has.

**Card processing.** On Basic that was 1.7% + €0.25 per online transaction on 6 August 2026, and there's the third-party gateway surcharge above if you can't use Shopify Payments where you are.

**Your weekends.** The migration itself is the expensive part and it never appears in anyone's comparison table. Products, images, descriptions, variants, URLs, and then the fortnight of small breakages afterwards.

None of that is a reason not to move. It's a reason to move once, deliberately, when the signals say so, rather than because three people on the internet told you to.

## Dan in Manchester, and why he was right to move

Dan sells work boots and outdoor gear from a unit in Manchester. About 420 SKUs once you count sizes, maybe 300 orders in a busy month, average order £68. He has a part-time packer three days a week, a stall at two trade shows a year, and a small wholesale line to three independent shops.

He ran a link-in-bio shop for a year and a half and it worked, right up until it didn't. The break came in October: he sold the last pair of size 10 Gore-Tex boots to a customer online while the same pair went out the door at a trade show. Two apologies, one refund, one review he'd rather not have.

He moved to Shopify. The subscription was the smallest part of the cost. He spent about £400 on a theme and a developer afternoon, three weekends migrating products, and he pays for two apps monthly. Against £20,000 of monthly revenue, all of that is noise.

What he got: one stock number across web, stall and wholesale. Shipping bands by weight, so a £9 boot postage doesn't apply to a £4 pair of socks. A packer who logs in and sees today's orders without messaging him. Sequential invoices his accountant stopped complaining about.

What he lost: nothing he misses, except that his conversion rate off Instagram dropped for two months while he got the mobile page fast again. The default theme he chose loaded in about four seconds on mobile data. His old shop page loaded in under one. He fixed it by cutting the hero video and resizing images, but he lost two months of sales learning that a "professional" storefront is often a slower one.

That's the detail worth taking from Dan: moving up a tier usually costs you page speed before it gives you anything back, and nobody warns you.

## When Shopify is the wrong answer, said plainly

- You have fewer than about 40 SKUs and no variants.
- Nobody but you touches an order.
- Your delivery price is one number, or free, or the buyer collects.
- Most of your buyers pay on a rail Shopify Payments doesn't cover in your country, so you'd pay the third-party fee on top of everything.
- You haven't proved anyone wants the product yet.

That last one is the big one. A shop platform is a fine answer to "how do I run this business" and a terrible answer to "does anyone want this". If you're still finding out, spend nothing and find out. The whole category of cheap ways to find out is laid out in [link in bio tools compared](/en/blog/link-in-bio-tools-compared).

## What a link in bio does that Shopify does not

Two things, and they're both about speed.

The first is time to a live page. You can have a link with real products and real prices in under an hour, and post it the same evening. Shopify will take you a weekend minimum and realistically longer, because a theme is a decision and decisions expand.

The second is what the buyer touches. A single page, opened from a bio, with prices visible before any scrolling, on a payment rail that might be a bank transfer or a WhatsApp handoff rather than a card form. On mobile data, that page can be under a second. A full ecommerce theme with a hero image, a font load and a cart is rarely under three.

For a seller whose entire distribution is Instagram, that difference is the business. For Dan, it's a rounding error against not overselling boots.

## Sailo's honest position against Shopify

Sailo loses this comparison for anyone matching three or more of the five signals, and it isn't close.

There's no inventory sync across channels. No point of sale. No shipping rate engine that computes by weight and zone. No staff accounts with permissions. No app ecosystem. No tax calculation by jurisdiction. If those are your problems, Shopify solves them and Sailo does not pretend to.

## The test to run before you pay anyone

Open a spreadsheet. Column one, every SKU you sell, sizes and colours as separate rows. Column two, how many of each you physically have. Column three, everywhere it can be sold from.

If filling that in takes ten minutes and column three has one entry for every row, you don't need Shopify this year. Spend the €228 on better photographs instead, and read [free versus paid selling tools](/en/blog/free-versus-paid-selling-tools) before you subscribe to anything.

If filling it in takes an afternoon, if column two is a guess, or if any row has two entries in column three, start the Shopify trial this week. You've outgrown the link, and the next thing to plan is the move itself, which has a right order and a wrong one: [moving off a platform without losing customers](/en/blog/moving-off-a-platform-without-losing-customers).
