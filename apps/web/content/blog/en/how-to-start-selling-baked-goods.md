Six people have now asked what you'd charge for a dozen, and six times you've said "oh, take them, it's nothing." Somewhere around the seventh you started resenting it. That's the point where this stops being a hobby and starts being a decision.

The start is smaller than people make it. Pick one thing you can make forty of without opening the recipe. Find out what your county or council requires of a home kitchen before you sell a single item. Price it so your hours are inside the number, not outside it. Put it on a link with a collection day and a hard weekly cap. Take the order on whichever app your buyers already message you on. You can be selling by next Saturday for roughly nothing in software and about $60 in boxes and labels.

The part that takes longer is deciding what business you're actually in, because a person who sells cookies and a person who sells cakes have almost nothing in common past the flour.

## Find out what your kitchen is allowed to sell

Do this first, before the logo, before the name, before you buy 200 boxes.

Almost everywhere has some version of a rule for food made in a home kitchen and sold to the public. What it's called and what it demands varies enormously by country, and inside the United States it varies by state and sometimes by county. Some places want a short registration and nothing else. Some want a class, a fee and an inspection. Some cap how much you can sell in a year. Some let you sell only in person and not by post. Almost all of them draw a line between food that's safe at room temperature and food that isn't.

Do not take my word for what applies to you, and do not take a Facebook group's word either. Search your state or local authority's own site for home food business or cottage food, or ring the environmental health desk at your local council and ask what a person baking at home for sale needs to do. It's usually a ten-minute call and they've had it a hundred times.

The line that matters most for your product list is this one: food that needs refrigeration to stay safe is treated far more strictly than food that doesn't, nearly everywhere. Cheesecake, cream-filled anything, custard tarts, buttercream that sits out for six hours in July, meat pies. These are frequently the things a home kitchen isn't cleared to sell at all, or can only sell under a much heavier permit. Cookies, brownies, banana bread, biscotti, granola, jam, most breads and most uniced cakes usually sit on the friendlier side of that line.

The second thing to sort out is allergens. Wherever you are, you'll be expected to tell people what's in the food, and you'll be asked about gluten by the third week. Answer that one honestly: if you bake with wheat flour in a domestic kitchen, you cannot sell a gluten-free product, because flour hangs in the air for hours. Say "I can't do gluten free safely from this kitchen" and you'll lose one sale and gain a reputation.

## Cookies and cakes are two different businesses

This is the thing nobody tells you and it decides your whole week.

A cookie business is a **product** business. You make a batch, you make it again, the unit is identical, the price is fixed, and someone can buy it at 11pm without talking to you. You can bake ahead. You can freeze dough. If four people order, that's the same afternoon as one person ordering, roughly.

A cake business is a **project** business. Every order is a quote, a conversation, a date, and a thing that has never existed before. You can't bake ahead. You can't take four for the same Saturday unless you've built for it. The price isn't on a page, it's in an email, and half the people who ask will never order.

Both are fine businesses. They are not the same business, and running them from the same shop with the same expectations is how people burn out in month five. Cookies pay in volume and predictability. Cakes pay in margin and eat your weekends.

If you want to do both, separate them mechanically. Cookies are listed with a price and a collection date, buyable without you. Cakes are an enquiry with a notice period, a deposit, and a number you only give after three questions. The detail on the cake side, deposits, notice periods and how to quote without guessing, is worked through in [running a small cake business](/en/blog/running-a-small-cake-business), which is written for the Philippines but the mechanics are the same in Ohio.

Here's the scheduling fact that makes the split obvious. Royal icing needs a night to dry between the flood layer and the detail, so a Saturday order is really a Wednesday decision. That's why almost every home baker's order cut-off lands on a Wednesday, whether or not they've ever worked out why.

## Shelf life decides what you can post and what you can't

Write your shelf life down before you write your price list, because it silently sets your delivery options, your batch size and your refund policy.

| What you bake | Realistic quality window | Can you post it | What it means for orders |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Decorated sugar cookies | 7 to 10 days | Yes, if boxed so they can't rattle | Bake to order, collect or post, low waste |
| Brownies, blondies | 4 to 5 days | Yes | Good for a weekly drop |
| Sourdough, crusty bread | Good for a day, edible for three | Badly | Collection or same-day local only |
| Buttercream cake | Best that day, fine the next | No | Collection or hand delivery, full stop |
| Cheesecake, cream fillings | Refrigerated, days | No | Often not permitted from a home kitchen at all |
| Biscotti, granola, cookies for post | Weeks | Yes, easily | The only line you can genuinely batch and stock |

That table is your business model. If everything you make is best on day zero, you're a collection business with a fixed pickup window, and you should stop trying to be a shipping business. If you want to post, you need at least one product that's still good on day five after being thrown into a van.

The people who get hurt are the ones who sell a sourdough loaf online for delivery on Thursday, bake it Wednesday night, and hand the customer something that's a day past its best while charging a premium price for freshness. Do it twice and the reviews say "dry".

## Price the hours, not the flour

Ingredients are the smallest number in your costs and the only one most new bakers count.

Work it in this order:

1. **Ingredients for one unit.** Weigh it once properly. A batch of 24 cookies might cost you $6 in butter, sugar, flour, egg and vanilla. That's 25c a cookie.
2. **Packaging.** Box, bag, sticker, tissue, ribbon, a card. This is often more than the ingredients. A rigid cookie box with an insert can be $1.80 on its own.
3. **Your time, at a rate you'd accept from someone else.** Mixing, chilling, rolling, baking, cooling, icing, drying, packing, replying to messages, driving. Not just the oven part.
4. **Waste and breakage.** Bake 26 to sell 24. Some crack. Some are ugly. Cost it at 8% and move on.
5. **The fees on the way in**, whichever rail the money comes down.

A dozen plain decorated cookies at $6 of ingredients and $2 of packaging is $8 of materials. If it takes you three hours across two days, and you want $22 an hour, the honest price is $74. Nobody in your town is paying $74 for a dozen cookies, so you have three options: get faster, simplify the design, or accept a lower hourly rate on purpose while you learn. Choosing knowingly is fine. Not knowing is what makes people quit.

There's a fuller method for this, including what to do when the honest price is above what your market pays, in [how to price what you make](/en/blog/how-to-price-what-you-make).

## The order form is a capacity limit wearing a disguise

Your shop page isn't a catalogue. It's a gate.

The three fields that stop the misery:

- **A cut-off.** "Orders close Wednesday 9pm for Saturday collection." Put it in the product description, not the bio.
- **A cap.** You know how many dozen you can genuinely do in a weekend. It's probably eight, not fifteen. When the eighth sells, the item goes unavailable. This is the single most useful thing an online shop does for a baker and most people never turn it on.
- **A collection window.** "Saturday between 10 and 1, at the door." Not "Saturday". The person who turns up at 8pm has ruined your evening and they think they've done nothing wrong.

Take the money at order time for anything personalised. A dozen cookies with a stranger's kid's name piped on them cannot be resold to anybody, so a no-show costs you the full amount. Plain stock can be sold at the door on Saturday morning. Personalised can't. Charge accordingly.

## Worked example: Renata, decorated sugar cookies, Tampa

Renata sells decorated sugar cookies. One product, one dozen per box, $42, collection only, Saturdays, cut-off Wednesday night.

Her numbers per box: $6.10 ingredients, $2.40 packaging, $0.60 waste. Materials $9.10. Icing and decorating a dozen takes her about two hours and twenty minutes now, down from four when she started, spread over Thursday evening and Friday morning because of the drying. Baking and dough is another hour for a triple batch, so call it 20 minutes per box. She's making about $12 an hour on the work, which is low, and she knows it, and the plan is to raise the price to $48 in the autumn once she has 30 reviews.

She caps Saturdays at ten boxes. Ten boxes is $420 a week, about $150 of it materials and fees, so roughly $270 for around eighteen hours. She takes payment by transfer at order time and marks each one paid on Sunday morning with coffee.

The interesting part is what she stopped doing. For four months she also offered custom cakes, priced by guesswork, averaging $95 each. One cake ate a Friday. When she worked out the hours, the cakes were paying her about $8 an hour and generating six enquiry threads for every order that happened. She dropped them and her income went up.

## Week nine, when it goes wrong

The starting-out guides all end at "launch". The actual problems arrive later.

**Two people want the last slot.** Whoever paid first has it. Not whoever messaged first. Say that out loud in the description before it happens, because saying it afterwards sounds like an excuse.

**Butter goes up.** Baking ingredient prices move, and they move most in the run-up to the holidays, which is exactly when your order volume peaks. Buy for a December order in November, and if you're quoting December work in September, quote with a note that the price holds for 30 days.

**Someone asks for a wedding cake.** They will describe it in one sentence and expect a number. Ask three questions before you answer: how many people, what date, and where does it need to be at what time. The delivery and setup, not the baking, is what makes wedding work expensive and risky.

**A collection no-show.** Message once at the end of the window. Hold personalised items until Sunday, then bin them and don't refund. Sell unnamed stock at the door.

**The scaling wall.** The first time you multiply a recipe by six, you'll discover your mixer bowl holds four, and you'll discover it at 11pm.

**Someone asks if you're registered.** Answer plainly and specifically: yes, with whom, and when it was done. Trust in food is not built with a nice logo. A seller who names their registration and their allergen policy in one line converts better than one with better photography, which is the whole argument in [proving you are a real seller](/en/blog/proving-you-are-a-real-seller).

## What to do next

Ring your local environmental health office this week and ask what a home baker selling to the public has to do. That's the call that unblocks everything else, and until it's made you're building on a guess.

Then pick your one product, weigh the ingredients for a single unit properly, and write the three numbers on paper: materials, minutes, price. If the price you can charge doesn't cover the minutes, you've learned the most useful thing you'll learn this month, and you've learned it before buying 200 boxes.

Photograph that one product next to a window on a Saturday morning, using the method in [what to photograph when you sell food](/en/blog/what-to-photograph-when-you-sell-food), put it on a link with a Wednesday cut-off and a cap of eight, and post it once. Then go and bake.
