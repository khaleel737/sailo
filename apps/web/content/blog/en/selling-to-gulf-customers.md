Your shop is an Instagram grid and your till is a WhatsApp thread. That isn't a stopgap you'll grow out of. In Dubai, Riyadh, Kuwait City and Doha it's the mainstream way a small brand sells, and it will still be the way you sell when you're doing AED 60,000 a month.

So the goal isn't to escape it. The goal is to stop losing money inside it.

Three things fix most of the leak: a page with prices that a buyer can read without messaging you, an Arabic version of everything, and delivery you can actually promise. An AED 320 abaya sold through a DM costs you nothing in payment fees and costs you twenty minutes of typing, and at ninety orders a month that's thirty hours you didn't have.

## Instagram sells, WhatsApp closes, and neither of them remembers

The pattern is reliable. A reel or a story does its job, someone taps through to the profile, they open the link or they DM. The conversation moves to WhatsApp because WhatsApp is where Gulf commerce happens. Sizing, colour, delivery date, payment, all of it in one thread.

What that thread doesn't do is remember. Three weeks later you cannot find whether Fatima ordered the 56 or the 58, whether she paid the AED 320 or the AED 340 with express delivery, and whether the AED 320 that landed in your account on the 14th was hers or someone else's.

The fix isn't to move off WhatsApp. It's to make the order exist as a thing before the chat starts, so the message arrives already written: item, size, colour, address, total. Then the chat does what chat is genuinely good at, which is the questions and the reassurance, and it isn't also serving as your order book, your price list and your accounts.

[Turning Instagram DMs into orders](/en/blog/turning-instagram-dms-into-orders) covers the handoff, and [how to take orders on WhatsApp](/en/blog/how-to-take-orders-on-whatsapp) covers what the message should contain.

## Stories sell, the grid gets you checked

Worth understanding the division of labour, because sellers spend their effort in the wrong place.

Stories are where the buying happens. They're immediate, they're where the "available now" and the restock and the behind-the-scenes go, and the link sticker is the shortest path from wanting something to ordering it that exists on the platform. Post there daily, even scrappily.

The grid is not where buying happens. The grid is where a buyer who is about to send you AED 320 goes to decide whether you exist. They scroll back six months. They look for photos of real customers wearing the thing. They check whether your last post was in March. A tidy grid with nine perfect flat-lays and no sign of a human being is less convincing than a messier one full of people.

So keep both, and stop trying to make the grid do the selling. What the grid needs is proof: customer photos, a packed parcel, your workshop, a face. What stories need is frequency and a link.

## The four messages you will get, forever

Read your last fifty DMs. They'll be some version of these:

1. "Price?"
2. "Is this available?"
3. "Do you deliver to [emirate / city]?"
4. "How long?"

Every one of those has a fixed answer, and you're typing it by hand. Put all four on a page and your DM volume drops without your order volume dropping.

One caveat on the first. Gulf buyers ask "price?" under posts that already show a price, and it isn't always because they missed it. Sometimes they're asking whether you're the kind of seller who negotiates. Decide your answer once. "AED 320, delivered anywhere in the UAE" said instantly and identically to everyone is a policy. "Let me see what I can do for you" is a negotiation you'll now have on every order forever.

> A price you always give the same way is worth more to you than the twenty dirhams you occasionally save by not giving it.

## Two languages, not one and a half

Your buyers are Emirati, Saudi, Egyptian, Indian, Filipino, Pakistani, British, Lebanese. Many of them read both English and Arabic comfortably and will use whichever you give them. Many will not.

Half-translating is worse than not translating. A page with an Arabic heading and English product names, sizes and delivery information reads as unfinished and it costs trust. Do the whole thing or do it in English and be clear that's what you're doing.

The parts that most need Arabic, in order: your delivery areas and times, your return policy, and your sizing. Those are the three where a misunderstanding turns into a refused parcel. Product names and marketing copy matter less than you'd think, because a photograph of an abaya communicates fine in any language.

Sailo ships in 35 languages, Arabic among them, so the shop page itself can be read in whichever your buyer picked. What it can't do is write your delivery policy for you in either language.

## Delivery: same day is the expectation, not the premium

Gulf buyers have been trained by food delivery apps and by Amazon's regional operation to expect a parcel within a day or two, and within hours in Dubai. That expectation applies to you whether or not it's fair.

You don't have to meet it. You have to be honest about it. A page that says "dispatched within 48 hours, delivered next day in Dubai and Sharjah, two to four days elsewhere in the UAE, five to seven days to Saudi and Kuwait" will lose fewer buyers than a page that says nothing and then disappoints.

Three practical points:

**Same-day courier services inside a city are cheap enough to build into your price.** Sellers who offer free same-day in Dubai and charge for everywhere else convert noticeably better in their home market, and the cost of that is a known number rather than a risk.

**Cross-border inside the GCC is a customs shipment, not a domestic one.** Dubai to Riyadh means paperwork, a declared value, and a buyer who might be asked for duty at the door. Quote it separately, warn them, and never fold it silently into the price.

**Address quality decides your return rate.** Villa numbers, building names and "near the mosque" are how people describe where they live here. Ask for a landmark and a phone number that will be answered, and message the buyer the morning the parcel goes out.

## Cash on delivery is still here

High card penetration hasn't killed it. Plenty of Gulf buyers, especially for a brand they've just discovered, will pick cash on delivery because it removes the only thing they're genuinely worried about, which is a new Instagram shop taking their money and vanishing.

It costs you the same way it costs everyone: the courier's handling fee, the delay before they remit, and the parcels that come back. Offer it, price it in, and consider capping it. "Cash on delivery available up to AED 500, prepaid above that" is a sentence that costs you almost no orders and protects you from the expensive refusals.

Bank transfer works fine here too, and Gulf buyers use it without complaint. The catch is the same one everywhere: a transfer arrives as a bare credit with whatever reference the buyer typed, or didn't. Give every order a reference and ask for it back. [How to know a bank transfer actually arrived](/en/blog/how-to-know-a-bank-transfer-actually-arrived) is the method, and [how to take payment as a small seller](/en/blog/how-to-take-payment-as-a-small-seller) compares every rail by what it costs you and what it leaves on your desk afterwards.

## Worked example: Layla, abayas, Dubai

Layla sells abayas she has made in a small workshop in Al Quoz. AED 320 standard, AED 480 for the embroidered line. Around 90 orders a month, roughly 65% UAE, 20% Saudi, the rest Kuwait, Qatar and Oman.

Her setup:

- Instagram for discovery, a bio link to a page with every design, every length, prices in AED and delivery times per country.
- WhatsApp for the conversation, with orders arriving pre-written rather than typed.
- Payment: bank transfer for UAE, cash on delivery up to AED 500 in Dubai and Sharjah, and card for the Saudi and Kuwait orders because a cross-border buyer paying a stranger by transfer is a much harder ask.
- Delivery: next-day within the UAE, three to five days GCC, quoted per country on the page.

Sizing is where she nearly lost the business. Abayas are sold by length, in centimetres, not by S and M, and her first six months of returns were almost entirely "too long" or "too short". She now puts a height-to-length chart on the page and asks every buyer for their height in the order, and asks it in Arabic and English. Returns on length went from about one in seven to under one in twenty.

Her other lesson is about hours. During Ramadan her DMs are dead from about 5pm and then busy from 10pm until well past midnight. The first year she answered messages on a nine-to-six pattern and lost most of a month's orders to people who'd moved on by morning. Now she shifts her own hours for those weeks. It's the single biggest calendar effect in her year and it's worth planning for.

## The calendar matters more here than most places

Ramadan and the two Eids reshape the selling year. Demand for clothing, gifts, sweets, home goods and anything hosting-related concentrates hard, and lead times get brutal because everyone's supplier is busy at once.

Order stock earlier than feels necessary. Publish your last-order-before-Eid date and stick to it, because the alternative is thirty apologetic messages. And check the working week before you schedule a launch or promise a delivery date, because the weekend doesn't fall on the same days in every Gulf country and a Thursday launch does not mean the same thing in Dubai and Riyadh.

## Licensing, and the honest limit of what I can tell you

If you're trading commercially from the UAE, you're expected to hold a licence, and there are different routes depending on the emirate, whether you're on the mainland or in a free zone, and what you're actually selling.

I'm not going to name a specific licence, a fee or a threshold here, because they vary by emirate, they get revised, and an out-of-date figure could cost you real money. Go to the economic development department for your own emirate, or to the free zone you're considering, and ask them directly what your activity requires. It's a phone call. Do it before you scale rather than after someone reports you.

The same applies in Saudi, Kuwait and Qatar, and the answers are different in each.

## What to do next

Open your last fifty DMs and count how many of them contain a price you typed by hand. That number, times two minutes, is the hours a page would have given you back last month.

Then write your delivery promise as four lines with real numbers: your own emirate, the rest of the UAE, the GCC, everywhere else. Put it where a buyer sees it before they message you, and put it in Arabic as well as English.
