You made forty pins in one weekend, got 12,000 impressions, and sold nothing. Meanwhile a single pin you made in March, half-heartedly, is still sending you two or three visitors a day in August.

That second thing is the whole reason to bother with Pinterest, and the first thing is why most sellers quit before they see it.

You sell on Pinterest without a merchant account or a product feed by using the thing every pin already has: a destination link. Point it at a page with the price on it, write the pin for the search box, and let it sit. No catalogue upload, no verification queue, no approval. A Sailo shop is free at $0 for up to {{free_products}} products, and a $34 digital template pinned in February can still be selling in October, which is not true of anything you post anywhere else.

## Pinterest is a search engine that happens to look like a feed

Treat it like Instagram and you'll fail at it. Nobody scrolls Pinterest to see what their friends are doing. They arrive with a job: kitchen ideas, a costume for a six-year-old, what to serve at a christening, how to organise a small pantry.

Three consequences, and each one changes what you should make:

**Intent is high and dated.** Someone searching "small bathroom storage" is going to buy something eventually. Not today, necessarily, and that's fine.

**Pins don't expire.** A feed post has a life measured in hours. A pin has a life measured in seasons. That flips the economics of making one: it's worth spending twenty minutes on a pin in a way it is never worth spending twenty minutes on a story.

**Search text does the ranking, not your follower count.** A brand-new account with a well-titled pin gets found. That's the closest thing to free distribution left anywhere, and it's why Pinterest is worth a look even if your other accounts are tiny.

## What sells on Pinterest, and what genuinely doesn't

Before you spend a month on this, be honest about your product. Pinterest is exceptional for some things and a complete waste of time for others.

| Works well | Struggles |
| --- | --- |
| Anything for a wedding, a party, a holiday | Impulse snacks and drinks |
| Home, interiors, storage, organisation | Anything that has to be delivered hot |
| Printables, templates, planners, patterns | Services that only work in one small area |
| Clothes and jewellery with a strong visual style | Products that need a demo to make sense |
| Recipes, food you can teach as well as sell | Trend items with a six-week life |

If your product is in the right column, close this tab and go back to whatever platform your customers are actually on. Pinterest rewards planning, and nobody plans a bubble tea three months out. You are better served by putting the same hours into the platform you already post on, and [which platform should you actually sell on](/en/blog/which-platform-should-you-actually-sell-on) makes that case properly.

## Every pin is a link, and that's the entire mechanism

A standard image pin has a destination URL field. Fill it in with the exact product page, not your home page.

That's it. That's the mechanism. There's no shop to set up, no feed to upload, no merchant review. You're using Pinterest as a very patient distribution channel for links to pages you control.

Two rules that matter more than they should:

**Link to the specific product, not the shop front.** If the pin is a photo of the eucalyptus wreath, the link goes to the eucalyptus wreath with its price on it. A buyer who lands on a shop front and has to find the wreath themselves is a buyer who goes back to Pinterest.

**Never link to a page without a price.** Pinterest traffic is cold and patient. It's not going to message you to ask.

Pinterest has changed which pin formats can carry an outbound link more than once over the years, and the answer differs by format and sometimes by region. Don't plan around anything you haven't seen with your own eyes. Make one pin of each format you're considering, look at whether the link field is there and whether it's tappable on a phone, and build on whichever one still works today.

## Write the pin for the search box

The text on a pin is doing SEO work, and most sellers write it like a caption.

Four fields, four jobs:

1. **Title.** The words someone would type. "Printable wedding invitation template, sage green" beats "New in the shop".
2. **Description.** Two or three plain sentences with the same words a person would use, plus the price. Not a keyword list. Pinterest is reading it, and so is a human deciding whether to tap.
3. **Board name.** Boards are categories. "Wedding stationery" is a board. "My stuff" is not.
4. **Text on the image itself.** Pinterest is visual search and people skim at thumbnail size. The words on the image are frequently the only thing read.

The test for any pin title: would a stranger type these words? If the title contains your brand name and nothing else, nobody will ever type it, and the pin will only ever be seen by people who already follow you. Which is nobody, because Pinterest followers barely matter.

## The image spec that stops you wasting an afternoon

Vertical, roughly two to three. A 1000 by 1500 pixel image is the standard that has held for years, and horizontal images get squashed into a small strip that nobody taps.

Beyond the ratio, three things that decide whether a pin gets tapped:

- **Readable at thumbnail size.** Hold your phone at arm's length. If you can't read the text, redo it.
- **One idea per pin.** A collage of nine products is a pin about nothing.
- **Text overlay on about half your pins.** Not all. Straight product photography works too, especially for jewellery and clothes, but a pin with three clear words on it tells a scroller what they're looking at in the fraction of a second you get.

Make five pins per product, not one. Different photos, different crops, different text, all pointing at the same page. This is normal on Pinterest and it's the main way sellers who do well differ from sellers who don't: they treat one product as fifteen pins over three months, not one pin forever.

## The seasonal lead time nobody accounts for

Pinterest runs ahead of the calendar, and further ahead than you'd guess. People plan Christmas in the summer, weddings a year out, and back-to-school in June.

Which means the pin you make the week before the holiday is late. Not slightly late. Structurally late, because the searches happened and the decisions were made while you were still photographing.

A workable rhythm for a small seller: pin seasonal products about 45 to 60 days before you want the orders, keep pinning through the season, and don't delete last year's pins. The old ones often outperform the new ones, because they've had time to accumulate saves.

## Maya in Portland: $34 invitation templates

Maya sells editable wedding invitation templates from Portland. Files, not paper. Six designs at $34 each, plus a $58 bundle of three.

She spent her first year on Instagram, where wedding stationery is a brutal, crowded feed, and she was making four or five sales a month from about 2,000 followers. The work was constant and the sales were flat.

She started pinning in January. Not cleverly, just consistently: five pins per design, one a day, each linking straight to the product page with the $34 on it. Vertical images showing the invitation on a table with a sprig of something, with three words of overlay text like "editable sage invite".

Nothing happened for six weeks. This is the part where most people stop.

By late March the pins from January were getting saves, and the saves were feeding new impressions. By May she was at about 30 sales a month, and the important detail is where they came from: her top five pins were all made in that first January batch. Pins she'd forgotten about.

Two things she'd tell you.

The first is that she stopped making new pins for new products and started making new pins for the products that were already selling. The winners compound. The losers stay lost.

The second is that she made her page do the work Pinterest wouldn't. A cold Pinterest visitor has never heard of her, so the page has to answer everything: what file formats, whether it's editable, whether it works on a phone, and what happens after payment. She sells a file that unlocks on payment, and she put the delivery process in the description in plain words. Sales went up when she added the boring paragraph. ## Watch outbound clicks, not impressions

Pinterest will show you a lot of impressions. Impressions are close to worthless.

The number that matters is outbound clicks: how many people actually left Pinterest and landed on your page. Those two numbers move independently, and it's entirely possible for impressions to climb for months while clicks stay flat.

So track the one that pays. Once a week, open your Pinterest analytics, sort by outbound clicks, and look at which pins actually sent people. Then make more pins like those three, and stop making pins like the rest.

## What you can't do without your own domain

One limitation is specific to running this without a website, and it's worth knowing before you invest three months.

Pinterest's website claiming asks you to prove you control a site, usually by adding a tag to its code or a record to its DNS. That needs a domain you own. A shop that lives on a subpath of somebody else's domain, `sailo.store/yourname` included, isn't something you can claim.

What you lose: the claimed-site badge on your profile, profile-level attribution when other people pin from your page, and the metadata-driven pin formats that depend on a claimed site. Check what Pinterest currently requires in your own settings, because these rules move.

What you keep: the ability to link to your page from every pin, which is the part that actually sells anything.

For most small sellers that trade is fine. If Pinterest is going to be your main channel for the next two years, buy a domain. It's the one case where a website, or at least a domain, genuinely earns its keep.

## Do this with your next five products

1. Pick the three products most likely to be planned for rather than bought on impulse.
2. Make five vertical pins for each, 1000 by 1500, each with the price on the destination page.
3. Title every pin with words a stranger would type, and put the price in the description.
4. Put them on boards named after categories, not after your shop.
5. Post one a day for fifteen days, then leave them alone for six weeks.

Come back at week seven and sort by outbound clicks. Whatever's at the top is your real product, and the next fifty pins should all look like it.
