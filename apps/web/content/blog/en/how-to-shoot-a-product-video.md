"My photos get twelve likes and a video of me folding a bedsheet got four thousand views."

That's not luck and it's not the algorithm being kind. A video answers a question a photo can't: what does this thing do when a human touches it. Shoot one action, start to finish, in under 20 seconds, vertical, on your phone, with no talking and the product visible in the first frame. Put the price on screen. That's the format, and a ₹2,400 quilt shot that way in nine seconds will outperform a photo of the same quilt every time.

The rest of this is how to get those nine seconds without spending a Saturday on them.

## One action, from beginning to end

The entire craft is choosing the right action. Everything else is holding the phone still.

A product video works when it shows a change of state. Something opens, pours, folds, unfolds, stretches, clicks shut, gets lifted, gets worn, gets cut into. The viewer watches the change complete, and the completion is what stops the scroll, because an unfinished action is uncomfortable to leave.

Good actions, by what you sell:

| What you sell | The action |
| --- | --- |
| Bedding, textiles, clothing | The unfold, or the fold. One continuous motion |
| Candles, soap, cosmetics | Lid off, then a finger through the surface or a wick lit |
| Food | The cut, the pour, the pull-apart, the steam |
| Bags, cases, wallets | Filling it. Phone, keys, bottle, all in, then zip |
| Jewellery | Putting it on. Ring onto finger, clasp fastened at the neck |
| Ceramics, glassware | Pouring something into it, or stacking three |
| Furniture, restored pieces | Opening a drawer, closing a door, one hand running along the top |
| Digital products and files | A hand scrolling the actual pages on a screen |

Bad actions: rotating the product on a turntable, a slow pan across a shelf, and any clip that starts with your logo. A logo intro is three seconds of nothing at the exact moment you needed something, and it's the most common way small sellers lose the whole audience.

## Twenty seconds is the ceiling. Eight is usually better

Length is not a target to fill. It's a budget to spend.

If your action takes six seconds, the video is six seconds. Adding a slow zoom on the end to reach fifteen does not make it better, it makes it a six-second video with nine seconds of waiting attached. Short videos also loop, and a loop is a second view for free.

The rule of thumb that survives contact with reality:

- **3 to 8 seconds.** One action. Best for anything with a satisfying single motion.
- **9 to 15 seconds.** Two or three actions, or one action plus a scale shot.
- **16 to 20 seconds.** Only if you're showing a process with real steps, like a piece being made.
- **Over 20.** You're making a different kind of video, and it's a tutorial, not a product video.

## The first frame is the whole battle

Whatever is on screen in frame one decides whether there's a frame two.

That means the product is already in shot before you press record. Not a hand entering an empty frame. Not a backdrop. Not a title card. The thing, filling most of the frame, in focus, from the very first moment.

The reliable trick: set the shot up, start recording, count one second, then start the action, and cut that first second off in editing. You get a clean opening frame with the product static and sharp, and no wobble from your thumb hitting the button.

Second trick, worth more than it sounds: shoot the action twice and use the second take. The first take is always slightly hesitant because you're thinking about the phone. The second one looks like something a person does normally, which is exactly what you want it to look like.

## No talking, but not silent

Most people watch with the sound off, which is why a video that depends on narration fails. But sound-on viewers exist and the sound your product actually makes is free content.

What to do:

- Record the real audio. Fabric moving, a lid, a zip, water pouring, paper. It's more convincing than music and it costs nothing.
- If you use music, use something without lyrics, because lyrics compete with any text you put on screen.
- Put the essential information on screen as text: what it is, the price, and where to order. Three short lines, big enough to read on a cracked phone in daylight.
- Never put text in the bottom fifth of the frame. That's where the platform puts captions, usernames and buttons, and it will be covered.

If you do want to speak, speak about something the video can't show: how long it takes to make, how many are left, what the fabric feels like after a wash. Don't narrate what's visible.

## Shooting it: the practical settings

You don't need a tripod. You need to stop holding the phone in the air.

**Light.** Same as photography. Daylight from the side, ceiling lights off, no direct sun through the window. Video is less forgiving of mixed light than stills are, because you can't fix white balance shot by shot afterwards.

**Steadiness.** Rest your elbows on the table. Or lean the phone against a mug and let the action happen in front of it. A locked-off shot with a moving product beats a moving camera with a static product almost every time, and it's the difference between watchable and seasick.

**Framing.** Vertical, full frame. Don't shoot horizontal and crop later; you'll lose the sides of the product and half your resolution. If you also want a square version, leave a little headroom at the top when you shoot.

**Focus.** Tap the product on screen before you record, and press and hold to lock it. If you don't lock it, the phone will refocus mid-action, usually at the exact moment the interesting thing happens.

**Resolution.** 1080p at 30fps is plenty. 4K fills your storage, takes four times as long to upload on a slow connection, and gets compressed to roughly the same thing at the other end. Only go to 60fps if you plan to slow something down.

**Hands.** Put them in the shot. A hand gives scale, motion and a signal that a real person owns this thing. Clean nails, no watch that catches the light, and move slower than feels natural, because hands always look rushed on video.

## The three mistakes that ruin most of them

**Moving the camera and the product at the same time.** Pick one. If the product moves, the camera stays put. If the camera moves, the product stays put and the move is slow, short and in one direction.

**Zooming.** Phone zoom in video is a crop, so it costs you sharpness, and a zoom mid-clip almost always wobbles. Get closer instead, or shoot wider and crop in editing where you can do it smoothly.

**Starting the recording before the shot is ready.** Four seconds of a hand reaching for the phone, then the action. Trim the front of every clip. Every single one.

## The four-clip formula, when one shot isn't enough

For something that genuinely needs more, this order works and takes about fifteen minutes to shoot:

1. **The action.** 4 seconds. The change of state, exactly as above.
2. **The detail.** 3 seconds. Close on the stitching, the grain, the seal, the finish. Static camera, product moves slightly.
3. **Scale or context.** 3 seconds. Being held, worn, or next to something recognisable.
4. **The full product, still.** 2 seconds, with the price on screen.

Twelve seconds total. Shoot all four in the same light, in the same session, with the same background, or the cuts will look like four different products.

## Editing, briefly

Trim the front. Trim the back. That's 80% of it.

Beyond that: one text card at most, appearing about a second in and staying long enough to read twice. Cut on the action rather than after it, so each clip ends slightly before the motion does. Don't add filters that shift colour, because that's a return waiting to happen when the actual product arrives a different shade. And export at the platform's native size rather than letting it re-compress something the wrong shape.

Colour drift in video causes the same refunds that colour drift in photographs does, and the fix is the same: get it right in the room, not in the app. The full version of that argument is in [how to photograph what you sell](/en/blog/how-to-photograph-what-you-sell), and it applies to every frame you shoot.

## Worked example: block-printed quilts, Jaipur, ₹2,400

Ritu sells hand-block-printed cotton quilts from Jaipur at ₹2,400, about 25 a month, mostly to buyers in Delhi, Mumbai and Bengaluru. Most of her orders arrive over WhatsApp and get paid by UPI to a plain UPI ID, which needs no gateway and no setup.

Her photos were fine. Flat, well lit, accurate colour. They sold about as well as everyone else's photos.

What she started doing instead took eleven minutes per quilt:

- Quilt folded on a bed near the window, ceiling fan off so nothing moves in the background.
- Phone propped against a book at the edge of the bed, locked focus on the fold.
- Record. One second of stillness, then both hands lift the quilt and it unfolds fully across the bed. Seven seconds.
- Second clip, three seconds: her hand running across the block print, close enough that you can see the slight irregularity where the block was re-inked.
- Text on screen for the last four seconds: "Double, 90x108 inches. ₹2,400. Ships across India."

Total: ten seconds of video, and the irregularity in the print is doing the heaviest work in the whole clip, because it's the one thing that proves the quilt was printed by hand and not by a machine in a factory.

The thing she got wrong for the first month, and it cost her: she shot it horizontally, because that's how the quilt is shaped. Cropped vertical, the quilt lost its edges and the unfold looked like a beige screen filling up. Reshooting vertical from further back, with the whole quilt in frame, fixed it entirely.

She now shoots two videos and six photos per design in one session, which takes about 40 minutes for a new print. Her orders per post roughly doubled, and the questions she gets are about the colour rather than the size, because the size is now visible.

## The video's job ends at the tap

A video's only job is to get someone to your link. It doesn't take the order and it doesn't collect the money.

Which means the thing on the other side has to match. If your video showed the block-printed quilt and your page opens with cushion covers, you've paid for the attention and then wasted it. Move whatever you just posted to the top of your catalogue the same day, every time: it takes thirty seconds and it's the difference between a video that gets views and a video that gets orders. [Building a catalogue people can scan](/en/blog/building-a-catalogue-people-can-scan) covers the ordering properly.

Post the same video everywhere. Reels, TikTok, a WhatsApp status, Facebook, a Pinterest pin. The clip is the expensive part and the posting is free. [Reels that actually sell something](/en/blog/reels-that-actually-sell-something) has the platform-specific parts, including what to do with the caption.

## The honest limitation

A video that lands puts fifty messages in your phone in an evening, and on manual payment rails you're the one who handles every single one of them. A Sailo order handed to WhatsApp arrives pre-written, with the item, the options, the address and the total already in the message, which saves the typing. It can't tell you a UPI payment or a bank transfer arrived, because only your bank can. Somebody has to check each reference against each order, and that somebody is you, at eleven at night, after a good day.

There's no native Sailo app either, so updating your page after a video takes off happens in a mobile browser on whatever connection you have. Worth knowing before you post something at a bus stop.

## What to do next

Pick your bestselling product and write down the one thing it does when someone touches it. Opens, pours, folds, stretches, clicks. That sentence is your video.

Then shoot it twice this afternoon, near a window, with the phone propped against something, and post the second take. Nine seconds. No music, no logo, no talking.

If you want the still photographs to match the video, shoot them in the same session with the same setup: [taking product photos with just a phone](/en/blog/taking-photos-with-just-a-phone) has the settings to change first, and they're the same ones that improve the video.
