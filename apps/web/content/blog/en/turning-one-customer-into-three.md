Somebody messages you saying "my friend bought from you". You take the order, you're delighted, and you have absolutely no idea which friend, which order, or how to make it happen again.

That's word of mouth working by accident. It can work on purpose, and it's the cheapest ₦0 marketing channel you'll ever have.

Three things make it happen. People need a sentence they can repeat without effort. They need a moment where repeating it is natural. And the link has to survive being forwarded twice through WhatsApp without breaking. Get those three right and one customer becomes two or three over a couple of months, which is a growth rate you can't buy for ₦7,000.

The rest of this is each of the three, plus how to trace the chain so you know it's happening rather than hoping.

## People don't talk because your product is good

They talk because they have something to say and a reason to say it.

Being good is the entry fee. Nobody recommends a shop that disappointed them. But plenty of perfectly good shops get no word of mouth at all, because there's nothing repeatable about the experience. The customer liked it, thought nothing more about it, and moved on.

What gets repeated is one of four things:

- **A specific detail.** "It comes in a glass bottle with a wooden cap, and it's ₦7,000." Details travel. Adjectives don't.
- **A story with a problem in it.** "The dispatch guy went to the wrong estate and she sent another one the same day, free."
- **A social payoff.** "Somebody asked where I got it." Anything visible or smellable creates its own referrals.
- **A price that surprises.** In either direction. Cheap-for-what-it-is gets talked about, and so does expensive-and-worth-it.

If your shop offers none of those, it's not a marketing problem. It's that there's genuinely nothing to say, and the fix is upstream in the product or the packaging.

## Give them the sentence

The single most useful thing you can do is decide what you want people to say about you, then say it to them, in those words, more than once.

Not a slogan. A plain factual sentence that a customer could repeat in a group chat without feeling like an advert.

Bad: "We're passionate about quality fragrance."
Good: "She makes perfume oils in Abuja, ₦7,000 for 30ml, and they last all day in this heat."

The good one contains a place, a price, a size and a claim that can be tested. Somebody can forward that sentence and it does the whole job. Nobody forwards "passionate about quality".

Where to put your sentence so it gets learned:

- In the message you send when the order is confirmed.
- On the note in the parcel.
- In your Instagram bio, in the same words.
- In the answer you give when someone asks "so what do you do?"

Say the same thing every time. Consistency is what makes a phrase repeatable. If you describe your business three different ways, nobody can quote you.

## Four moments that produce talk

Word of mouth doesn't happen at a random point. It clusters around specific moments, and you can put a small prompt into each.

**The moment the order is confirmed.** They're pleased, they've committed, and they're often already in a chat with someone. This is when people send screenshots. Make your confirmation message screenshot-worthy: item, price, delivery window, your name, and the link. If it's a wall of text they won't forward it.

**The moment the parcel opens.** The highest-energy moment in the whole transaction, and most sellers waste it on plastic. A folded note with a hand-written first name outperforms a printed insert, and [what to put in the box](/en/blog/what-to-put-in-the-box) covers what earns its place in there.

**The moment you fix something.** Counter-intuitively, a customer whose order went wrong and got sorted properly talks more than one whose order went perfectly. A perfect order is unremarkable. A recovered one is a story with you as the hero. So treat problems as marketing budget, not as loss.

**The second purchase.** Somebody who buys twice has decided. That's the moment to ask directly, because they've already proven they mean it, and [how to get repeat buyers](/en/blog/how-to-get-repeat-buyers) is the machinery underneath that.

## Make the link survive a forward

This part is unglamorous and it's where most Nigerian sellers lose the chain.

A customer wants to recommend you in a WhatsApp group. What do they actually send? Usually one of these:

| What they send | What happens |
|---|---|
| Your shop link | Works. The person taps and sees products and prices |
| A screenshot of a product | Person has to ask "where's this from" |
| Your Instagram handle typed out | Person has to search, maybe finds you |
| "I'll send you her number" | Never happens |

Only the first one converts on its own. So the whole job is making sure the thing that's easiest to send is the link.

Practically:

1. **One link, short enough to type from memory if needed.** `sailo.store/yourname` fits in a sentence.
2. **The link goes in every message you send a customer.** At the bottom, every time. It costs nothing and it means the link is always one scroll away in the thread they'd forward from.
3. **The page it lands on must show prices.** A page with no prices produces a "how much?" message to you instead of an order, and a stranger in a group chat won't send that message.
4. **It must work for someone who's never heard of you.** Open your own link on a phone with the app closed. If the first screen doesn't say what you sell and what it costs, fix that before anything else.

Group chats are their own channel in Nigeria, and estate groups in particular move product fast. There's an etiquette to it, and [selling to a group chat](/en/blog/selling-to-a-group-chat) is worth reading before you post into one you were added to yesterday.

## Trace the chain, or you're guessing

Ask every new customer one question, in the same message where you confirm the order.

Not "how did you hear about us?" That produces "Instagram", which tells you nothing you can act on. Ask:

> Quick one, who sent you? I like to say thank you.

"Instagram" is an answer. A name is a lead. That's the difference, and it's the whole reason to phrase it as a person rather than a channel.

Then write the name down next to the order. A column in whatever you already use. Within three months you'll have a map, and the map will surprise you, because it's almost never the customer you'd have guessed.

What you do with it: thank the referrer, specifically and quickly. A message the same week saying "Amaka, Chinedu said you sent him, thank you" costs one minute. People who get thanked refer again. People who don't, mostly don't, because they never found out it worked.

## Halima's chain, in Abuja

Halima sells perfume oils in Abuja. ₦7,000 for a 30ml roll-on, ₦12,000 for two, delivery in Abuja ₦2,500 by dispatch, and she does about 30 orders a month.

She started asking "who sent you?" in March. By the end of May she had this, from one customer:

| Order | Buyer | Value | Traced to |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Amaka | ₦7,000 | Instagram post |
| 2 | Amaka's colleague | ₦12,000 | Amaka, office |
| 3 | Amaka | ₦12,000 | Repeat |
| 4 | Amaka's sister | ₦7,000 | Amaka |
| 5 | Colleague's neighbour | ₦7,000 | Order 2 |
| 6 | Amaka's colleague | ₦7,000 | Repeat |
| 7 | Sister's friend | ₦12,000 | Order 4, estate group |

Amaka's ₦7,000 became ₦64,500 across seven orders in eleven weeks. Three of those orders trace two steps away from Amaka, meaning Halima never met the person who caused them.

Two things Halima did that made this visible rather than invisible. She asked who sent them, every time, in the confirmation message, so it never felt like an interrogation. And when order 2 came in, she messaged Amaka within a day to say thank you and sent her a free 5ml sample with her next order, unannounced.

The sample is the part worth copying. It wasn't a discount, so it didn't reset Amaka's sense of the price. It was a gift, which triggered a story, which is what she posted about, which is where order 4 came from.

The unglamorous detail: order 5 originally messaged asking for ₦5,000 because "my neighbour said you gave her a deal". Halima said no, politely, in one line: "₦7,000 for the 30ml, that's the price for everyone, but delivery's on me for a first order." They bought at ₦7,000. Holding the price with one small concession attached is the move, because a referred customer who negotiates you down teaches the next three to try it.

## The offline half, which nobody writes about

Most advice on this treats word of mouth as an online event. In Abuja, Lagos, Nairobi or Manila, a large share of it happens in rooms.

The places where a recommendation actually gets made:

- **Salons and barbershops.** People sit for two hours and talk about what they're wearing and smelling of. Anything visible or scented gets asked about here more than anywhere else.
- **Offices.** One person brings something in and four people ask. Halima's order 2 came from an office.
- **Church, mosque, the gym, the school run.** Regular gatherings of the same people, which is exactly the condition a referral chain needs.
- **The estate gate and the dispatch rider.** Riders see what's in the bag and where it goes. More than one seller has picked up orders because a rider mentioned them.

You cannot manufacture any of that. What you can do is make sure the physical object carries the information, because in a room there's no link to tap. That means your name on the bottle or the box, legibly, and a way to reach you printed on it. Not a QR code alone. A name someone can search and a number someone can type.

The cheapest version of this is a sticker with the shop name and the link, applied to every item, not just the packaging that gets thrown away. The packaging goes in the bin the day it arrives. The bottle sits on a dressing table for two months, and that's the surface that's still doing marketing in week six.

## When word of mouth stalls

It stalls for identifiable reasons, and they're all fixable.

**Nobody knows what to call you.** If your Instagram name, your shop name and the name on the parcel are three different things, the chain breaks at the first retelling.

**The product is fine but not remarkable.** Nothing to say. Change one visible thing: the packaging, the size, an unusual variant.

**You're the only one who can take an order.** If a referral messages you at 10pm and gets a reply at noon the next day, half of them are gone. That doesn't mean you should answer at 10pm. It means the link should take the order without you, which is most of the argument in [getting your first ten orders](/en/blog/getting-your-first-ten-orders) too.

**You've never asked.** The most common one. Referrals happen far more often when someone is asked plainly, and [asking customers for referrals](/en/blog/asking-customers-for-referrals) is the specific wording for that ask.

## What word of mouth won't do

It won't scale on its own past a certain point. A referral chain runs through social circles, and circles have edges. Halima's chain will exhaust Amaka's office and Amaka's estate eventually. When it does, growth needs a second source, and that's a different job.

It's also slow to start and impossible to schedule. You cannot run a word-of-mouth campaign in a week because you need orders first, and each order takes its own time to produce a story. Which is why it's the wrong thing to lean on if you need money this month, and the right thing to build if you want month nine to be easier than month three.
