You've been posting useful things for free for a year and a half. Somebody told you to put it behind a paywall. It doesn't feel right, and it isn't, and you can't quite explain why.

Here's the shape of it: free content answers "what should I do". Paid answers "do it with me", "do it for me", or "here it is, already done". The upgrade isn't more information. It's less work for the buyer. Move along that axis and people pay; paywall the same words and they don't, because they already had them.

Marcus, who we'll come back to, sold 31 copies of a $29 checklist in his first two months from an account with 4,100 followers. The checklist contains nothing he hadn't already said in free videos. What it contains is all of it, in order, on one page, for people who didn't want to watch nine videos to find out what order to do things in.

## The three upgrades that turn free into paid

Every paid offer that comes out of free content is one of these. Pick one deliberately.

**Done with you.** A call, a workshop, a live session, a review of their thing. You take what you've been explaining to everybody and apply it to one person's situation. Fastest to sell, easiest to price, capped by your hours.

**Done for you.** They hand you the job. You do it. Highest price, lowest volume, and the one small audiences underestimate most.

**Done already.** The templates, the files, the settings, the finished version. Scales infinitely, sells slowly, and only works once people believe you're good at the thing.

There's a fourth that people miss, and it's often the best first product: **the same information, in order, in one place**. Free content is scattered by nature. Nobody can find your post from March. Assembling your own material into a sequence, with the dead ends removed, is genuinely valuable, and it's the only paid offer you can build entirely from things you've already made.

> Nobody pays for information any more. People pay to stop searching.

## The audit: what your posts are actually telling you

Before you decide anything, spend an hour reading your own back catalogue as though someone else wrote it.

**Sort by saves and shares, not likes.** A like is politeness. A save is someone saying "I'll need this later", and later is where the money is. Most platforms will show you both if you look.

**Read the comments for questions, not compliments.** Write down every question that appeared more than once, in the words the person used. Not your paraphrase. Theirs. That vocabulary is what you'll put on the sales page later, and it converts far better than anything you'd write yourself.

**Find the posts that produced DMs.** These are the strongest signal on the list. A comment costs nothing. A private message means someone had a problem specific enough to be embarrassed about in public.

**Ignore the viral one.** The post that reached 200,000 people probably reached the wrong 200,000. The post that makes money is more often the one with nine comments where six of them are questions.

**Note what you keep re-explaining.** If you've answered the same thing four times in six months, that's not repetition. That's a market.

## The question after the question

The most useful move in this whole process: for every recurring question, work out what the person actually wanted.

"What camera do you use" is almost never about the camera. It's "my photos look worse than yours and I don't know why". The camera answer is free and useless. The real answer, applied to their photos, is a product.

"How long does it take" is often "am I too slow, is something wrong with me".

"Where do you get your fabric" can be "I've been burned by a supplier and I don't know who to trust".

"Do you do this for other people" is not a question at all. It's an attempt to pay you that failed for lack of a price.

Your free content answers the surface question, because that's what got asked in public. Your paid offer answers the one underneath, because that's the one they'd rather not put in a comment.

## What to keep free, permanently

Creators get nervous here and start hoarding. It backfires.

Keep free: the what, the why, and the proof that you're good at this. Show the work. Show results. Answer the common question completely, not partially.

Charge for: the doing. The specific application to their situation. The finished artefact. The speed.

Giving away a genuinely complete answer makes the paid offer easier to sell, not harder, because it removes the buyer's main worry, which isn't "is this information available elsewhere" but "does this person actually know what they're talking about". A half-answer designed to force a purchase reads exactly like what it is.

The one thing that genuinely helps is letting people see a piece of the paid thing itself: one page of the file, ten minutes of the workshop, one worked example. There's a practical guide to doing that without giving away the product in [offering a free sample of a paid file](/en/blog/offering-a-free-sample-of-a-paid-file).

## Packaging: the four lines that make it buyable

An offer needs four things written down before you can sell it. Most stalled offers are missing two of them.

1. **Who it's for**, specifically enough to exclude people. "For people who've been baking for a year and still get a dense crumb" beats "for bakers".
2. **What they'll have at the end.** A thing, not a feeling. A finished bio. A working spreadsheet. A plan for their balcony. "Confidence" is not a deliverable.
3. **What it costs and how long it takes.** Both numbers. Publicly.
4. **What's not included.** This is the one everybody skips and it's the one that prevents the bad first sale. Two revisions, not unlimited. One hour, not "until you're happy".

Write those four lines before you make anything. If you struggle with line two, the offer isn't ready and no amount of production will fix it.

For the number in line three, [how to price your first paid offer](/en/blog/how-to-price-your-first-paid-offer) covers the specific problem of pricing something with no sales history, which is a different problem from pricing your fifth product.

## The bridge: going from free to paid without it feeling like a switch

The transition is where most people wince and then do it badly, usually by disappearing for three weeks and coming back with a launch.

A version that doesn't feel like a betrayal:

- **Keep posting the free stuff at the same rate.** Nothing gets worse. The paid thing is added, not swapped in.
- **Say what you're doing, once, plainly.** "People keep asking me to look at their setups, so I'm going to start doing that properly. It's $60 and takes about an hour."
- **Attach it to the content it came from.** When you post the free version of the answer, mention that the applied version exists. Same post, one line at the end.
- **Show the work you do for buyers.** With permission, post what a session looked like. This is the single best advertisement and it doubles as free content.
- **Repeat it more than you're comfortable with.** Your audience sees a fraction of your posts. The offer you've mentioned four times has been seen once by most people, and the point at which you're sick of saying it is roughly the point at which they first notice.

## Worked example: Marcus, Atlanta

Marcus posts 60-second videos about fixing bicycles. Brake adjustment, chain wear, why your gears skip. About 4,100 followers, mostly commuters, a lot of them in cities where a bike shop appointment is a two-week wait.

His free content is genuinely complete. He doesn't hold anything back and never has.

The audit, when he did it, turned up three things:

- The most-saved video by a distance was the one about which repairs are worth doing yourself and which aren't.
- The most common DM was some version of "I watched your video and now it's worse, what did I do".
- He'd answered "what tools do I actually need to start" fourteen times.

That produced two offers, in this order.

**The $29 checklist.** A single-page PDF plus a short video: the order to check things in when a bike feels wrong, what each symptom usually means, and the exact tool list with prices. Everything in it came from videos he'd already published. 31 copies in two months, $899.

**The $60 video call.** Thirty minutes, the buyer points a phone at the bike, he tells them what's wrong and talks them through fixing it. Four to six a month, which is $240 to $360 and about three hours of work.

The call is his better business per hour. The checklist is what makes the call get booked, because it's the thing people buy when they're not yet ready to show a stranger their rusty drivetrain.

The number that surprised him: eleven of the 31 checklist buyers had never liked or commented on a single one of his videos. They'd been watching for months in silence. He'd have told you before this that he had about 300 real followers.

He takes payment by card, which for him is worth it: at 35 or so orders a month across both offers, the Business plan at ${{business_monthly}} plus Sailo's {{fee_range}}% on the goods works out to a small percentage of revenue, and the checklist file unlocks the moment the payment clears without him touching anything. That last part is why he chose it. He works a day job and can't be marking orders paid at lunchtime.

## When your free content can't become a paid offer

Some accounts genuinely aren't sitting on a product, and it's worth knowing before you spend two months finding out.

**Your content is entertainment, not help.** If people watch because it's funny or beautiful and nobody has ever asked you how to do it, the upgrade path is sponsorship or merchandise, not a how-to product. That's a legitimate business, just a different one, and the comparison is in [brand deals versus selling your own thing](/en/blog/brand-deals-versus-selling-your-own-thing).

**Nobody has ever asked you anything.** No DMs, no questions, no "can you look at mine". That's a signal about the content. Answer harder questions in public for eight weeks and check again.

**The problem you solve is a one-off.** If the thing you know helps a person once and then never again, you have a small market that doesn't repeat. It can still work, but it needs constant new audience, and you should know that going in.

**You'd have to become someone else to deliver it.** If the only product your content supports is one you'd hate making, don't. You'll sell four and stop.

If none of your content converts, the answer isn't necessarily to give up on selling. It might be that the right product isn't derived from the content at all: [what to sell when you have no product](/en/blog/what-to-sell-when-you-have-no-product) lists the alternatives in order of how quickly each can produce a first sale.

## The delivery bit nobody plans for

Once the money moves, someone has to actually get the thing, and this is where a decent offer turns into a bad experience.

If you sell a file, decide now what happens at 2am. On Sailo, a digital file unlocks when the payment is confirmed. Card payments confirm themselves. Bank transfer, cash, UPI and mobile money don't, because Sailo never touches that money and can't see it arrive. You mark the order paid, and the file unlocks then. So a buyer paying by transfer at midnight waits until you wake up.

That's fine as long as you say so. "Files are sent within 12 hours" on the page costs you nothing and prevents the message that starts "hi, I paid but". Silence is what creates that message.

If you sell your time, decide your notice period before you take the first booking. Twenty-four hours minimum, or you'll get a same-day request at 6am from a buyer in a different timezone who was being perfectly reasonable.

## What to do this week

Open your posts and sort by saves. Write down the top five, then write down every question in their comments in the words people used.

Pick the question you've answered the most times. Write the four packaging lines for it: who it's for, what they'll have at the end, price and turnaround, what's excluded. That's an evening.

Then post it once, plainly, attached to the free content it came from. If two people ask how to pay, build it this weekend. If nobody does, you've spent an evening and learned something that would otherwise have cost you a month, and the wider map of routes is still there in [turning an audience into income](/en/blog/turning-an-audience-into-income).
