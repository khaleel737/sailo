You opened the bale, and now there are a hundred and eighty pieces on your bedroom floor, about thirty of which are actually good, and you have no idea what to charge for any of them.

Start here: wash and press before you photograph, measure every piece in inches instead of trusting the label, list each item as its own product with its own photos, and take payment before you reserve anything. That last one is the whole trade. Thrift is a one-of-one business, and the moment you hold an item for someone who says "I'll send it tomorrow", you have taken a real product off sale for a maybe. If a bale of 100 wearable pieces cost you ₦250,000, every day you hold a ₦4,500 shirt for a stranger costs you real money.

Everything else in this article is downstream of the fact that you have exactly one of each thing.

## A bale is not stock until it's sorted

The arithmetic first, because people buy a bale on vibes and then discover the cost per piece afterwards.

Say the bale cost ₦250,000 delivered, and it held 140 garments. Twenty are damaged past repair, thirty are the wrong market for your buyers, and ninety are sellable. Your true cost is not ₦1,785 a piece. It's ₦250,000 divided by 90, which is ₦2,778, plus washing, plus the dry cleaner for the ones that need it, plus the tailor for the two you'll repair, plus transport from the market, plus the bags.

Call it ₦3,300 a sellable piece. That's the number your pricing has to clear, and it's about 85% higher than the number most first-time sellers use.

Sort in this order, once, properly:

1. **Bin.** Holes in visible places, permanent staining, broken zips on cheap items, anything smelling of mildew that survives a wash.
2. **Repair.** One button, one seam, a hem. If the fix costs more than a third of the sale price, bin it instead.
3. **Grade A.** No flaws, good fabric, current shape, brand people recognise. These pay for the bale.
4. **Grade B.** Wearable, minor flaws, sells at a lower price, still profitable.
5. **Bundle.** Plain tees and basics that are not worth photographing individually. Sell in fives at a flat price.

The fifth pile is where most sellers lose their weekends. Photographing and listing a ₦1,200 plain t-shirt takes exactly as long as photographing a ₦12,000 leather jacket. Don't do it. Bundle them, or sell them at a physical stand on a Saturday.

## Wash before you photograph, always

Nobody buys anything that smells like a bale, and the smell survives the photo in a way that's hard to explain until you've had an item returned.

Wash, dry properly, press. Steam the ones you can't iron. A pressed garment photographs as clothing; an unpressed one photographs as second-hand clothing, and those are two different prices for the same shirt.

This is also when you find the flaws. Every stain, every thin patch, every loose seam shows up on a wet garment and under a window that it never showed under bedroom light. Find them now, on your terms, rather than in a message from an angry buyer on Thursday.

## Size is a measurement, not a letter

The label on an okrika piece tells you which country the garment left, and not much else. A US medium, a UK medium and a Chinese medium are three different garments. A 1990s medium is bigger than a 2020s medium. Half your labels are faded to nothing anyway.

So stop selling sizes and start selling measurements. Lay the garment flat, don't stretch it, and take these in inches:

| Garment | What to measure | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shirt, tee, jacket | Pit to pit | Straight across the chest, armpit seam to armpit seam |
| Shirt, tee, jacket | Length | Top of the shoulder seam straight down to the hem |
| Shirt, jacket | Shoulder | Seam to seam across the back |
| Long sleeve | Sleeve | Shoulder seam to cuff |
| Trousers, jeans | Waist flat | Across the top, buttoned, doubled to get the full waist |
| Trousers, jeans | Inseam | Crotch seam to the bottom of the leg |
| Trousers, jeans | Rise | Crotch seam up to the top of the waistband at the front |
| Dress, skirt | Waist flat and length | Same method, plus hip at the widest point |

Put those numbers in every listing, and add one sentence about fit: "Fits like a modern large, roomy through the body." Buyers who have been burned once will measure a shirt they already own and compare. Those are your best customers, and they will never return anything.

The single most common Nigerian thrift complaint is "it didn't fit". Measurements in the listing kill about two thirds of it, and the other third becomes your problem to solve with a tailor recommendation rather than a refund.

## Photographing worn fabric so nobody argues later

Thrift buyers are buying two things: the shape and the condition. Your photos have to answer both, and the condition photos are the ones that stop arguments.

Shoot near a window during the day, with the ceiling lights off. Flat lay on a plain sheet, ironed, or hang the piece against a plain wall. Six frames per item:

1. **Whole garment, front, flat and square.** No angle, no artful drape. People are judging the cut.
2. **Whole garment, back.**
3. **Fabric close-up.** Close enough to see the weave. This is the frame that sells quality, and almost nobody takes it. A heavy cotton and a thin polyester look identical from four feet away and completely different from six inches.
4. **The label.** Brand, size, country, care. Even a faded one. It's proof you have the item in your hand.
5. **Every flaw, close up, with something for scale.** A coin next to the mark.
6. **On a body if you can.** Doesn't have to be a model. A friend, a mannequin, or the garment on a hanger held against a person's shoulders for scale.

Two rules that matter more than lighting. Never use a photo from Pinterest or a brand's site, ever, because thrift buyers reverse-search and you will be called out publicly. And never edit a stain out. If the buyer finds a flaw you didn't photograph, you've lost the money and the review. If they find one you did photograph, you've lost nothing.

The general technique for shooting garments without a model is worked through in [photographing clothes without a model](/en/blog/photographing-clothes-without-a-model), and it applies here with one change: for thrift, the flaw photos are not optional.

> A flaw you photographed is a feature of the listing. A flaw you didn't is a refund.

## Say the condition out loud

Use the same words every time, so buyers learn your scale:

- **Like new.** No flaws found. Says nothing about age.
- **Excellent.** Worn, no visible flaws at arm's length.
- **Good.** Light pilling, faded slightly, small mark noted and photographed.
- **Fair, priced down.** Real flaw, described exactly, sold cheap on purpose.

Write the specific flaw in the description in plain words: "Small ink mark on the inside of the left cuff, photo 5. Doesn't show when worn." That sentence sells more than it costs, because it tells the buyer you looked.

## Pricing one-of-one stock

You can't A/B test a price on a garment you own one of. So price by tier, not by item, or you'll spend your life deciding.

Set three or four price points and sort every piece into one. For example ₦4,500, ₦8,000, ₦15,000 and ₦25,000+ for the rare pieces. New sellers price everything in the middle; the money is at the ends. The ₦25,000 leather jacket pays for a third of the bale on its own, and the ₦4,500 tees create the traffic.

Two things to hold firm on. First, don't discount a good piece in week one because it hasn't sold. One-of-one stock has no expiry, and the right buyer for a size 44 blazer might turn up in March. Second, expect to be haggled, and price with a little room for it if your buyers do that, because refusing to move at all costs you sales in markets where negotiating is the normal way to close. There's a whole method for holding your margin through that in [pricing for a market that haggles](/en/blog/pricing-for-a-market-that-haggles).

## Taking the order without selling the same shirt twice

This is where thrift shops actually break.

You post a jacket. Four people comment "how much". You reply to all four. Two DM you. One says "send account details", one says "I'll pay tomorrow", and by Wednesday you've told two people the jacket is theirs. Now you refund one of them and they tell people.

The rule that fixes it, and it needs to be written where buyers can see it:

**The item belongs to whoever pays first. Not whoever asked first.**

Then make that mechanically true. Every garment is its own product with its own photos and its own stock count of one. When it's paid for, it goes out of stock and off the page. If your listings live only in a WhatsApp status or a carousel post, there's no such thing as out of stock, and you're doing the reconciliation in your head. That's the failure mode.

When an order comes through a shop link into WhatsApp, the message arrives already written: item, size, price, address. Nobody types the wrong price and nobody argues about which blue shirt they meant. The mechanics of that handoff are in [how to take orders on WhatsApp](/en/blog/how-to-take-orders-on-whatsapp).

On payment, bank transfer is the rail your buyers already use, and Sailo takes nothing on it because it never touches the money. What Sailo can't do is tell you the transfer arrived. Only your bank can, which means you check and mark it paid yourself. Ten minutes a day, and the method for doing it without getting scammed by a doctored screenshot is in [how to know a bank transfer actually arrived](/en/blog/how-to-know-a-bank-transfer-actually-arrived).

One more honest limitation, and it matters in Nigeria specifically. Sailo's card rail runs on Stripe, and only Stripe. There's no Paystack support and no mobile money rail. If you can't open a Stripe account where you are, the card button won't appear no matter which plan you're on, and the Business plan at ${{business_monthly}} a month buys you nothing. Check that before you plan around cards. For most Lagos thrift sellers the answer is transfers and cash, and that's a working answer, not a downgrade.

## Cash on delivery and one-of-one stock

COD is normal in Nigeria and buyers will ask for it. Understand exactly what it costs you on this kind of stock.

You send a ₦12,000 jacket with a dispatch rider. The buyer tries it, decides the sleeves are long, refuses it. You've paid the rider both ways, the jacket has been out of your hands for a day, and two people who wanted it that morning have moved on. On a shop with 400 identical units, a refusal is an inconvenience. On a shop with one of each, it's a stockout.

Reasonable middle ground, used by a lot of sellers who run this well:

- Full payment before dispatch for anything above your top price tier.
- A part payment, say ₦2,000 or half, that covers the round-trip rider cost if they refuse. Say clearly that this part is not refunded if the item comes back unworn and undamaged.
- COD only inside your own city, only on cheaper tiers, only with the measurements confirmed in writing first.

Ask for the measurement confirmation in the chat before you dispatch. "The pit to pit is 22 inches, please check it against a shirt you own, then I'll send." One message. It cuts refusals more than anything else you can do.

## Worked example: Chidera, Yaba, Lagos

Chidera buys one bale of men's shirts and jackets a month. Last one cost ₦250,000 and gave her 90 sellable pieces, so ₦2,778 each before washing. Laundry, two tailor repairs, transport and bags took her to about ₦3,400 a piece.

She lists in four tiers: ₦4,500, ₦7,500, ₦14,000 and a handful above ₦20,000. In a good month she sells 60 of the 90, roughly split 20 at ₦4,500, 25 at ₦7,500, 12 at ₦14,000 and 3 at ₦22,000. That's ₦90,000 plus ₦187,500 plus ₦168,000 plus ₦66,000, which is ₦511,500 on stock that cost her ₦306,000 landed. Call it ₦205,000 before delivery costs and her own hours.

The 30 unsold pieces roll into next month, and about half eventually sell. This is why she keeps a "last chance" tier at ₦3,000 and clears it at a physical stand every second Saturday rather than discounting online, where a cheap price on the feed drags down what people expect to pay for everything else.

The thing she changed in month three: she stopped replying to "how much" in comments and started replying with the link. Her order count didn't move much. Her evenings came back.

## What to do next

Take the ten best pieces from your current pile. Wash them, press them, and photograph the six frames on the next sunny morning. Measure each one in inches and write the four numbers into the description with the flaw sentence.

List those ten as ten separate products, each with a stock count of one, at three different price points. Then write one line into your shop and your bio, in the same words every time: *items are held for whoever pays first*.

If you're buying to resell rather than clearing your own wardrobe, the sourcing side, how to pick a bale and what a good market day looks like, is its own subject and it's covered in [starting a thrift flip business](/en/blog/starting-a-thrift-flip-business).
