The file is finished. The page is built. There's an empty box asking for a price and you've been sitting in front of it for four days, because the thing has no ingredients, no shipping weight, no supplier invoice, nothing to mark up. It cost you six weekends and that number is no help at all.

Pick $7, $19, or $47. Or the local equivalent. Write today's date next to it in a note, put the thing on sale, and change the price in three weeks when you have something to change it from. Your first price is not a promise. It's an experiment, and the only way it produces a result is if you run it.

What follows is why those numbers and not others, and how to read what happens next.

## Your first price is a test with a date on it

The reason four days go by is that you're treating the price as a permanent statement about the worth of your work. It isn't. It's a variable you get to change any Tuesday you like, and digital products are the only things you can sell where changing the price costs literally nothing. No relabelling, no repricing stickers, no supplier renegotiation. You type a different number.

So the goal isn't to be right. The goal is to be in the correct neighbourhood, quickly, and then to move.

Set a date. "Review on 27 August." Between now and then, change nothing else on the page, because if you change the price and the cover image and the description at the same time, you'll learn nothing from any of it.

## Prices live in bands, not on a line

Buyers don't experience price as a smooth scale. They experience it as a small number of categories, and the category matters much more than the exact number inside it. A ₹449 template and a ₹499 template will perform almost identically. A ₹499 template and a ₹4,999 template are different products sold to different people, even when the file is byte-for-byte the same.

Roughly, the bands are:

| Band | What the buyer does | USD | INR | GBP |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Impulse | Doesn't really decide | $5 to $12 | ₹199 to ₹599 | £3 to £9 |
| Small deliberate | Reads the page | $15 to $39 | ₹999 to ₹2,499 | £12 to £29 |
| Considered | Reads it twice, checks who you are | $47 to $120 | ₹2,999 to ₹7,999 | £39 to £95 |
| Investment | Talks to someone about it | $200+ | ₹15,000+ | £150+ |

Your job on day one is picking the band, not the number. The band is decided by what the thing replaces. A template that saves someone an afternoon is impulse or small deliberate. A system that changes how someone runs their invoicing for a year is considered. A thing that gets someone a job is investment, and priced at $12 it will be assumed to be worthless.

## Why $7 and $47 both work and $23 rarely does

There's a line in every buyer's head, and it's lower than you think. Below it, they don't evaluate. Above it, they do.

**$7 is under the line.** Nobody builds a spreadsheet for $7. They think "sure", they tap, they move on. The entire sale rests on whether the thing looks vaguely useful in the four seconds before they decide. You need volume, and you get almost no information about your buyer.

**$47 is well over the line.** The buyer switches into evaluation: what exactly do I get, will I actually use it, who is this person, is there a refund. If your page answers those four questions, $47 converts perfectly well. It converts to a better buyer, too, because someone who spent $47 opens the file. The $7 buyer often doesn't.

**$23 is the worst of both.** It's over the line, so the buyer evaluates. But it doesn't look like a thing that rewards evaluation, so the evaluation ends in "maybe later". You've paid the full cost of making someone think, and collected a third of the price for it. Worse, $23 reads as arrived-at rather than chosen. It looks like the output of a calculation, and buyers pick that up without being able to say why.

If your instinct lands on $23, go to $19 or go to $39. Same product, same buyer, much clearer signal. The number should look decided.

One convention worth knowing: prices ending in .99 read as retail, which is a category digital sellers usually don't want to be in. Round numbers and 7-endings read as chosen by a person. It's a small effect and it costs nothing to get right.

## The number next to your price does most of the selling

A price on its own is meaningless. A price next to another price is a comparison, and comparisons are what people are actually good at.

Two ways to do this, and you want both.

**Anchor against the alternative, not against other files.** Don't position your ₹499 workbook against other ₹399 workbooks. Position it against the thing the buyer would otherwise do: "One hour with me is ₹2,000. The workbook covers the first three sessions and it's ₹499." Now ₹499 isn't a price, it's a saving. This works because the buyer was never comparing you to a competitor. They were comparing you to doing nothing, or to hiring someone.

**Show three tiers and make the middle one obvious.** The top tier's real job is to make the middle one look sensible. The middle one is what you want to sell.

| Tier | Price | What's in it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The file | ₹499 | The workbook, PDF |
| The file plus | ₹1,499 | Workbook, the editable spreadsheet, email support for 30 days |
| With a call | ₹4,000 | All of it, plus a 45-minute setup call |

Two rules for this to work honestly. The top tier has to be real: if someone buys it and you'd resent delivering it, don't list it. And the anchor has to be visible on the same page at the same moment. An anchor you mention later in an email does nothing, because the comparison has already happened.

## Don't convert your price, choose it

This is where most sellers outside the US and UK go wrong. They pick $12, run it through a converter, and print ₦18,470 or ₱698 on the page.

That number is a tell. It looks like a foreign price wearing local clothes, and buyers who spot it assume, often correctly, that they're being charged a tourist rate. It also sits in no band at all, because it wasn't chosen for the market it's in.

Price natively. Round, familiar, the shape of numbers people already see:

- **Naira:** ₦2,500, ₦5,000, ₦12,000, ₦25,000
- **Rupees:** ₹199, ₹499, ₹999, ₹2,999
- **Pesos:** ₱299, ₱750, ₱1,500, ₱3,500
- **Shillings:** KSh 500, KSh 1,500, KSh 3,000
- **Pounds:** £5, £15, £39, £79

If you sell across borders and have to hold one price, pick the number that looks deliberate in your biggest market and let the others be slightly odd. Slightly odd in a secondary market beats obviously converted everywhere.

## What the price has to survive

Whatever you charge, some of it leaves before it reaches you. How much depends entirely on how you get paid, and this is the part where the honest answer about Sailo matters.

On manual rails, bank transfer, cash, a UPI ID or an M-Pesa till number written into your payment instructions, Sailo takes no commission at all. None, on any plan including the free one. The money goes straight to you and Sailo never touches it. Your only cost is whatever your bank charges, and your own time confirming each payment.

On card, three things stack up. Sailo takes 0.5% of the goods, after discount, not counting delivery or tax. Stripe takes its own fee, and Stripe's fee has a fixed component, which is what quietly destroys small orders. And card payments require the Business plan at $19.99 a month plus a Stripe account cleared for charges.

Run that against your price:

| Your price | Sailo's 0.5% | Sales/month to cover the $19.99 plan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $7 | 3.5c | 3 |
| $19 | 9.5c | 2 |
| $47 | 23.5c | 1 |

The commission is not the story. The subscription is. At $7 a copy and nine sales a month, card payments cost you more than they earn you and you should stay on manual rails until that changes. At $47, the plan pays for itself with the first sale of the month. That's a genuine argument for pricing higher rather than lower, and it's rarely made. ## Worked example: Priya in Jaipur

Notion template for freelancers who chase invoices badly. She launched at ₹199 because it felt safe and because the first thing anyone tells you is to price low to get reviews.

Two months in: reasonable number of sales, not much money, and almost nobody asking questions. That last part is the signal people miss. Buyers at ₹199 don't ask questions, because they haven't invested enough to care whether it works.

She moved it to ₹499. Sales per month dropped a little. Revenue went up by roughly two and a half times, and the messages changed completely: people started asking how to adapt it for retainer clients, which told her what to build next.

Then she added a second tier at ₹1,499 with the template, a filled-in example workspace, and 30 days of email answers. About one buyer in six takes it. That one tier earns more than the base product does, and it was two hours of work she'd already done in her own account.

What she'd do differently: launch at ₹499, not ₹199, and add the second tier on day one instead of month four. Starting low doesn't get you feedback. It gets you silence and a price you're now scared to raise.

## Reading the test, three weeks later

You need roughly 40 to 50 people to reach the page before the number means anything. Below that you're reading noise, and most first-time sellers change their price after nine visitors and four days, which tells them nothing and costs them the comparison.

What the outcomes actually mean:

- **Nobody visits.** This is not a pricing problem. Stop touching the price.
- **People visit, nobody buys.** Usually the page, not the price. Missing samples, no idea what's inside, no refund policy, no reason to trust you.
- **People buy immediately and nobody asks anything.** You're too cheap. Raise it.
- **People ask a lot and don't buy.** You're in the evaluation band and your page isn't finishing the job. Answer their questions on the page and keep the price.
- **Steady, small, and buyers use the thing.** You're in the right band. Now build the tier above it.

Change one thing at a time and give each change three weeks. Raising a price rarely halves your sales. Dropping one rarely doubles them. That asymmetry is the single most useful thing to know about pricing, and it points in one direction.

## When the answer isn't a price at all

If your product needs a class of buyer who won't pay under $100, the problem might be that you've built a file when you needed a course, or a course when you needed an hour. A workbook at ₹499 and the same material as a course at ₹4,999 are different businesses with different work attached, and the way to tell which you've got is in [selling a course without a course platform](/en/blog/selling-a-course-without-a-course-platform).

And if you're pricing time rather than a file, the whole logic inverts, because you can't sell more hours to make up for a low rate. [How to sell digital products and services online](/en/blog/how-to-sell-files-and-time-online) covers why those two need opposite pricing instincts.

## Pick your number now

Choose the band from what the thing replaces. Pick the number in that band that looks decided rather than calculated. Write the price and today's date in a note.

Then add a second tier at three to four times the base price, with the editable file or your time in it, because that tier will earn more than you expect and it makes your main price look reasonable. Set a reminder for three weeks from today, and until then, leave the number alone.
