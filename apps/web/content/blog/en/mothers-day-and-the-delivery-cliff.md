Everything you sell for Mother's Day has to arrive on one day, and that day is a Sunday, and most couriers don't deliver on Sunday.

So your real deadline is Saturday. Not "the weekend", not "by Mother's Day", Saturday, and in a lot of networks Saturday delivery is either an extra-cost service or simply isn't offered on the class of postage you normally use. Sellers plan a whole spring around the second Sunday in May and then discover in the last week that the parcel they posted on Thursday will sit in a depot until Monday. A $38 cookie box arriving on Monday is a refund.

Work backwards from Saturday, cap what you can make, and stop taking orders when the cap is gone. That's the entire plan and everything else is detail.

## The date moves, and it moves differently in different countries

If you sell across borders at all, this catches people out badly, because "Mother's Day" is not one date.

- **United States, Canada, Australia, Philippines, India, most of Latin America:** the second Sunday in May.
- **United Kingdom and Ireland:** Mothering Sunday, which is the fourth Sunday in Lent. That's in March, and because it's tied to Easter it moves by up to a month year to year.
- **Much of the Arab world, including Egypt and the Gulf:** the 21st of March.
- **Mexico:** the 10th of May, whatever day of the week that falls on.
- **Thailand, Indonesia and others:** their own fixed dates entirely.

Two consequences. If you have UK buyers and US buyers, you have two Mother's Days, roughly seven weeks apart, and you should run them as two separate events with separate stock. And if you have Mexican buyers, note that theirs is a fixed date and can land midweek, which removes the Sunday problem and creates a different one: a weekday delivery to someone who's at work.

Check the actual date each year. Don't assume, and definitely don't assume last year's date shifted by exactly a week.

## Why the Sunday is the whole problem

Three things stack up and they all point the same way.

**Standard delivery doesn't run on Sunday in most networks.** Some carriers do Sunday delivery for specific services or specific customers, usually at a premium. Your ordinary parcel service almost certainly doesn't. So Friday and Saturday become the only two acceptable arrival days, and everything upstream compresses into them.

**Saturday delivery often costs extra or requires an upgraded service.** Look up what your courier actually charges for a Saturday-guaranteed delivery before you price your product, not after you've taken 60 orders. On a $38 box, a $9 upgrade is a quarter of your margin.

**Late has no value at all.** This is the difference between Mother's Day and almost every other seasonal peak. A birthday present a day late is fine. A Christmas parcel on the 20th is fine. A Mother's Day parcel on Monday is a thing the buyer has to apologise for, and they will make that your fault, reasonably.

Early, though, is completely fine. Nobody minds a gift arriving on the Wednesday before. This asymmetry is the lever: aim for Thursday or Friday arrival, treat Saturday as your emergency buffer, and never plan to use it.

## Backwards from Saturday

Fill this in with real dates on a calendar. It takes ten minutes and it's the difference between a good May and a bad one.

| Step | How to set it |
| --- | --- |
| Target arrival | Thursday or Friday before. Saturday is the buffer, not the plan |
| Dispatch | Courier's stated transit time, plus one day, because peak weeks run slow |
| Pack and label by | The evening before dispatch |
| Make by | Daily output multiplied by the days you'll genuinely work |
| Published order cut-off | Whichever comes first: the cap running out, or four days before dispatch |
| Materials ordered | Your supplier's lead time plus a week. Flowers and perishables have their own rules |
| Announce | Two weeks before the cut-off, so the first wave has somewhere to go |

The "plus one day" on transit is not padding. Peak weeks run slower than the published estimate because the whole network is full, and the published time is a good-week number.

## Capacity is the number that decides everything

Your cut-off is not a date you pick. It's the day your capacity runs out, and you can calculate it before you take a single order.

Count what you can genuinely finish in a day, on an ordinary day, not your best one. Multiply by the days you'll work between now and the make-by date. Subtract the orders you already have. That's how many Mother's Day orders you can accept.

Then publish the number, and publish the countdown. "60 boxes, 22 left." It does four things at once: it caps your downside, it makes the urgency real instead of manufactured, it gives you an honest reason to close, and it tells you exactly when to place your materials order.

> A published cap is a boundary you can hold on the Thursday night. A published date is a boundary people will argue with.

Overselling capacity is the most common way this goes wrong, and it doesn't feel like a mistake while it's happening. It feels like a great week.

## The address problem is worse here

Mother's Day orders go to third parties even more than Valentine's does, and half the addresses you're given are typed from memory by someone who last posted a letter there in 2019.

The habit that fixes most of it: read the whole address back in your confirmation message and require a reply. Not a summary. The actual lines, formatted as you'll print them on the label.

> Confirming: 1 x Spring box, $38, shipping to Margaret Hall, 1140 Woodland St Apt 3B, Nashville TN 37206. Arriving Thursday or Friday. Reply YES and I'll get it packed.

You'll get corrections on a handful of orders, and every one of those was a parcel that would have come back to you on the Monday after, when it was worthless. It's the cheapest quality control in the business.

Three more, all learned the hard way:

**Ask whether it's a home or a workplace.** A parcel to an office on Friday afternoon may sit in a mailroom over the weekend.
**Ask whether the recipient knows it's coming.** If she doesn't, you can't call her about a delivery problem, and you'll need the buyer's number instead.
**Never put a price-bearing invoice inside a gift box.** Your packing routine is automatic eleven months of the year. This is the month it shouldn't be.

The full version of the confirmation habit is in [how to write an order confirmation](/en/blog/how-to-write-an-order-confirmation).

## Selling early rather than discounting

You will not run out of demand. You'll run out of Thursdays.

So the promotional job isn't to create more orders, it's to move existing orders earlier. Discounting does the opposite of what you want here: it costs you margin on orders you were going to get anyway, and it does nothing for a buyer who hasn't started thinking about Mother's Day yet.

What moves orders earlier:

- **A cap with a visible countdown.** The strongest tool available and it costs nothing.
- **A dispatch date guarantee for early orders.** "Order by the 24th and I'll confirm your dispatch day in writing." Late orders get "we'll do our best."
- **Free gift wrapping or a handwritten card until a date.** Costs you 60 cents and reads as generous.
- **A pre-order that opens three weeks out** with a stated dispatch day. Gets you the money for materials before you have to spend it.

If you do want to run a discount for some other reason, the arithmetic on what it costs you is in [how to run a discount without losing money](/en/blog/how-to-run-a-discount-without-losing-money).

## What to do when it's going to be late

At some point a parcel won't make it. The rule is simple: tell the buyer 48 hours before, not 48 hours after.

A buyer told on Wednesday that their gift will arrive Monday can do something about it. They can buy flowers locally, they can call, they can adjust. A buyer told on Monday has been embarrassed and there's nothing you can do to fix that except refund, which doesn't fix it.

Have the message drafted before the week starts:

> I'm sorry, I've got a problem with your order and I'd rather tell you now than let you find out Sunday. It won't arrive before Monday. I'm refunding you in full and posting it anyway, so she still gets it, and you don't pay for something that turned up late. If you'd rather I just cancel, say the word.

Refund and ship is expensive and it's almost always the right call at this volume. You'll lose $38. You'll keep somebody who buys every May.

## Worked example: Jess, cookie boxes, Nashville

Jess decorates iced sugar-cookie boxes from a home kitchen in East Nashville. A box of nine is $38, a box of eighteen is $68. Materials, packaging and shipping run about $19 on the small box.

Her constraint is decorating, not baking. She can bake 200 cookies in a day but she can only ice and flood about 45 to a decent standard, which is five small boxes an hour on a good run.

Her plan:

- **Target arrival:** Thursday and Friday before the second Sunday in May.
- **Ships Tuesday** by a two-day service, which gives her Wednesday to Friday of slack.
- **Make-by is Monday.** She works the preceding Thursday, Friday, Saturday and Monday, four decorating days, roughly 40 boxes a day at the pace she can hold. Call it 140 boxes with room for error.
- **Cap: 120 boxes.** Published as a countdown in her bio and on the product page.
- **Cut-off:** whenever the cap runs out, or the Thursday ten days before, whichever is first.
- **Six local pickup slots** held back for the Friday and Saturday, at $45, for people who missed everything.

The first year she ran it without a cap, took 190 orders, and iced cookies until 4am four nights running. Eleven boxes went out on the Thursday and arrived Monday. She refunded all eleven and made $1,900 less than the order total said she had.

With the cap: 120 boxes, sold out eight days before the cut-off, all shipped on the Tuesday, two late deliveries, both flagged to the buyer on the Wednesday before. The revenue was lower on paper than her uncapped year and higher in her bank account.

The number she keeps: 45 iced cookies a day. Everything else in the plan is derived from it. If you don't know your equivalent number, you don't have a plan, you have a hope.

## The honest limitation

If you want card payments on a Sailo shop, two things have to be true at once. You need a connected Stripe account that Stripe has actually cleared for charges. Sailo takes {{fee_range}}% of the goods after discount, excluding delivery and tax, on top of Stripe's own cut. On Jess's $38 box that's 19 cents, which is nothing.

The subscription is the real cost, and the timing is the real risk. Stripe clearance is not instant and it can ask for documents. Signing up in the last week of April and expecting to take cards on the first of May is how sellers end up taking bank transfers by DM during their busiest fortnight instead. If cards matter to you for this peak, set it up in February.

On every other rail, bank transfer, cash, WhatsApp, Instagram, Telegram, email and phone, Sailo never touches the money and takes nothing, which also means it can't tell you the money arrived. You confirm those yourself.

## Do this eight weeks out

Work out your real daily output number. Multiply it out, subtract a margin for the day something breaks, and write the cap down. Then set your dispatch date backwards from Thursday arrival, and your cut-off backwards from that.

Order materials this week, not next month. Ask your courier, in writing, what Saturday delivery costs on the service you use and whether it's guaranteed.

Then write the address read-back message and the going-to-be-late message before the season starts, so you're not writing either of them tired.
