The most common mistake is photographing the whole menu. The second is photographing it at night, under the kitchen lights, on a stainless steel counter.

You are not making a catalogue. You are making someone hungry.

## One window is a studio

Daylight from the side, at an angle, is the only light you need. Put the plate on a table near a window, stand so the light comes across the food rather than from behind you, and shoot.

Turn the ceiling lights **off**. Mixed light — warm bulbs plus cool daylight — is what makes home photography look like home photography.

## Shoot the best seller first

If you photograph five dishes and three are blurry, you have wasted an afternoon. Photograph one dish properly:

- Top-down for anything flat: pizza, bowls, boards.
- Forty-five degrees for anything with height: burgers, stacks, cake.
- Close enough that the food fills the frame. Then closer.

## The details that sell

| What you show | What the buyer reads |
| --- | --- |
| Steam, or a cut edge | It is hot, it is fresh |
| A hand reaching in | It is real, someone eats this |
| An empty plate beside it | It is a portion, not a prop |

## Then stop

Six good photographs will carry a shop for a year. Take those, put a price under each one, and go back to cooking.

The same one-window rule works for anything you sell, not just food — there's a fuller version in [product photography for small sellers](/en/blog/how-to-photograph-what-you-sell). Once the pictures are done, the next thing that decides whether someone orders is [what you write underneath them](/en/blog/writing-product-descriptions-that-sell).
