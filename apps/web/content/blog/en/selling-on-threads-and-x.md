You posted a clean photo of the thing you make, wrote "back in stock", and it got two likes from people who already own one.

Meanwhile a throwaway reply you left on somebody else's post got 40,000 views.

That is the whole lesson about selling on Threads, and most of the lesson about X. Neither is a shopfront. There is no product tag, no cart, no checkout, and no meaningful product search. What they are is conversation, and conversation is where a stranger decides whether you are worth buying from. The order itself happens on a page you own, reached from your bio or from a link in a reply. Threads has been rolling out multiple profile links, up to five last time we looked, and X gives you one website field. A Sailo shop is $0 on the free plan for {{free_products}} products and sits at `sailo.store/yourname`, so it fits in a bio with room left over for an actual sentence.

Go and look at your own edit-profile screen before you plan around any of that. Link fields on both platforms have changed repeatedly and they will change again.

## What each one is actually good at

They feel similar and they behave differently.

**Threads** hands reach to people with no followers. That is unusual and it is the reason a seller with 300 followers can have a post seen by 60,000 people. The trade is that the audience arriving is broad, uninterested and passing through. High reach, low intent.

**X** gives less free reach to a small account, especially without Premium, and gives more to a niche. If you sell something with a community around it, mechanical keyboards, film photography, sourdough starters, single-origin coffee, X still has that community and they still talk to each other. Lower reach, much higher intent.

The practical consequence: on Threads you are building recognition, on X you are building relationships. The first turns into sales in three months. The second turns into sales this week, but only if you are already in the conversation.

## The post that sells is not the post about your product

This is the part people resist, and it is the reason most sellers give up on both platforms inside a month.

A text feed rewards a sentence somebody wants to repeat. It does not reward a product announcement, because a product announcement is an advert and everyone can feel one coming from the first three words. "New drop, link in bio" is a sign you hung in a room where people came to talk.

What works instead, and this is not a trick, it is just what the format is for:

- **The specific complaint.** "Third courier this month who has decided a candle is a liquid." People who make things reply to that.
- **The number nobody shares.** "A £14 candle costs me £4.10 in wax, glass and wick, £1.90 in postage and about 25 minutes."
- **The opinion you would defend.** "Soy wax being 'better' is marketing. It throws scent worse and it frosts."
- **The behind-it photo with a caption that is a sentence, not a caption.** The mess, the failed batch, the 200 labels you printed upside down.

Every one of those makes the product visible without being about the product. Someone reads it, taps your name, and lands on a profile with a link. That is the funnel. It is slow and it is the only one that works on a text platform.

The post that sells on Threads is almost never the one with the product photo. It is the reply you left on somebody else's post at eleven at night, which is exactly why sellers who only post and never reply conclude that Threads does not work.

> On a text platform, your profile is the shop and your posts are the reason to visit it. Get that the wrong way round and you will post for six weeks and sell nothing.

## Your bio has about twelve seconds of a stranger's attention

Someone read one good post and tapped your name. What they see next decides everything, and most seller bios waste it on a job title.

Three things belong in a bio on either platform:

1. **What you sell, in nouns.** "Small-batch hot sauce, Chicago." Not "founder, maker, chaos".
2. **A price or a range.** A number in a bio does more work than any adjective. It filters out people who were never going to buy and it stops the first DM being "how much".
3. **One link that is a shop, not a menu.**

That third one matters more on these platforms than anywhere else, because the visitor arrived from a text post and has approximately no patience. A link that opens a page of buttons pointing at other pages is a second decision you are asking a stranger to make. If Threads gives you five link slots, that is not permission to use five. Use one for the shop and, if you must, one for the newsletter. The rest are noise. ## X and the link problem

Sellers on X have said for years that posts containing an external link get less reach than plain text or native video. You will find confident percentages quoted for this. Treat all of them with suspicion, including the ones in the platform's own history of statements, because nobody outside the company can measure it and the ranking changes.

What is not in doubt is the habit that grew out of it. Sellers post the thought on its own, then put the link in the first reply to their own post. It costs nothing, it takes four seconds, and if the reach penalty is real you have avoided it, and if it is folklore you have lost nothing.

Two things that habit does badly, and you should plan for both:

- **The reply is a second tap.** Fewer people take it. Which means the main post has to be able to sell on its own, with the price in it, so that a reader who never taps the reply still knows what the thing costs and what your shop is called.
- **Replies get buried.** On a post with 200 replies, yours is somewhere in the middle. Pin the post to your profile instead, and make the pinned post the one that has the price, the photo and the shop name written out as text.

The pinned post is the single most underused thing on X for a small seller. It is permanent, it sits above everything, and it is the first thing a stranger who tapped your name reads after your bio.

## DMs on a platform where strangers can message you

Both platforms let people message you, and both let you restrict who can. Check your own settings, because the defaults have changed and a seller with DMs closed to non-followers is turning away buyers without knowing it.

Once they are open, the practical problem is the same one Instagram sellers have: a DM conversation about an order takes six messages and you will forget half of them. What it costs, what colour, what size, where do I send it, how do I pay, did you get it.

The fix is not a better DM technique. It is to answer with a link to a page that already contains the answers, and to keep the conversation for the things a page cannot do. The full version of that argument, written for Instagram but true on any chat surface, is [turning Instagram DMs into orders](/en/blog/turning-instagram-dms-into-orders).

One thing worth knowing about X specifically: a stranger who DMs you about a product on X is often further along than a stranger on any other platform, because they had to read a thread to get there. Answer those fast. A four-hour reply to an Instagram DM is normal. A four-hour reply on X is often too late, because the conversation that produced the interest has already scrolled away.

## What to do when a post takes off and you have nothing to sell them

Text platforms produce spikes that are useless if your profile is not ready, and they produce them at random.

Before you post anything else this week, make sure the following are true, because you will not have time when it happens:

| The thing | Why it matters on a text feed |
| --- | --- |
| Price visible on the shop page without a tap | The visitor arrived from a sentence and will leave for a loading screen |
| Stock counts set | Spikes are national and instant; you will oversell |
| A pinned post with the product and the price | The reach lands on your profile, not the post |
| Bio link points at the shop, not a menu | One decision, not two |
| Shipping stated in one line | Half the replies to a viral post are "do you ship to..." |

None of that takes an afternoon. All of it is worthless after the spike has passed.

## Marcus in Chicago, $11 a bottle

Marcus makes hot sauce in Chicago. One habanero, one smoked serrano, $11 a bottle, $28 for a three-pack, flat $6 US shipping.

He spent four months posting bottle photos to Threads with "in stock" underneath and got, by his own count, eleven orders. He assumed the platform was not for physical products.

Then he changed what he posted, not where. He started writing about the failures: the batch that separated, the label glue that came off in a fridge, the eight-week fight with a co-packer, the fact that his first hundred bottles were filled with a jug and a funnel at a kitchen counter. One post about why he refuses to put xanthan gum in a sauce did around 90,000 views and 400 replies, most of them arguing.

His bio at the time said "Chicago. Hot sauce. DM for orders." He changed it to "Small-batch hot sauce, Chicago. $11 a bottle, ships US. sailo.store/marcusheat" and stopped taking orders in DMs entirely.

Orders went to somewhere between fifty and eighty a month, lumpy, spiking whenever one of the argument posts did numbers. He runs the same thing on X, gets maybe a fifth of the reach and a comparable number of orders, because the people arguing about xanthan gum on X are the people who buy hot sauce.

The line he says about it: he sold more sauce by admitting a batch failed than by photographing the good ones.

## When to stop bothering

This section costs us a sale and it is still the right advice.

If you sell something visual and impulsive, jewellery, food, clothes, candles, a text feed is the wrong shape for it and you will do better spending those hours on video. If your product needs to be seen from three angles, Threads and X are a bad trade for your evenings. [Which platform should you actually sell on](/en/blog/which-platform-should-you-actually-sell-on) works through that choice properly.

If you have no opinions about your own trade, or no appetite for having them in public, do not force it. A seller who posts opinions they do not hold gets found out fast, and the platform where that happens fastest is X.

And if you are already getting orders from one platform, adding a second one badly is worse than running the first one well. Two half-tended feeds is the most common way small sellers spend twelve hours a week for nothing.

## Do this this week

1. Rewrite your bio on both platforms so it contains a noun, a number and one link.
2. Pin a post with the product, the price and the shop name spelled out as text.
3. Write five posts that are not about your product but could only have been written by someone who makes it.
4. Reply to ten other people's posts a day for two weeks. Set a timer for fifteen minutes so it does not eat the evening.
5. Put the link in the first reply on X, not in the post.

Then check where your shop traffic comes from after a month. If direct visits show up alongside link clicks, the name is doing its job.
