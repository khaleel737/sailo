"Could you do that one in green, but with my dog on it, and could I have it by the 14th?"

Price the work, not the object. Then take the money in stages tied to the customer approving something, never to a date in the calendar, and write down what finished looks like before you start. Almost everything that goes wrong with custom orders is one problem wearing different clothes: nobody agreed what "done" meant, so the job has no end, and a job with no end has no price.

A $450 custom piece that takes four hours is a good job. The same piece with eleven messages, two mockups, a colour change and a rush charge you didn't apply is a $450 job that took nine hours, and you'll notice the difference around the third one.

## Where the money actually goes on a custom job

The hours you charge for are not the hours you spend. Everyone knows this and almost nobody prices for it, because the invisible hours don't feel like work while they're happening.

Here's a realistic breakdown of a $450 custom sign, quoted as "about five hours".

| What happened | Hours | Charged for? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nine messages agreeing what they wanted | 0.75 | No |
| Mocking up two layout options | 1.0 | No |
| Sourcing a colour you don't stock | 0.5 | No |
| Making it | 4.0 | Yes |
| One revision after they saw a photo | 1.0 | No |
| Packing, courier booking, chasing the balance | 0.75 | No |
| Photographing it for your own feed | 0.5 | No, and it's the only one that pays you back |

Eight and a half hours against four charged. That's not a customer being unreasonable. It's an ordinary, pleasant, well-behaved custom order, and if you quoted it at your hourly rate for the making time you're working for roughly half of what you think you are.

The fix isn't charging double. It's building the invisible hours into the number, and then protecting them with a definition of done.

## Build the number from four parts

**Materials, at what they cost you today.** Not what they cost last year. If you have to buy something specially, it goes in at full price plus whatever the delivery was.

**Making time at your rate.** If you don't have a rate, [how to price what you make](/en/blog/how-to-price-what-you-make) is the place to start, because everything below assumes you know your hourly number.

**A specificity premium.** This is the part people skip. The narrower the request, the higher the price, because the narrower it is, the less chance anyone else on earth would buy it if the customer walks. A sign that says "Welcome to the Kowalczyk Family Farm" has exactly one possible buyer. Charge for that. Between 15% and 30% on top of your normal rate is defensible and nobody has ever asked you to itemise it.

**A revision allowance.** Decide how many changes are included, price them in, and say the number out loud. Two is the right answer for most work. One feels mean and gets argued with. Three is a project.

Add them, round up to something a person can say, and quote that. One number, not a breakdown. A breakdown invites line-by-line negotiation and you will lose that negotiation, because you know what each line costs and they only know what they want to pay.

## The three-part quote

Every custom quote you send should have three sections, and it should fit on a phone screen without scrolling much.

> **The job.** A 24-inch routed cedar sign, one colour, reading "Welcome to the Kowalczyk Family Farm", hung mounts on the back. Ready for pickup by Friday 14 August.
>
> **The price.** $520 total. $260 to start, $260 when you approve the photo before it's sealed.
>
> **What's included.** Two rounds of changes at the design stage. After the design is approved, changes are quoted separately because the wood's already cut.

Three short blocks and you've eliminated most of the arguments this order was ever going to produce. Note what the third block does: it doesn't forbid changes. It prices them and it tells the customer exactly when the free window closes.

The words "because the wood's already cut" matter more than the policy they explain. A customer will push back on a rule and will not push back on a physical fact.

## Define done before you define price

The single most useful habit in custom work is naming the moment the job ends, in the quote, in a way that's checkable by both of you.

Bad: "until you're happy with it."

Good: "when you approve the mockup, I cut. When you approve the photo of the finished piece, I seal it and it ships."

Two approval gates, both requiring a "yes" from the customer, both moving the job into a state where changes cost money. That's a definition of done a reasonable person can't argue with, and it converts an open-ended relationship into a sequence with an exit.

Get the approval in writing, even if writing means a message that says "yes, go ahead". Screenshot it if you're worried. This isn't paranoia, it's the thing you'll want on the day someone says the green is wrong and you can show them the green they approved.

## Stage the money against approvals, not dates

Payment stages tied to the calendar fail, because the calendar slips and then you're chasing money for work that isn't visibly done. Payment stages tied to approvals never fail, because the customer only approves when they've seen something they like, and someone who's just said "that's perfect" is the easiest person in the world to ask for money.

| Stage | Trigger | Share | Why here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Booking | They say yes to the quote | 50% | Covers materials and ends the shopping |
| Design approved | They approve the mockup | 0% or 25% | Only worth a payment on jobs over a week |
| Before it ships | They approve the finished photo | The balance | They're delighted and they haven't got it yet |
| After delivery | Nothing | 0% | Never. You've already handed it over |

Two stages for most work. Three for anything over about three weeks or a couple of thousand dollars, where carrying half the cost for a month is a real drag on a small shop.

That last row is not negotiable and it's the mistake almost every new maker makes once. The balance is due before the thing leaves your hands. Not on delivery, not "when they've had a chance to look at it", not net 14 unless you're selling to a company. The wording that makes the up-front half feel normal rather than suspicious is worked out in [taking a deposit before you start](/en/blog/taking-a-deposit-before-you-start).

## Revisions: give two, price the third

Say the number in the quote. "Two rounds of changes included." Then track them, out loud and cheerfully.

When they ask for the second change: "That's the second of your two, happy to do it."

When they ask for a third: "That's outside the two rounds, so it'd be $60 and adds two days. Want me to?"

No annoyance, no lecture, no reference to what you agreed. Just a price and a date, offered as a service. The customer who's genuinely being fussy pays it or drops it, and either outcome is fine. The customer who didn't realise they'd used their rounds apologises and stops.

What makes this work is having said "two" at the start. Introducing a limit halfway through a job reads as a punishment. Naming it in the quote reads as a professional standard, and customers actively like sellers who have standards.

## The one sentence that handles scope creep

Never say no. Say the price and the new date.

> Happy to add the second line of text. It's another $80 and pushes pickup to the 19th. Want me to go ahead?

That sentence has never once caused an argument, and it works because it treats the request as legitimate, which it usually is. The customer isn't trying to get free work. They're imagining, and imagining is free for them and expensive for you, and the only thing that connects those two facts is a number.

Three things it does at once. It says yes, so nobody gets defensive. It attaches a cost, so the request becomes a decision. It attaches a date, which is often the part that stops them, because the reason they wanted a custom piece was usually an event.

Use it every time, verbatim, including for small things. Especially for small things. The $15 additions are how a job quietly loses its margin, and the seller who charges for the big changes and eats the small ones ends up eating fourteen small ones.

## The free mockup problem

You'll get enquiries that want a design before they'll commit. Some are serious. Many are collecting free concepts from four makers and choosing the cheapest, or have no budget at all and are enjoying the process.

Charge for the mockup. $40, $50, whatever's about 10% of a typical job, and credit it in full against the order if they go ahead.

You'll lose enquiries. That's the point. The ones you lose are the ones that were going to cost you a Sunday afternoon and produce nothing, and the ones that stay are people who have decided. A mockup fee is the cheapest filter in this entire business and almost nobody uses it.

If you can't bring yourself to charge, at least cap it: one rough concept, hand-drawn or in low resolution, ten minutes maximum, before any money moves. Never send a finished-looking design to someone who hasn't paid you anything. Finished-looking designs get taken to someone else with a laser cutter.

The same logic applies to commissioned art and illustration, where the norms are slightly different and the file itself is the product. [Selling digital art and commissions](/en/blog/selling-digital-art-and-commissions) covers that variant.

## When to say no to the whole thing

Some custom enquiries should be declined and it's a skill to spot them early.

- **They lead with the budget and it's below your materials.** Not a negotiation. A different business.
- **The date is impossible and they're relaxed about it.** People who are relaxed about an impossible date are people who will not be relaxed on the 13th.
- **They've already fired another maker.** Sometimes the other maker was bad. Ask what happened, and listen to whether the story has a villain.
- **You can't picture it after three messages.** If you can't see it, you can't quote it, and you'll discover the gap after you've cut something.
- **It's outside what you do, but only slightly.** The slightly-outside job is the dangerous one, because you'll say yes and then learn a new technique at your own expense.

Declining is one line: "That's not really my thing, but it's exactly what [name] does." Referring the job costs you nothing and buys you a relationship with the other maker, who will refer back.

## Worked example: Ana, hand-painted signs, Austin

Ana paints wooden signs and does around twelve custom pieces a month out of a garage in East Austin, between $180 and $700.

Her first year was a mess. She quoted by size, so a 24-inch sign was $220 whatever was on it. A wedding seating chart with forty-one names took eleven hours and paid the same as a "The Garcias" house sign that took two. She'd send full-colour mockups to anyone who asked and about one in four ordered.

Four changes, made over about six months:

**She priced by element, not by size.** Base price for the board, plus per line of text, plus a flat charge for any illustration. Same effort to quote, and the seating chart is now $610 instead of $220.

**She added a mockup fee of $45**, credited against the order. Her enquiries halved. Her orders went up slightly. The Sunday afternoons came back.

**Two stages, both tied to approvals.** Half at booking, half when she sends the photo of the finished piece before it's sealed and packed. She has never had a balance go unpaid, because the customer is looking at a photo of the exact thing they wanted at the moment she asks.

**Two revisions, stated in the quote.** She used to accept changes until she got bored and then feel resentful, which is a worse system than any policy.

The thing she says was worth more than all four: she stopped quoting in the DMs. Every enquiry now gets the same three-block message pasted into a reply, filled in, sent as one thing. Before that, price, timeline and inclusions arrived in five separate messages over two days, and customers only ever remembered the price.

Her rush charge is 30% for anything under ten days, and she says it out loud without apology. Roughly a third of people pay it. The other two thirds move their date, which is what she actually wanted.

## What Sailo does here, and what it doesn't

Custom work is where a shop platform helps least, and it's worth being straight about that.

There's no quote builder. No way to send a customer a one-off price for a job that isn't already a product. No revision tracking, no approval gates, no staged payments, no deposit field. An order is `unpaid`, `pending`, `paid` or `refunded`, with nothing in between, so a half-paid custom job is a thing you're tracking yourself.

What does work:

- **A "custom order" listing at your booking price.** List it like any product, priced at the deposit, with product options for the choices you actually offer. The buyer orders it, and you have a real order with a reference instead of a conversation.
- **Options on the product** so the order arrives with the choices attached rather than scattered across a chat. When a buyer taps through to WhatsApp from a Sailo shop, the message arrives pre-written with item, options, address and total, which is roughly a quote confirmation you didn't have to type.
- **The services setup** if what you're selling is time. Duration, location, a date picker and a notice period, which is the field that stops someone booking you for tomorrow morning.
- **Digital delivery** if the output is a file. It unlocks on payment confirmation, with download limits and expiry available.

## Write your three-block quote today

Take your last custom order and rewrite it as the three blocks: the job in one specific sentence, the price with its stages, and what's included with the revision number named.

Save it as a template with blanks. Send it as one message on the next enquiry, before you've had six exchanges about colour.

Then add up the invisible hours on that last job, honestly, including the messages. If the number surprises you, your quote is the thing to change this week, not your work rate.
