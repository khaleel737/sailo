Three orders in June. You had 31 in December. Nothing has changed about the product, the photos or the posting, and you're now doing the thing where you refresh the shop page hoping it's a caching problem.

First job is to work out whether this is a season or a decline, and the test takes about ten minutes. Then, if it's a season, the quiet months get spent on the three things you physically cannot do when it's busy: rebuilding the catalogue, working the customer list, and ordering the stock for the next peak.

The thing not to do is discount your way through it. A summer sale on winter product converts almost nobody who wasn't going to buy anyway, and it teaches the people who were going to buy in October to wait.

## Season or decline

Two different problems, opposite responses, so get this right first.

**If you have last year's numbers**, compare the same month to the same month. June against June, not June against December. If this June is roughly last June, you have a season. If this June is half of last June, you have a decline dressed as a season, and no amount of catalogue tidying will fix it.

**If you don't have last year's numbers**, which is most people in year one, use these three checks instead:

1. **Does your category have an obvious shape?** Knitwear, swimwear, school supplies, gifting, exam tutoring, wedding anything. If yes, you probably have a season and you can find the shape by asking any older seller in your category.
2. **Are enquiries down as well as orders?** If people are still asking and not buying, that's a conversion problem, not a season. If nobody's asking, demand has genuinely gone somewhere else.
3. **Is it down everywhere or just one channel?** A drop that's only on Instagram is a reach problem. A drop across your link, your DMs and your repeat customers is a market.

The commonest misread is a seller in month five with no history, deciding a normal quiet stretch is a failure, and quitting in it. If enquiries are down and it's the same month your whole category goes quiet, that's the calendar, not you.

There's a version of this that isn't a season at all, and it's worth ruling out early. If you've never had a busy month, you don't have a quiet one. You have a start, and the work is different: it's forty individual messages rather than a catalogue rebuild, which is what [getting your first ten orders](/en/blog/getting-your-first-ten-orders) walks through.

If it's genuinely a decline, this article is the wrong one. [What to do when nobody is buying](/en/blog/what-to-do-when-nobody-is-buying) is the diagnostic for that, in order of what's cheapest to check.

## Money first, before anything else

A quiet month is a cash problem before it's a marketing problem, and the cash problem has a deadline attached that the marketing one doesn't.

Work out three numbers this week:

- **What leaves your account every month regardless of sales.** Subscriptions, storage, a phone plan, insurance. Total it. It's usually more than people expect.
- **How many months of that you can cover** from what you have.
- **When the next stock payment is due**, and whether the quiet months land before or after it.

That third one is where people get caught. Quiet months are exactly when the reordering deadline for the peak falls, because suppliers need lead time. The shop that's dead in July is the shop that's out of stock in November, and the reason is that July felt like the wrong month to spend £900 on yarn.

If cash is tight, cut recurring costs now rather than in month three of quiet. A subscription you can pause in June and restart in September is money you get to spend on stock instead.

## The four jobs worth doing, in order

Not a list of busywork. These are ranked by return, and the first one beats the other three combined.

### 1. Message every past customer, individually

This is the highest-return work available to a small seller in a quiet month, and it's the one that gets skipped because it feels like begging.

It isn't begging if the message carries something. Take your order list. Message everyone who bought in the last year, by name, one at a time, with something specific to them. Not a broadcast.

> Hi Rhian, hope you're well. You got the grey cable jumper last November. I'm doing the wool order for this winter in the next fortnight, is there a colour you'd actually wear? Genuinely asking, it shapes what I buy.

That message does three things. It reminds a warm customer you exist during the months they'd otherwise forget. It asks a question with a real answer, which gets replies where "hope you're well!" doesn't. And the answers are free market research at the exact moment you need it, because you're about to spend money on stock.

Some of them will buy out of season. Not many. That isn't the point. The point is that a customer messaged in July orders in October, and one who isn't, doesn't.

Budget about two minutes per message and do them in batches of fifteen. Seventy-four customers is roughly two and a half hours of work spread over a fortnight, which is less than you'll spend making a single post you'll delete.

### 2. Rebuild the catalogue properly

You've been meaning to reshoot half your products since March.

Quiet months are the only time this is possible, and the return is real but delayed. The June you spend rewriting 40 product descriptions is the December you don't spend answering "what size is this" two hundred times.

The order to do it in:

- Fix the products that get the most questions, not the ones that look worst. Questions are a measurable defect.
- Put every measurement in the text. Not in the image.
- Reshoot the best-seller first, then the second best. Do not start at product one.
- Write the delivery cost on every listing, including the international one if you take those orders.

### 3. Build the list for the next peak

Whoever's on your contact list in November decides how good November is. Building it in November is too late.

Quiet months are cheap months to grow a list, because you're not competing with everyone else's launch. Collect names, emails, numbers, whatever you can reach directly. And if your next peak involves a launch rather than a steady trickle, [building a waiting list for a drop](/en/blog/building-a-waiting-list-for-a-drop) is a three-week job you can start any time.

### 4. Fix the one process that broke last peak

There's always one. Packing took too long, you ran out of a box size, you double-sold a variant, the delivery quotes were wrong for Northern Ireland, you didn't sleep for four days.

Pick the worst one. Fix it now, while it costs an afternoon instead of a Christmas. Then write down what you did, because in eleven months you won't remember.

## Why not to discount

The obvious move in a quiet month is a sale, and it usually costs more than it makes.

Three reasons.

Out-of-season demand is small and price-insensitive. The handful of people who want a wool jumper in June want it for a specific reason, usually a trip somewhere cold, and they're not waiting for 20% off.

You're borrowing from the peak. Every customer who learns you discount in the quiet months is a customer who might wait next time. That's a permanent tax on your best months in exchange for a few orders in your worst.

And a discount doesn't fix the actual problem, which is that fewer people want the thing right now. You can't discount your way to a demand that hasn't arrived yet.

The exception is genuinely dead stock. Last season's colourway that will never sell at full price is a different thing from this season's product in the wrong month, and clearing it is a legitimate quiet-month job. [End-of-year stock clearing](/en/blog/end-of-year-stock-clearing) covers that without wrecking your pricing.

## Counter-seasonal products, honestly

The obvious idea is to sell something else in the off months. Sometimes that works and often it's a distraction.

It works when the new thing uses the same skill, the same supplier and the same customer. A knitter selling wool kits in summer to the same people who buy jumpers in winter is a real business. A knitter selling phone cases in summer is starting a second business badly.

The test is whether you can describe it in one sentence to an existing customer and have it make sense. If it needs explaining, it's a different shop.

There's a cheaper version worth trying first: sell the same product to a different season. Wool jumpers sell in June to people going somewhere cold. Cake sells in the quiet weeks for exam results, new jobs, apologies. The demand exists, it's smaller, and it costs you nothing to test with three posts.

## Sian's summers, in Cardiff

Sian hand-knits jumpers in Cardiff. £58 for the standard cable, £74 for the chunky, £4.50 postage in the UK. Her year has a shape you could set a watch by.

| Month | Orders |
|---|---|
| October | 14 |
| November | 26 |
| December | 31 |
| January | 6 |
| February to May | 3 to 5 each |
| June | 3 |
| July | 2 |
| August | 4 |
| September | 9 |

Year one, she spent June to August posting more, then discounting to £45 in July, and sold four jumpers at a price that barely covered the wool.

Year two she did none of that. In June she messaged 74 past customers individually over about two weeks. Thirty-one replied. Two bought immediately. But 22 of the replies named a colour, and one colour came up eleven times, which is why she ordered nine kilos of a mustard she'd never have chosen.

She reshot her top four listings in July with measurements in the text, and she put the postage cost on every page.

October to December of year two: 84 orders against 71 the year before, at full price, and the mustard sold out by the end of November.

The unglamorous part: her wool supplier needed the order by the second week of July for October delivery. That deadline sat in the deadest week of her year, when spending £900 felt insane. She almost delayed it, and delaying it would have cost her the whole autumn.

## The part where people quit

Most small shops that die do it in a quiet stretch, not in a bad month with a clear cause.

It's not usually a financial decision. It's that four weeks of near-silence is genuinely demoralising, and if you don't know it's normal for your category, you conclude the thing doesn't work. Then you stop posting, which means September's baseline is lower, which confirms the conclusion.

Two things help, and neither is inspirational. Know the shape of your year, so silence is expected rather than diagnostic. And give yourself work that produces visible output in a quiet month, because the reason the messaging job is first on the list is that 31 replies feels like something happening, and it is.

## Keep a record longer than your dashboard does

This is where the honest limitation is worth naming, because it bites exactly on this topic.

Sailo's free plan keeps {{free_analytics_days}} days of analytics. {{free_analytics_days}} days is fine for checking whether a post did anything and useless for seasonality, because seasonality is a comparison between this June and last June. Pro at ${{pro_monthly}} a month holds a year, which is the minimum for a year-on-year comparison, and Business holds three.

Even three years runs out eventually. So keep your own tally somewhere permanent: a spreadsheet with one row per month, orders and revenue, updated on the first of the month. It takes two minutes and it's the only record that will still exist in year five. [How to read your own numbers](/en/blog/how-to-read-your-own-numbers) covers what's worth putting in it.

## What to do this week

Open a spreadsheet and write out your last twelve months of orders by month, from whatever record you've got, even if it's WhatsApp threads and memory. Look at the shape. That's your year, and now you know when the quiet part is.

Then take the list of everyone who bought from you in the last twelve months and message the first ten, by name, asking a real question about what you should make next. Not a sale. A question. That's the job for this week, and it's the one that pays for the autumn.
