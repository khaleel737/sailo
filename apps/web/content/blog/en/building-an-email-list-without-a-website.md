Everyone tells you to build an email list. Every guide you open starts with "add a signup form to your website", and you don't have a website, and you're not about to build one just to collect addresses.

You don't need one. Every email tool worth using hands you a hosted signup page on a URL you can paste into a bio, and the free tiers are generous enough that the cost of starting is zero. On Kit's own pricing page in August 2026, the free plan covers 1,000 subscribers with unlimited landing pages, unlimited forms and unlimited broadcasts, at $0 a month. MailerLite's free tier that same week was 250 subscribers and 2,500 emails a month. Buttondown's was the first 100 subscribers.

So the form was never the problem. The problem is the reason somebody types their address into it, and that's a much more interesting question.

## The tool takes fifteen minutes

Get this out of the way so you can stop researching it.

Pick one of the three above, or any equivalent. Make an account. Create a landing page, which is a form on a page they host, with a headline and one field. Copy the URL. Put it somewhere people can tap it.

That's the whole setup. It will take longer to choose between them than to do it, which is why the correct move is to pick the first one that has a free tier covering your next twelve months and never think about it again. You can move a list of addresses later. Every one of these tools exports a CSV.

What you do not need: a domain, a website, a sequence, an automation, a welcome series, or a logo.

## Where addresses actually come from

Ranked by what they yield for a small seller, which is roughly the opposite of the order most guides use.

**1. People who have already bought from you.** By a distance the highest-yield source, and the one nobody does, because it means going back through months of messages and asking one at a time. These people have paid you money. They will give you an email address. Ask them.

**2. Anyone who has ever messaged you asking a question.** Same principle, slightly cooler. "I'm starting a list so people hear about drops first, want me to add you?" works, because you're offering to solve a problem they already have, which is missing your drops.

**3. A physical object.** A card in the parcel. A QR code printed on a tag, a receipt, a sticker on the box. This one keeps working forever with no further effort and almost everybody skips it.

**4. A post that asks directly, before a launch.** "Drop's on the 14th. It'll go to the list an hour before it goes public. Reply with your email or use the link." The hour is what makes it work.

**5. The link in your bio.** Last, not first. It's passive and it collects slowly, and it's still worth having because it costs nothing once it exists.

Notice that four of the five don't involve a signup page at all. They involve asking, in a specific moment, for a specific reason.

## What to offer in exchange

The default advice is a free PDF. It's usually the wrong answer for a seller.

A free PDF attracts people who want free PDFs. If you make hoodies, a person who downloads "10 tips for styling streetwear" is not necessarily a person who will spend R450, and you've spent a weekend finding out.

What works better, in order:

- **Early access.** The list sees the drop an hour, or a day, before everyone else. This is the strongest offer a seller has, it costs nothing, and it self-selects for buyers.
- **Restock alerts.** Especially if you actually sell out. "I'll message you when the size 10s are back" is worth an address to precisely the person you want.
- **A discount for the first order.** Effective, and it trains people to wait for discounts. Use it once, on a specific thing, not permanently.
- **The thing people keep asking you for.** If four people a month ask for your care instructions, your pattern, your recipe, that's the asset. You've already made it, in fragments, in your DMs.

What doesn't work: "sign up for my newsletter". Nobody wants a newsletter. They want a specific thing at a specific moment.

## The offline half

If you sell at markets, fairs, church halls, pop-ups or out of the back of a car, that's where your best addresses are, because those people have met you.

A clipboard works. A clipboard with a pen already attached works better. A phone with a form open works if it's unlocked and handed over. What kills it is asking somebody to type a URL while holding a bag.

A printed QR code that goes straight to your signup page, laminated, standing on the table, is the single cheapest thing on this list. [QR codes that actually get scanned](/en/blog/qr-codes-that-actually-get-scanned) covers where to put them and what has to be written next to them, because a bare QR code with no sentence beside it gets ignored.

## What about WhatsApp and Telegram

For a lot of sellers this is the real question, because their buyers live in WhatsApp and check email once a fortnight.

| | Email list | WhatsApp broadcast | Telegram or WhatsApp channel |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Portable if the app changes | Yes, it's a CSV | Phone numbers, so mostly | No, followers stay there |
| Reach per message | Low, but it's yours | Very high | Medium, and falling as it grows |
| Effort to build | Slow | Slow, and manual | Faster |
| Requirement on the reader | An address | They must save your number | A tap |
| Feels intrusive at | Weekly | Twice a week | Daily is fine |

The catch with WhatsApp broadcast lists is specific and people find out too late: a broadcast only reaches contacts who have your number saved. Somebody who messaged you once and never saved you will not receive it. So the list you think is 400 people is often 150.

Channels solve that and give something up in exchange, which is that the followers aren't yours. You can't export them. If the platform changes the rules in March, the list goes with it.

The sensible answer for most sellers is both, with email as the one you treat as the asset. There's a fuller argument in [owning your customer list](/en/blog/owning-your-customer-list), and it matters more the longer you do this.

## Worked example: Thabo, Johannesburg

Thabo screen-prints hoodies and T-shirts. About 12,000 followers on TikTok, 3,100 on Instagram, no website, and drops every six to eight weeks. Hoodies are R450, tees R220.

For eighteen months he ran drops entirely through social. Post the drop, get 200 comments, sort it out in the DMs over the following nine days. The last one sold 23 hoodies and cost him most of a week in messages.

In February he started a list. Kit's free tier, one landing page, no sequence, no automation.

Where the addresses came from over five months:

| Source | Addresses | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Past buyers, asked individually in DMs | 214 | Four evenings |
| A post asking people to reply before a drop | 143 | One post, twice |
| Link in bio to the signup page | 180 | Set up once |
| QR code printed on the neck tag | 96 | R0, tags were being printed anyway |
| Market stall clipboard | 61 | Two Saturdays |
| **Total** | **694** | |

> Going back through eight months of DMs and asking 214 past buyers, one at a time, produced more addresses than every automated method combined. It took four evenings and it is the only part of this that can't be shortcut.

The July drop went out as one email at 8pm on a Thursday, an hour before he posted it publicly. Forty-one hoodies in about four hours, R18,450. The public post the next morning mopped up nine more.

Compared with 23 over nine days on the previous drop, with the same product and roughly the same following.

What changed wasn't reach. It was that 694 people got told at the same moment, by something that landed in a place they check, rather than being shown to a fraction of an audience over a week by a system optimising for something else.

## Consent, and the part you shouldn't get lazy about

Two rules that keep you out of trouble everywhere, regardless of which country's law applies to you.

**Ask, don't assume.** Somebody buying a hoodie has given you their address so you can send them a hoodie. That is not permission to add them to a marketing list. Ask them, in the order confirmation or in a message, and let them say yes. It costs you a few percent of the list and it saves you the person who reports you as spam, which damages every email you send after that.

**Say what they're getting and how to stop.** "Drop announcements, roughly one a month, unsubscribe any time." Then honour it immediately. One line, and it removes almost every reason someone has to be annoyed with you.

If you're storing names, numbers and addresses, it's worth knowing what you're responsible for. [Privacy and what you do with buyer data](/en/blog/privacy-and-what-you-do-with-buyer-data) covers the practical version, which mostly comes down to collecting less, keeping it somewhere sensible and deleting it when it stops being useful.

## What to actually send

The failure mode isn't collecting addresses. It's collecting 400 of them and then sending nothing for eight months, so that when you finally do, half the recipients have forgotten who you are and the other half mark it as spam.

Send the first one within a week of somebody joining. Short, from you, no design. What you make, when the next thing is coming, and one line about how it's made. Three paragraphs.

Then send something roughly every three to four weeks whether or not you have a drop. It doesn't have to sell. A photo of a bad batch and what went wrong keeps a list warm better than a discount code does.

And when you do have something to sell, the shape of a launch to a list this size is specific and short: [how to launch to a small list](/en/blog/how-to-launch-to-a-small-list) has the seven-day version.

## The gap the shop doesn't fill

One thing worth being clear about before you assume your shop is quietly keeping a record for you.

Sailo's free plan holds {{free_analytics_days}} days of analytics. Pro, at ${{pro_monthly}} a month, holds a year and adds CSV export. Business holds three years. If your plan for owning your audience is "the shop will remember who came", the free plan remembers for a month, and getting the data out in a file is a paid feature.

That's not an argument for upgrading. It's an argument for collecting addresses yourself, starting now, into a tool that's free at your size and that you can take with you. The addresses are the thing that survives you changing platform, changing product, or a feed deciding in November that it prefers video.

## What to do this week

Make the account. Make one landing page with one field and a headline that promises early access, not a newsletter. Fifteen minutes.

Then open your DMs, scroll back as far as you have patience for, and message every single person who has ever bought something from you. One at a time, not a broadcast. Ask if they want to be told first next time.

Do fifty. Count how many say yes. That number is the honest size of your audience, and it's usually both smaller and far more valuable than the one on your profile.
