"There's a tiny upside-down photograph of me in every single one of my rings."

Jewellery is three problems in one object, and they have three separate fixes. Polished metal is a mirror, so you control what it reflects rather than trying to remove the reflection. Nobody can judge size from a photo of a small object alone, so every piece gets shot on a body. And most phones can't focus closer than about 8cm, so you back off and crop instead of pushing the lens in until everything goes soft.

Get those three right and a $65 silver ring photographs properly on a kitchen table next to a window. Get them wrong and no amount of editing rescues it.

## Reflection: photograph what you want the metal to show

A polished band is a 360-degree mirror. It doesn't reflect "light", it reflects your room, your ceiling, your phone, and you.

So the job isn't reducing reflection. It's deciding what gets reflected.

**Wear black.** A dark t-shirt, and if you're serious about it, a dark towel over the back of the chair behind you. This is the cheapest fix in this article, it takes ten seconds, and it removes the pale blob that appears in every curved surface you photograph. It also looks completely ridiculous and it works.

**Diffuse the window.** Tape a sheet of baking paper, a white bedsheet or a shower curtain over the glass. Direct light on polished metal produces a small, brutally bright highlight with a hard edge. Diffused light produces a broad soft one that follows the shape of the piece, and shape is what you're selling.

**Build a tent for the difficult pieces.** A cardboard box with the top and one side cut out, lined with white paper, with a small hole for the lens. Put the piece inside, light it through the paper from the window side, and the metal now reflects a smooth white surround instead of your kitchen. Cost: a box. Time: eight minutes with a knife.

**Add a black card for definition.** Fully surrounded by white, silver goes flat and reads as grey plastic. Stand a piece of black card just outside the frame on one side and the metal picks up a dark edge that defines the curve. This is the trick that makes polished silver look like metal rather than putty, and almost nobody does it.

**Angle out the worst of it.** Move a few degrees until the visible reflection is the bright window rather than the dark doorway. Small movements make big differences, which is why you shoot five frames of a ring and not one.

## Scale: the hand shot outsells the flat lay

Ask any jeweller which question fills their messages and it isn't price. It's some version of "how big is it".

Nobody has ever asked what a ring is. They ask how wide the band is and whether it'll look chunky, and a photo of it on a finger answers both in a way that "2.2mm" never will. A flat lay of six rings on marble is a lovely image that leaves every one of those questions open.

So: every piece gets one shot on a body.

| Piece | Where it goes |
| --- | --- |
| Ring | On a finger, hand relaxed and slightly turned, shot from the side of the hand |
| Chain or pendant | On a neck, from just off-centre, shoulders in frame |
| Earrings | On an ear, hair tucked back, shot from slightly behind the cheekbone |
| Bracelet or bangle | On a wrist, arm resting on the surface so it doesn't shake |
| Anklet | On an ankle, foot flat on the floor, from above and slightly in front |
| Tiny things, or nothing to model on | Beside a coin, a matchstick or a grain of rice, held between two fingers |

Then put the millimetres in the description as well. Band width, drop length, chain length, stone diameter. The photograph does the feeling and the numbers do the certainty, and you need both, because a buyer who feels sure still checks.

The flat lay isn't useless. It's the range shot, the one that shows five variations together, and it belongs at position three or four in the set. It should not be the main image, which is the mistake that costs the most sales in this entire category.

## Focus, and the 8cm problem

Most phone main cameras can't focus closer than about 8 to 10cm. Get closer than that and the image goes soft, which people interpret as the camera being bad rather than the distance being wrong.

What to do instead:

**Back off and crop.** Shoot from 20 to 25cm and crop in afterwards. A crop from a sharp 12-megapixel photo beats a full-frame blurry one every time, and there's more resolution in a phone photo than a shop page can use.

**Be careful with macro mode.** On most phones it switches to the ultrawide lens, which is a lower-quality sensor with more distortion. It's genuinely useful for texture and hallmark shots. It's usually not the right choice for the main image.

**Tap to focus, then lock it.** Press and hold on the piece until focus and exposure lock. Otherwise the phone will refocus onto the background at the exact moment you press the shutter.

**Prop the phone.** A stack of books, a mug, anything. Then use the three-second timer or the volume button, because pressing the screen shakes the phone, and at close range that shake is the difference between sharp and nearly sharp.

**Focus on the front, not the middle.** Depth of field at 20cm is a few millimetres. On a ring, focus on the front of the band or the front facet of the stone. A ring focused on its centre is a ring that's blurry everywhere a buyer looks.

**Shoot three, at slightly different distances.** You will not be able to tell which one is sharpest on a phone screen. You will be able to tell on a laptop, and by then the piece is back in the drawer.

## Stones die from dust and flat light

Two things make a gemstone look like plastic, and both are fixable in the room.

**Dust.** At close range a speck is enormous, and your eye genuinely cannot see it while you're shooting. A rubber blower costs about the same as a coffee and removes 90% of it. A microfibre cloth for fingerprints, cotton gloves or clean hands, and tweezers for positioning, and you'll spend a tenth of the time you'd otherwise spend removing spots in editing afterwards.

**Light that's too soft.** This is the one that surprises people, because everything else in product photography says diffuse the light. A faceted stone needs a small bright source to throw sparkle. If your light is entirely even, the facets have nothing to catch and the stone reads as grey glass.

The fix: diffuse most of the window, then leave a gap. Tape baking paper over three quarters of the glass and let a strip of direct light through. The soft light does the metal, the hard strip does the stone. Then rotate the piece a few degrees at a time and watch the sparkle move in the viewfinder until it's where you want it.

Opaque stones, pearls, matte finishes and oxidised silver want the opposite. Fully diffused, no gap, and a dark card to give them an edge.

## Silver, gold and brass are three different colour problems

Metals go wrong in different directions, and each one has a different cause.

**Silver** photographs blue when it's lit by open sky in shade. Get it into window light with the ceiling lights off, and shoot a sheet of white printer paper in the first frame of the session so you have something true to set white balance against afterwards.

**Gold** picks up a green cast under fluorescent tubes and goes brassy on a warm wooden surface. Shoot it on a neutral grey or a cool stone, never on orange wood.

**Brass and bronze** are already warm, so a warm background pushes them into orange and buyers receive something that looks more brown than the photo. Cool the background down or go neutral.

The general rule that saves you: one light source, one colour temperature, one white reference in the frame at the start. Then leave the saturation slider alone. Every nudge upwards is a return you're paying postage on later, and metal colour is one of the top three things people send jewellery back over.

## Backgrounds for something small and bright

Small, shiny objects need a background that doesn't compete and doesn't reflect.

What works: unpolished stone, slate, a matte painted board, fine linen, concrete, a plain mid-grey card. Slight texture is good, because texture at jewellery scale reads as expensive.

What doesn't: mirrored acrylic risers, which double every reflection problem you already have. Glass. Velvet, which collects lint you won't see until you crop in. White marble tiles, which are in every jewellery photo on the internet and now read as a template. And glossy anything, because gloss bounces the window straight back into the lens.

Tone matters more than colour, and the rule is contrast: oxidised or dark pieces on pale surfaces, bright silver and gold on mid or dark ones. The full version of that, including why white is usually the wrong choice, is in [what a good product photo background looks like](/en/blog/what-a-good-product-photo-background-looks-like).

## The six shots for one piece

Shoot them in this order, every time, and sorting takes forty seconds.

| Shot | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| On the body | The hero. Size, proportion, how it sits |
| Straight on, plain background | The catalogue image. Whole piece, sharp, centred |
| Angled three-quarter | Shows depth and the side profile |
| Close on the detail | Setting, clasp, texture, hallmark. This is where macro mode earns its place |
| Next to something for scale | A coin or a fingertip, for the buyers who want a number |
| The group or range | All finishes or all sizes together. Where second orders come from |

Six shots, roughly four minutes per piece once the setup exists. The setup is the slow part, so build it once and photograph everything you own in one afternoon. That principle, and the six-shot habit it comes from, is the whole of [how to photograph what you sell](/en/blog/how-to-photograph-what-you-sell).

## Prep takes longer than the shoot

For jewellery this is unusually true. Budget twice as long for preparation as for photography.

- Clean every piece with a microfibre cloth immediately before it goes in frame, and handle it with the cloth afterwards.
- Use museum wax or a scrap of poster putty to stand a ring upright, hidden under the band.
- Lay chains with tension in them rather than dropping them in a pile. A chain in a heap looks tangled even when it isn't, and "does it tangle" is a real objection.
- Position earrings as a mirrored pair, not side by side facing the same way.
- Check the frame edges before every shot. A stray hair or a crumb of putty at the edge is a five-minute editing job you can avoid in two seconds.

## Hands, and the honest part about them

The hand shot is the most valuable image in the set and it's the one sellers avoid, usually because they don't like their own hands.

If it's yours: relax the fingers, bend them slightly, support the hand on something so it doesn't tremble, and shoot from the side rather than the back of the hand. Straight-on knuckles are unflattering on everyone. Skin looks better in the same soft window light as the metal, and short clean nails photograph better than a fresh manicure that pulls attention off the ring.

If it isn't yours, ask a friend, and ask a few different friends over time. If you sell into a mixed market, seeing the piece on more than one skin tone does real work, because a buyer trying to picture a warm gold band against their own hand is doing guesswork otherwise.

If you genuinely can't face it, the fallback is a jewellery bust or a hand form, plus a strict discipline about millimetres in the description. It's a worse photograph and it's better than nothing.

## Six seconds of video does what a still can't

Jewellery moves. Metal changes as it turns, stones flash, a chain has weight, and a still image can't show any of that.

A six-second clip of a ring rotating slowly on a fingertip, shot in the same light, is often the single best-performing thing a jeweller posts. No talking, no music with lyrics, price on screen. The format and the settings are in [how to shoot a product video](/en/blog/how-to-shoot-a-product-video).

## Worked example: silver stacking rings, Providence, $65

Nadia makes silver stacking rings in Providence, Rhode Island, and sells them at $65, around 45 a month, mostly through Instagram.

Her photos were flat lays on a white marble tile: six rings arranged in a fan, shot from directly above, on a bright day. They looked good on the grid and they generated a specific message, over and over: "is it thin or chunky?"

She was also shooting from about 5cm, wondering why nothing was ever quite sharp, and blaming her phone.

What changed, in one Sunday:

- Black t-shirt, dark towel over the chair behind her.
- Baking paper taped over three quarters of the window, a strip of direct light left open for the stones.
- A piece of grey slate from a garden centre instead of the marble tile.
- Phone propped on a stack of books at 22cm, three-second timer, focus locked on the front of the band, then cropped in afterwards.
- One shot of every ring on her own hand, shot from the side, hand resting on the table.

The hand shot became the main image on all eleven listings.

Two things she measured over the following two months. The "thin or chunky" messages went from most days to almost never. And her resize requests, which had been running at about one in nine rings sold, dropped to roughly one in twenty, because people could see the proportion before they bought rather than after.

At $65 a ring, one avoided resize is a shipping label each way plus an hour at the bench. That's the whole return on a sheet of baking paper.

## The same problems in a different market

Sneha sells oxidised silver anklets in Hyderabad at ₹1,850, with most payments arriving by UPI to a plain UPI ID, which needs no gateway and no setup.

Her problem was the mirror image of Nadia's. Oxidised silver is dark, and she'd read that dark backgrounds look premium, so her anklets were photographed on black velvet. They vanished. The detail in the oxidised work, which is the entire reason her pieces cost more than machine-made ones, was invisible.

She swapped to pale grey linen, ironed, and put a strip of white card on the shadow side to lift the darker areas. Same phone, same window, same anklets. The change took an afternoon and it's the difference between a photo of a dark shape and a photo of a piece of work.

If your metal is dark, your background is light. If your metal is bright, your background is mid or dark. There's no third option worth trying first.

## What to do next

Put on a black t-shirt, tape baking paper over most of your nearest window, and photograph one ring on your own hand from 20cm with the phone propped against something. That's the whole method and it takes fifteen minutes.

Look at the result on a laptop rather than a phone, because a phone screen hides softness and hides dust, and both will be visible to your buyer.

Then replace the main image on your best-selling piece with the hand shot and leave everything else alone for two weeks, so you can see what one change did.
