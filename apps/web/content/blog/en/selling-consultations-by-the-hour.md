Somebody has asked what you charge and you're staring at the message, doing sums that keep coming out differently. Meanwhile you've already had four "quick calls" this month that were forty minutes each and earned you nothing.

Three decisions turn that into a business. The rate, which matters less than you think. What's inside the hour, which matters far more. And whether the first call is free, where the honest answer for most people is no.

Start at ₹2,500 for 45 minutes if you have real experience and no track record of charging for it. That number is not a recommendation for your market forever. It's a place to begin so you can stop calculating and start booking, because the rate you can defend is discovered by charging it, not by working it out on paper.

## Your hour is not what they're buying

They're buying the end of a specific confusion. That's it.

Nobody wants an hour of your time. They want to stop being stuck on whether their ad account is broken or their product is wrong. The hour is the delivery mechanism, the same way a bottle is the delivery mechanism for hair oil, and pricing the bottle instead of the oil is how consultants end up cheap.

This changes your listing. "60-minute consultation" is a spec. "Work out why your ads stopped working, in 45 minutes, with a written plan after" is a product. The second one can be priced at four times the first and nobody blinks, because it names an outcome rather than a duration.

It also changes who books you. A page that sells hours attracts people who want to pick your brain. A page that sells an answer to a named problem attracts people who have that problem, and those people arrive with a question ready and leave satisfied. Same hour, entirely different afternoon.

## Getting to your first number

Three routes. Use whichever one you can actually complete today.

**The salary route.** Take the annual salary you'd want for this work full time. Divide by 1,000, not 2,000. The halving accounts for the fact that only about half of a freelance week is billable, and the other half is admin, chasing people and finding the next client. ₹18 lakh a year becomes ₹1,800 an hour of billable time as a floor, not a target.

**The comparison route.** What does an agency charge for the same advice, and what would this person spend if they got it wrong instead? If a bad month of ad spend costs them ₹80,000, an hour that prevents it is not expensive at ₹4,000. Say that on the page, with the number.

**The flinch route.** Say a number out loud. If you don't flinch, it's too low. Add 40% and say it again. Most people's honest first rate is roughly double what they were about to type.

Then round it to something that looks decided. ₹2,500, not ₹2,350. A price that looks calculated invites negotiation.

## Why ₹1,500 is worse than ₹4,000

Low rates do not bring easier clients. They bring the opposite, reliably, and every consultant discovers this in the same order.

At ₹1,500 an hour you attract people for whom ₹1,500 is a real decision. They arrive anxious, they want maximum value extracted from the slot, they run over, they message afterwards with follow-ups they consider included, and a few of them will ask for a discount anyway. You need eight of them a week to make anything.

At ₹4,000 you attract people spending someone's budget, or their own money on a problem they've already decided is worth solving. They turn up on time with notes. They ask two good questions instead of twenty vague ones. They book again.

The dangerous zone is the middle, where the price is high enough to create expectation and low enough that you resent the work. If you catch yourself hoping a client cancels, your rate is wrong and no amount of efficiency will fix it.

Raising it is a smaller event than it feels. New clients see the new number and have no idea it changed. Existing clients get one message: "From 1 October my rate is ₹4,000. Your current work continues at the old rate until it's done." Nobody has ever needed a longer version of that message.

## Write the scope where the buyer reads it

The scope is what stops an hour becoming a week. Put it in the service description, not in a document nobody opens.

Four lines do most of the work:

- **What we'll cover.** "One account, one problem. Ads or landing page, not both."
- **What you send me beforehand, and when.** "Access plus your last 90 days of numbers, at least 24 hours before."
- **What you get after.** "A written summary with three actions, within two working days."
- **What isn't included.** "I don't make the changes. I don't write the copy. Implementation is quoted separately."

That last line is the one people skip and it's the one that saves your month. Without it, "can you just fix that while we're on the call" happens on the call, in front of you, and saying no in the moment is much harder than having said it in writing a week earlier.

## The free discovery call is a tax you pay yourself

The advice to offer a free 15-minute discovery call is everywhere and it's wrong for most people selling hours online.

Here's what happens. The 15 minutes becomes 35, because you're trying to be helpful and they're getting value. You do the diagnosis for free, because the diagnosis is what a good consultant does in the first ten minutes and you can't switch it off. They thank you sincerely, say they'll think about it, and never come back, because they already got the thing they were confused about.

You've now delivered your actual product, for free, to someone who was never going to pay. Do that four times a month and you've worked a full unpaid day.

There's a second cost that's harder to see. A free call teaches you that your time is a marketing expense. That belief leaks into how you price, how you handle overruns, and how quickly you say yes to "one more quick question".

The replacement is a paid short call. ₹1,000 for 20 minutes, credited in full against a full session if they book one within a fortnight. It filters out everyone who wanted free advice and loses you almost nobody who was going to buy. Some of your best clients will start there.

## When a free call genuinely earns its place

Two cases, and you should know which one you're in.

**When the engagement is large.** If what you're selling is a three-month retainer at ₹1,50,000, a 20-minute unpaid call is a rounding error and both sides need to check the fit. Sell the retainer, not the hour, and give the call away without thinking about it.

**When you have no proof yet.** Your first five clients are buying a stranger. A free call is a legitimate way to get the first ones, as long as you set an end date on the offer and hold it. "Free calls until the end of September" is a campaign. "Book a free call" living permanently on your page is a policy, and it's the wrong one.

## Book 45, block 90

The gap between the session length and the slot length is where consultants quietly lose a day a week.

A 45-minute call needs 15 minutes before it to read their material and remember who they are, and 20 minutes after it to write the summary while it's fresh. That's 80 minutes for a 45-minute product. If you sell three in a day and schedule them an hour apart, you'll write no summaries, and the summary is half of what they're paying for.

So set the duration on the listing to the honest slot, and stack your calls rather than spreading them. Three calls back to back with proper gaps is a hard morning and a free afternoon. Three calls spread across a day is a day gone with nothing else in it.

## What the date picker does and doesn't do

This is the honest limitation, and for consulting it's the one that will embarrass you if nobody tells you.

Sailo's booking picker asks the buyer for a preferred date and time and enforces your notice period, so nobody can grab a slot sooner than you've allowed. It does not read your calendar. It doesn't know you're at a wedding on Saturday or already booked at 4pm on Tuesday, because it has no connection to whatever calendar app you actually use.

So two people can ask for the same Tuesday 4pm, and the person who notices is you. Which means the workflow has to be: request comes in, you check your own calendar, you confirm or you offer an alternative. Treat every booking as a request until you've replied to it, and say so on the page: "I'll confirm your slot within a few hours."

It's more manual than a paid scheduling tool. It's also free, it lives on the same page as everything else you sell, and at four to ten calls a week the checking takes about ninety seconds a day.

## Get paid before, not after

Invoicing after a consulting call is how you end up chasing ₹2,500 for six weeks.

Take the money at booking. On a manual rail that means the buyer sends a UPI payment to the ID you've put in your payment instructions, or a bank transfer, and you confirm it before you confirm the slot. Sailo cannot tell you the money arrived. Only your bank can, so build one check into your morning and one into your evening.

## Worked example: Meera in Pune

Meera spent six years running paid social at an agency and now advises D2C brands on their ad accounts. She started at ₹1,500 for an hour because it felt like what a person could reasonably ask.

Month one: eleven calls, ₹16,500, and about nine hours of unpaid follow-up messages. She was exhausted and slightly bitter.

What she changed over the next quarter:

1. Raised to ₹2,500 for 45 minutes and renamed the listing from "Consultation call" to "Ad account teardown, 45 minutes plus written actions".
2. Added a required pre-call form: account access, last 90 days of spend, and the one question they most want answered.
3. Replaced her free discovery call with a ₹1,000 20-minute triage, credited against a full session.
4. Wrote "I don't make changes to your account" into the description.
5. Capped herself at six calls a week, Tuesday and Thursday mornings only.

Month four: six calls a week at ₹2,500, so around ₹60,000 a month, and follow-up down to under two hours because the written summary answered what the messages used to. Two of those clients turned into monthly retainers at ₹35,000.

Her hardest lesson had nothing to do with pricing. She learned to send the written summary within two working days, always, even when the call went badly, because the summary is what gets forwarded to the person who controls the budget. Three of her retainers came from someone reading a summary she'd nearly not bothered to write.

She takes payment by UPI, which is how everyone pays her already and always has. Sailo handles the page, the duration and the date request; the money goes where it always went and Sailo takes nothing from it, because that's a manual rail.

## Turn the hour into something that isn't an hour

The ceiling on selling hours is arithmetic and it arrives faster than you expect. Six calls a week at ₹2,500 is roughly ₹60,000 a month, and the only ways up are a higher rate or more hours, and one of those has a hard limit.

Three moves out, smallest first. Sell a package rather than a call: four sessions over six weeks at ₹9,000 stops you re-selling to the same person every fortnight. Sell an audit instead of a call, where they send material and you send back a document, because that's work you can do at 6am and it isn't a slot. Sell the thing you say every single time as a file, because by call thirty you're repeating yourself and repetition is a product.

## Do these three things today

Write the four scope lines and paste them into your service description. Set your duration to the honest slot including your prep and your write-up, not the length of the call. Take your free discovery call off the page and replace it with a ₹1,000 triage credited against a full session.

Then send your next enquiry a booking link instead of a paragraph about your availability. If a client needs to move a session after that, [how to write a cancellation policy for bookings](/en/blog/rescheduling-and-cancellations) has the exact wording to put on the page.
