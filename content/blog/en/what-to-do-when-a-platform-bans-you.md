You opened the app and it said your account has been disabled. No warning you can find, no reason beyond a sentence about community guidelines, and 6,000 people who used to be able to reach you now cannot.

Then you noticed the orders you had not shipped were in a DM thread you can no longer open.

Do four things, in this order. **Read the notice properly**, because it tells you which kind of removal this is and gives you a date. **Appeal once, carefully, inside that window**, which on Meta has typically been around 30 days before deletion starts and longer in the EU. **Do not open a replacement account** while the first is under review. And **reach the customers you can reach by other means today**, because the appeal may take a fortnight and your business does not pause for it. A Sailo shop is $0 on the free plan for 20 products and lives at `sailo.store/yourname`, which is worth setting up on a quiet Tuesday rather than on the morning this happens.

The rest of this is what nobody writes down: what you actually lose, how the appeal is really read, and what to do when the answer is no.

## The first hour

Panic makes people do the two things that make it permanent. Slow down.

**Do this:**

- Screenshot the notice. All of it, including any reference number and any date.
- Check your email, including spam, for a message from the platform. It sometimes carries more detail than the app does.
- Check whether you can still get into the business tools on desktop. Meta accounts in particular sometimes lose the app while Business Suite still opens, and that is where your export lives.
- Post from any other account you already run, telling people where to find you.
- Message every buyer with an unshipped order through any channel you have: phone, email, a different app.

**Do not do this:**

- Do not make a new account on the same platform. This is the single most common self-inflicted escalation. Platforms detect the device and the phone number, and a new account made during a review is often read as evasion, which turns a suspension into something much harder to undo.
- Do not appeal eleven times. Repeated identical appeals get treated as spam and the queue does not reward volume.
- Do not pay anyone offering to recover it. The people advertising account recovery on the platform that just banned you are not, on the whole, doing account recovery.

## The notice tells you which kind of problem you have

There are broadly three, and they need different responses.

| What the notice says | What it usually is | What actually helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A specific policy, one post named | Automated enforcement on one item | Appeal, referencing the post, calmly |
| Community guidelines, nothing named | A pattern, or a wave of reports | Appeal, plus evidence you are a real business |
| Impersonation, or "we could not verify" | Identity or a report from someone else | ID upload, business documents |

If a specific post is named, go and look at it. Very often it is a reel with a song in it, a product claim you did not think twice about, or a photo that looked fine to you and looked like something else to a classifier.

If nothing is named, the honest answer is that you may never find out why. That is genuinely how it works, it is unsatisfying, and pretending otherwise wastes your week.

## How to write an appeal that gets read

Assume the first read is by a system, and the second, if you get one, is by a person with about ninety seconds.

Short. Specific. No emotion. No paragraph about how much this means to you, however true it is.

What to include:

1. **What the account is.** "This is the account for a registered wig business in Lagos. We have been trading since 2023."
2. **Evidence you are what you say.** A website or shop link, a business registration number if you have one, an email on your own domain, a linked Facebook page, an advertising history.
3. **The specific thing, if one was named.** "The post flagged was a product photo of a lace frontal wig. It contains no nudity. It was reported on 4 August."
4. **What you have done about it.** If a rule was genuinely broken, say so and say it is removed. Appeals that admit a mistake do better than appeals that argue.
5. **A request in one line.** "Please review the account for reinstatement."

Then stop. Do not attach nine screenshots.

If you have ever run ads, or you subscribe to a paid verification tier that includes support, use that route. Whether it produces a human varies, but it is a different queue and a different queue is worth trying.

Expect days rather than hours. First responses on the standard route commonly land somewhere in the first week; escalations take longer, and some appeals are simply never answered, which is a possibility you should plan around rather than refresh at.

## What you actually lose, itemised

This is the part sellers underestimate until it happens, so here it is written out.

- **The followers.** Not the people, the *reach* to them. They still exist. You just have no way to speak to them.
- **The DM history.** Which for most social sellers is the order book. Addresses, sizes, what someone ordered last time, the conversation where you agreed a custom price.
- **The content.** Years of photos and videos, and in a lot of cases the only copies.
- **The proof.** Reviews, tagged photos, comments from real buyers. Trust that took three years to build and is not portable.
- **The link.** Every business card, every parcel insert, every printed sticker pointing at a handle that no longer resolves.
- **Search.** People typing your name find nothing, or worse, find someone else.

Notice how much of that is *record-keeping* rather than marketing. The followers are the loss everyone talks about. The addresses are the loss that stops you trading this week.

> Your follower count is the thing you would grieve. Your order history is the thing you actually needed. They are stored in the same place and that is the mistake.

## Export before you need to, because the export lives inside the account

Every major platform has a "download your information" tool. Every one of them sits behind the login you have just lost.

So the export is not a recovery step. It is a maintenance task, and it belongs on a calendar.

Once a quarter, twenty minutes:

1. Request the data export from each platform you sell on. Save the file somewhere that is not the platform.
2. Copy the phone numbers and email addresses of everyone who has actually bought from you into one plain spreadsheet. Not the followers. The buyers.
3. Save your best twenty photos and videos at full resolution, off the app.
4. Write down your handles, your linked email addresses and your recovery phone numbers somewhere you can reach without the phone.

Point two is the whole ballgame and it is worth doing properly. A list of people who have paid you money is the only asset in this business that no platform can switch off. [Owning your customer list](/en/blog/owning-your-customer-list) makes the full case, and [building an email list without a website](/en/blog/building-an-email-list-without-a-website) covers how to collect one without adding a chore to every order.

## The three ways small sellers usually get hit

Not the only ways, but the common ones, and two of them are avoidable.

**Music.** A reel with a commercial track behind it, used by a business account. Rights differ by account type and by country, and the enforcement is automatic. Sellers get muted, then limited, then in bad cases removed. Use the platform's own licensed audio from within the app, or use no music, and do not export a video with a song in it from one app into another.

**Restricted categories.** Supplements, anything making a health or weight claim, cosmetics with medical language, vapes, alcohol, live animals, some second-hand goods, some medical devices. "It's just skincare" is not a defence when the caption says "clears eczema in five days". Read the commerce policies for your category once, properly. It is an hour and it is cheaper than an appeal.

**Reports.** A competitor, an angry buyer, or a coordinated group can report an account repeatedly, and volume matters more than merit in automated systems. There is not much you can do to prevent this beyond being unremarkable in the ways that trigger reviews: no engagement bots, no follow-unfollow tools, no bought followers, no third-party apps you gave your password to.

That last one deserves its own sentence. Giving your login to a growth service is how a large number of "hacked, then banned" stories start, and the ban happens because the service posted something you never saw.

## Chidinma in Lagos, ₦45,000 a unit

Chidinma sells wigs in Lagos. A bob unit at ₦45,000, longer pieces from ₦85,000, and installation at ₦15,000 if you come to her. Instagram was the whole business: 22,000 followers, everything in the DMs, payment by bank transfer, delivery by dispatch rider.

On a Wednesday in March her account was disabled. No post named. She still does not know why, though she suspects a competitor who had been leaving comments for a month, because two other sellers in the same trade went down the same week.

She had fourteen unshipped orders sitting in a DM thread she could no longer open.

What she did, over the next month:

1. Appealed the same day, in four sentences, with her CAC registration number and her shop link. Then left it alone.
2. Did not make a second account, though everyone told her to.
3. Posted from her personal Facebook and her WhatsApp status, both of which she had ignored for two years, saying what had happened and asking anyone with an outstanding order to message her.
4. Sent a WhatsApp broadcast to 380 numbers she had been collecting for six months, one per buyer, because she had got into the habit of saving the number of anyone who actually paid.

Eleven of the fourteen buyers came forward within two days. She got the account back after nineteen days with no explanation.

Her own summary of it is the part worth stealing: the appeal did not save the business, the 380 numbers did. She had built that list slowly and for no particular reason, and it was the only thing she owned.

She now runs the same shop link on every parcel insert, on her Facebook, on her WhatsApp, and in her Instagram bio, and she has stopped treating any single one of them as the address.

## Rented and owned, honestly labelled

| Thing | Who controls it |
| --- | --- |
| Followers | The platform |
| Your handle | The platform |
| DM history | The platform |
| Your content, if it only exists there | The platform |
| Phone numbers and emails of buyers | You |
| A domain you registered | You |
| A shop page on someone's service | Shared. Yours to leave, theirs to run |

That last row is the honest one and it includes Sailo. `sailo.store/yourname` is a link you control the contents of, on a domain Sailo owns. It cannot be taken away by Instagram, which is the point. It is not immune to Sailo, which is also true, and any tool telling you otherwise is selling you something. What that risk looks like in practice, and how to reduce it for any tool you depend on, is in [what happens if a tool shuts down](/en/blog/what-happens-if-a-tool-shuts-down).

The mitigation is the same one either way. Keep the buyer list in a file you can open without logging into anything. Then no single failure takes more than a fortnight off you.

## Do this this week, even though nothing has happened

Because the only useful version of this article is the one you read before.

1. Request a data export from every platform you sell on. Put the files somewhere that is not that platform.
2. Start a spreadsheet of buyers. Name, phone or email, what they bought, when. Add to it after every order, not in a batch.
3. Set up a shop link with your name on it and start putting it on parcels, in bios, and in your voice.
4. Find and remove any third-party app that has your password.
5. Read your platform's commerce policy for your actual category, once.
