Most small sales die on one reply, and it's this one. "Hi! Thanks so much for reaching out, how can I help you today?" The buyer asked a question. You answered with a greeting and handed the work straight back to them.

A good first message does four things and takes under fifty words. It names the specific thing they're asking about. It gives the price and the delivery cost in the same breath. It adds one detail that answers the question they haven't asked yet. And it ends with a question they can answer in three words or fewer.

Here's one, at 34 words:

> The black one is AED 320, delivery's free anywhere in Dubai and it's usually next day. That's the heavier crepe, not the light one. I've got two left in medium, want one held?

Everything below is how to build that for your product, and the three openings that reliably cost you the order.

## The four parts, and why each one is there

**The specific thing.** "The black one", not "our products". If they mentioned a photo, name what's in the photo. This is the part that proves you read the message, and it's the difference between a reply and a template.

**The price, plus delivery.** Both, always, in the first message. More on this below because it's the part people argue about.

**One thing they didn't ask.** The size, the material, the fact that it runs small, how long it lasts, whether it's in stock. Pick the thing that generates your most common second question and answer it before it's asked. You know what that question is, because you've answered it two hundred times.

**A closing question with a tiny answer.** "Want one held?" "Which size?" "Medium or large?" A buyer facing a three-word decision replies. A buyer facing an open field of possibilities closes the app.

That last part carries more weight than the other three put together. Never end a first message with a full stop after a price. "AED 320." is a fact to file. "AED 320, want me to hold one?" is a decision to make, and a decision gets an answer.

## The three openings that kill it

**"Hi! Thanks for reaching out."** Costs a message and delivers nothing. If someone asks a question at 9am and gets a greeting at 11am, they've now waited two hours for zero information.

**"DM sent!" or "Check your DMs."** A comment reply that requires a second app switch. You'll lose a meaningful share of people at that hop, so put the answer in the comment or send the DM and say what's in it.

**"Hello dear, what would you like to order?"** Sounds friendly, reads as a bot, and it's close to the exact phrasing fake accounts use. In markets where buyers are wary of being scammed, warmth that sounds automated makes you less trustworthy rather than more.

## The one-word enquiry

A large share of your incoming messages will be a single word. "Available?" "Price?" "How much" with no question mark and no clue which item.

Do not reply "which one?" That's a wasted round trip and it's where a third of them stop.

Reply with your best guess plus an escape hatch:

> Hi, if it's the black crepe from yesterday's post, AED 320, free delivery in Dubai. If you meant a different one send me the picture and I'll price it.

You've given a real answer to a non-question. Nine times in ten your guess is right, because they're asking about the thing you posted most recently, and the tenth time they just send a photo.

The other one-word message you'll get a lot in Dubai, Lagos, Mumbai and Manila is "last price?". Answer it the same way every time, in one line, without irritation: "AED 320 is the price, and delivery's free in Dubai, that's the best I can do on one." Then ask your closing question anyway. A buyer who tries a haggle and gets a calm firm number usually buys, because the haggle was a habit rather than a condition.

## When they message you first

Most first messages aren't cold. They're replies. Somebody saw a post and asked something.

Rule one: answer the question they actually asked, in the first sentence, before anything else. If they asked "how much?", the first word of your reply should be a number.

Rule two: then add the thing they didn't ask.

Compare:

> Hi! Thank you for your interest. Which item were you asking about? We have several styles available.

Against:

> AED 320 for the black crepe one. Free delivery in Dubai, next day usually. It's the heavier fabric, sits better in the heat. Want me to put one aside?

Same information available to both sellers. One of them has a customer and the other has a conversation.

Rule three: if you genuinely don't know which item they mean, ask with a picture rather than a sentence. Send two photos and say "this one or this one?" It's faster than making them describe it.

## Put the price in

The argument against is that quoting a price up front loses the chance to sell them on value first. In a DM, with a stranger, that's wrong nearly every time.

Somebody who asks the price and doesn't get it does one of three things. They ask again, more tersely. They assume it's expensive and leave. Or they wait, cool off, and never come back. None of those is a sale you saved.

Withholding also reads as a negotiation opening in markets where haggling is normal, which means you've started a negotiation you didn't want. Say the number, say it once, say it the same way every time.

Say the delivery cost in the same message. Delivery is the single most common reason a warm conversation goes cold two messages later. A buyer who has agreed to AED 320 and then discovers AED 40 of delivery feels ambushed, even at a fair price, because the number moved after they'd committed.

## Keep it under fifty words

Not a style preference. A long first message reads as a pitch and gets skimmed, and the closing question at the bottom is the part that gets skipped.

Fifty words fits on a phone screen without scrolling, which is the actual constraint. If you need more than that, you've either got a complicated product or you're explaining something that should be on the product page.

One message, not five. Sending six short bubbles in a row is worse than one paragraph, because each notification costs the buyer something and the sixth one is where they mute you.

## Voice notes, emoji, and how formal to be

**Voice notes**: not for a first message. They require the recipient to be somewhere they can listen, and a lot of people are on a bus. They're excellent later, once the order's confirmed and you're explaining something fiddly.

**Emoji**: one, maybe, if that's genuinely how you write. Three or more reads as a template. Zero is completely fine and is the safer default with a business buyer.

**Formality**: match the buyer. If they wrote "hi how much is the black one", write like a person. If they wrote a full paragraph with capital letters, write back in sentences. Copying the register of the incoming message is the closest thing to a universal rule here, and it works across every market.

One thing that isn't universal: names. In much of the Gulf, India and Nigeria, using a title and a name reads as respect. In the US and UK it can read as a call centre. If in doubt, use no name in the first message and pick it up from how they sign off.

## Layla's abayas, and the message that moved

Layla sells abayas from an Instagram account in Dubai. AED 320 for the heavy crepe, AED 260 for the light, free delivery inside Dubai, AED 45 to the other emirates. She takes about 40 orders a month and almost all of them close on WhatsApp.

In the Gulf the storefront is Instagram and the till is WhatsApp, so the message that matters most is the one that moves someone across. That move costs you a chunk of people every time, unless the item and the price travel with them.

What she used to do: reply to an Instagram comment with "DM for price", then in the DM say "hi dear, which one?", then move to WhatsApp with "send me a message here" and a number. Three hops, and by hop three a lot of people had gone.

What she does now, over one week in April:

| Stage | People | Continued |
|---|---|---|
| Asked price under a post | 46 | |
| Got a public reply with the price and a link | 46 | 29 opened it |
| Started a WhatsApp order | 29 | 21 |
| Confirmed and paid | 21 | 17 |

Seventeen orders from 46 questions, against six the week before she changed it.

The change was small. She now answers the price in the comment, publicly, with the delivery cost, because every other person reading that post had the same question. And when the WhatsApp conversation opens, the item and the size are already in the message rather than being re-established from scratch.

The bit she got wrong first, and it's worth knowing: for two weeks she sent a long welcome message when someone landed on WhatsApp, with her return policy and her working hours in it. It halved her replies. Nobody wants a terms and conditions page as a hello. She cut it to one line and put the policy on the shop page where somebody who cares can go and find it. There's more on that hop in [turning Instagram DMs into orders](/en/blog/turning-instagram-dms-into-orders) and on the market itself in [selling to Gulf customers](/en/blog/selling-to-gulf-customers).

## Four situations, four first messages

| Situation | The message |
|---|---|
| They asked "how much?" | "AED 320 for the black crepe, free delivery in Dubai. That's the heavier one. Want it held?" |
| They followed you but haven't asked | Nothing. Do not message people who followed you. |
| A referral sent them | "Hi, Fatima said you were after the light crepe. AED 260, free delivery in Dubai. Which size are you?" |
| A shop wants ten | "Ten at AED 260 each works, that's AED 2,600. I'd need half up front and about two weeks. Shall I send an invoice?" |

The second row is deliberate. Messaging every new follower with a sales pitch is the single fastest way to get muted, reported, and to make your account look like the fake ones. Let them come to you and make the coming easy.

The referral message is easier than the others because the trust is already borrowed. Name the referrer in the first sentence. That one word does more than any amount of copywriting, and it's most of why a recommendation converts the way it does.

## What a first message can't fix

It can't sell something with no price on the page, because the buyer will still be doing arithmetic instead of deciding.

It can't recover a conversation you took nine hours to join. Speed beats wording. A rough reply in ten minutes beats a perfect one tomorrow, and if you're routinely answering the next day, the problem to solve is coverage, which [answering messages when you are asleep](/en/blog/answering-messages-when-you-are-asleep) deals with directly.

And it can't do the job on its own. Roughly half of first messages get no reply for reasons that have nothing to do with you, and the sequence after that matters, which is [how to follow up without being annoying](/en/blog/how-to-follow-up-without-being-annoying).

One practical note on what the tooling does and doesn't cover. A Sailo WhatsApp order arrives pre-written, with the item, the options, the address and the total already in it, which removes the transcription errors that cause most wrong-size deliveries. It doesn't write the human part, and there's no app and no auto-reply. A message that lands at 1am sits there until you pick up your phone. That's the honest shape of it.

## Rewrite your saved reply today

Open your phone's saved replies or quick replies, the ones you paste twenty times a day. Read the first one out loud.

If it starts with a greeting instead of a number, rewrite it now, using the four parts: the thing, the price with delivery, the one detail they didn't ask for, the three-word question. Save it. Use it on the next message that comes in, and count the replies for a week against what you were getting before.

If nothing's coming in to reply to yet, the problem is upstream, and [getting your first ten orders](/en/blog/getting-your-first-ten-orders) is the part that comes first.
