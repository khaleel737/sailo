You posted nine story frames yesterday. Four hundred people saw the first one, sixty made it to the last one, and the one with the product in it was somewhere in the middle.

Stories work, but not the way most sellers use them. They're not a broadcast channel. They're a queue with a leak in it, and the leak gets worse with every frame you add.

Selling on Instagram stories comes down to three things: put the price in the first frame, put the link sticker on the same frame as the price, and stop at three frames when you're selling. The link sticker is available to every account now, with no follower minimum and no verification, which is a change a lot of sellers still haven't noticed. Your shop page does the rest, and a Sailo one is free at $0 for up to 20 products with the price and options already on it.

The rest of this is what to put in those three frames, and what to do on the other six days.

## The link sticker is free, and the 10,000-follower rule is long gone

For years, external links in stories were reserved for verified accounts and accounts above 10,000 followers, via the old swipe-up. That ended in late 2021 when Instagram replaced swipe-up with the link sticker and opened it to everyone.

If you have 340 followers, you have a link sticker. Personal, creator or business account, it's there. Open the story camera, tap the sticker icon, choose Link, paste your URL.

Two practical notes that matter more than they sound:

- You can rename the sticker text. Don't leave it as your raw URL. "Order the sandals, KSh 3,500" is a sticker people tap. `sailo.store/njerileather` is a sticker people read and scroll past.
- On a private account, only your followers see the sticker at all. If you're running a shop from a private account, that's a decision you should make on purpose rather than by accident.

## Reach falls off a cliff between frame one and frame five

Go into your own insights right now and look at the view count on frame one versus frame six of any story you posted this week. You already know what you'll find. Frame one is the biggest number you will ever see, and it drops with every tap.

This isn't a statistic anyone should quote at you. It's your own data, and it's the single most useful thing in your account, because it tells you exactly where your price has to go.

It has to go in frame one.

Not frame four, after the build-up. Not frame six, after the behind-the-scenes. The people who make it to frame six are the people who were always going to buy. Frame one is where the maybes are, and they're the ones you're trying to convert.

> The frame where you finally reveal the price is the frame nobody is still watching. Reveal it immediately and spend the remaining frames on reasons, not suspense.

## The three-frame sell

When you're actually selling something, three frames is the whole thing.

**Frame one: the product, the price, the sticker.** A clear photo or a five-second clip, the number written on it in text large enough to read at arm's length, and the link sticker sitting right under the price. One frame, three jobs done.

**Frame two: the proof.** A customer's photo, a message someone sent you, a shot of the parcels going out. Not a graphic you made in Canva. Something that shows another human already did this.

**Frame three: the constraint.** How many are left, when you ship, or when it stops. "Six left, posting Thursday" is a constraint. "Available now" is not.

Then stop. Post something else in four hours if you want, but don't run nine frames on one product. Every extra frame is a chance to tap forward, and the person who taps forward past your price doesn't come back for frame seven.

The sequence people get wrong is the reverse of this: build-up, behind-the-scenes, teaser, and the price at the end. That's how you'd design a launch if attention were free. It isn't.

## Stickers are not decoration, they're a cheap yes

The interactive stickers do something specific and underrated. They get a person to touch the screen once before you ask them for money.

Poll, quiz, slider, question box, countdown. Each one is a tiny commitment, and a viewer who has already tapped once is measurably easier to move than one who has only watched. That's ordinary human behaviour, not an Instagram trick.

Where each one earns its place:

| Sticker | Use it for | What you get back |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Poll, two options | Which colour to restock | A stock decision made for free |
| Question box | "What size are you looking for?" | The exact sizes to make next |
| Countdown | A drop with a time on it | A reminder Instagram sends for you |
| Slider | Reaction to a new product | A rough temperature, nothing more |
| Quiz | Product education, not sales | Watch time, and less mute risk |

The countdown is the one small sellers skip and shouldn't. Anyone who taps it gets notified when it ends, which means Instagram does the follow-up you'd otherwise have to do by hand.

A word of caution on the question box. It generates replies, and replies land in your DMs, which means a good question box on a Friday night is fifty unread messages on a Saturday morning. That's a nice problem and still a problem. If your inbox is already the bottleneck, the way to keep story replies from swallowing your weekend is in [turning Instagram DMs into orders](/en/blog/turning-instagram-dms-into-orders).

## Highlights are the part of stories that doesn't expire

Stories die after 24 hours. Highlights don't, and most sellers treat them as a decoration on their profile rather than as the shop window they actually are.

The four highlights that carry a small shop:

1. **Prices.** Literally: each product, one frame, price on it, link sticker. This is the highlight people open.
2. **Reviews.** Screenshots of real messages, faces blurred if you like. Fifteen of them beats a testimonial page.
3. **Delivery.** Parcels, courier labels, doorsteps. It answers "is this person real" without you saying you're real.
4. **How to order.** Three frames. Where to tap, what happens next, how long it takes.

Link stickers stay live inside highlights, so a highlight frame you made in March is still a working shop entrance in August. Update it when your prices change, or you'll be selling last season's numbers to strangers.

## What to post on the days you're not selling

The account that only ever appears with a price attached gets muted. Not unfollowed, muted, which is worse, because you never see it happen and your view counts just quietly sag.

A ratio that holds up: sell hard on two days a week, and spend the other five being someone worth watching. Packing orders. A batch that failed. A courier story. The thing you're testing. Your face.

None of that needs a link sticker. The point isn't to sneak a sale in. It's that a viewer who watched you fix a broken batch on Tuesday taps your price on Thursday without hesitating, and the one who's only ever seen prices doesn't.

If you want to spike the audience side of that on purpose, a giveaway is the standard tool and it goes wrong in standard ways, all of which are in [how to run a giveaway that sells](/en/blog/how-to-run-a-giveaway-that-sells).

## The reshare is the only free reach stories have

A story only goes to people who already follow you. That's the whole design, and it's why stories convert well and grow you not at all.

There is one exception, and it's the most underused thing on the platform. When a customer posts your product to their story and tags you, you can reshare it to yours, and their original sits in front of their followers, none of whom follow you yet. That's a stranger recommending you to their own friends, which is the only kind of reach that arrives pre-trusted.

So build the ask into the order. Not "tag us if you like it", which nobody does. Something with a reason attached:

- A card in the parcel with your handle on it and one line: "tag us and we'll send you the restock date first."
- A message when you mark the order shipped, asking for a photo when it lands.
- A dedicated highlight of customer tags, so people can see that being reshared is a thing that happens here.

Two of these a week, resharing every one, and your story becomes something other people build for you. It costs the price of a printed card.

One mechanical note: resharing only works if your account is public, and it only works if they tagged your account rather than typing your name as text. Sellers lose most of these to a customer writing "@" and then the wrong handle, which is another argument for one word, spelled the same way, everywhere.

## Njeri in Nairobi: KSh 3,500 sandals, sold on Tuesdays

Njeri makes leather sandals in Nairobi. Two styles, KSh 3,500 a pair, made in batches of about twenty. She has around 4,800 followers, most of them Kenyan, and she used to sell entirely by posting a photo and answering "price?" thirty times.

Her stories were the standard mess: eight frames, the sandals appearing around frame five, the price in a reply to whoever asked. On a good week she'd do six pairs.

What she does now takes about four minutes a week and looks like this.

Tuesday, 7pm, three frames. Frame one, sandals on a mat with "KSh 3,500" written across the top and a link sticker underneath saying "get a pair, size 36 to 42". Frame two, a photo a customer in Kisumu sent her of the sandals on. Frame three, "14 pairs this batch, delivery countrywide, posting Friday."

She sells eleven to fifteen pairs most weeks now, from a smaller audience than she had a year ago.

The part she didn't expect: the link sticker made the DMs shorter, not fewer. She still gets messages, but they say "ordered, is Friday still on" instead of "how much and do you have 39". Buyers pay her M-Pesa till, which she's used for years and which Sailo has nothing to do with, and she matches payments to orders herself on Friday morning with the till statement open next to the order list.

One detail worth stealing: her link sticker text always contains the size range. She added it after a week of nothing but size questions, and it cut them roughly in half overnight.

## The measurement problem nobody solves

You will not be able to prove which story frame caused a sale. Not with Instagram's numbers, not with your shop's, not with any tool.

The buyer taps a sticker, opens a browser inside the app, closes it, thinks about it, comes back four hours later by typing your name, and orders. That path is invisible to everyone including Instagram.

What you can do instead is crude and works: post a selling story at a fixed time, and look at the orders in the two hours after it. Do it four Tuesdays in a row. The pattern shows up even though the attribution never will.

This is also where Sailo's honest limit sits. The free plan keeps 30 days of analytics. That's enough to compare this Tuesday to last Tuesday, and not enough to answer "was December better than last December", which is exactly the question you'll want in January. A year of history needs the Pro plan at $9.99 a month, three years needs Business at $19.99. If you're going to run a shop on a seasonal rhythm, know that going in rather than discovering it when the data has already rolled off.

There's no mobile money rail either, so Njeri's M-Pesa till isn't something Sailo processes. She puts the till number in the bank transfer instructions, buyers see it at checkout, and the reconciliation is hers. That's the arrangement for most sellers outside the card economy, and it's worth reading [how to take payment as a small seller](/en/blog/how-to-take-payment-as-a-small-seller) before you design your week around it.

## Do this to tonight's story

Three things, in this order, before you post anything else:

1. Take one photo of your best seller and write the price on it in the largest text that fits.
2. Add a link sticker under the price, and rename the sticker to the action plus the number.
3. Post it as frame one. Not frame four.

Then look at your insights tomorrow and compare the sticker taps to the frame-one views. That ratio is the only number in your Instagram account that has anything to do with money, and once you've seen it you won't go back to burying the price at frame six.
