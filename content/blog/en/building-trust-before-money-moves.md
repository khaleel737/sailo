Eleven minutes of friendly conversation about a Rp185.000 tunic. She's asked about the fabric, the length, whether it comes in navy. Then you send your account number and she never types again.

The instinct is to think you lost her at the account number. You didn't. By the time a buyer sees payment details the decision is mostly made, and if she walks at that moment it's because something four steps earlier didn't land. Trust in DM selling isn't a mood you create with a nice profile. It's built at seven specific moments, in order, and money is the sixth of them.

This is the map of those seven, what each one is actually being judged on, and the wording that works. It's written around Indonesian selling because that's where the pattern is clearest, with cash on delivery as the default and the whole conversation happening in WhatsApp and Instagram before anyone opens a payment app. The sequence is the same anywhere people buy from strangers on their phones.

## The seven moments

1. **The profile**, judged in about eight seconds, before anyone messages you.
2. **The first reply**, judged on speed more than content.
3. **The price answer**, judged on whether you make them ask twice.
4. **The proof**, judged on whether a scammer could have produced the same thing.
5. **The payment ask**, judged on how much risk you're putting on them.
6. **The confirmation**, judged on whether anything happened after they paid.
7. **The after**, judged weeks later, and it decides the second order.

Most sellers work hard on one and three and neglect two, four and seven. Two and seven are where the money is.

## One: the profile

A stranger arrives from a reel and spends a few seconds deciding whether you're a business or a risk. What they check, in roughly this order: is there a price anywhere, is there a face, how old is this account, and does anything here look like other people have actually bought.

What kills it: a grid of supplier catalogue photos with no prices, a bio that says "trusted, no tipu tipu", zero posts older than two months, and comments that are all from the same three accounts.

What fixes it costs nothing. Put prices where they can be seen. Put your own face in at least one recent post. Stop deleting old posts to keep the grid tidy, because your archive is the one credential nobody can fake in a hurry. And pin a post that shows parcels going out, with waybills visible.

## Two: the first reply

This one is worth more than everything else on this list and almost nobody treats it as a trust signal.

Reply inside ten minutes and you'll usually get the order. Reply in two hours and you'll get "sorry sis, sudah beli" from someone who bought a nearly identical thing at a similar price from someone who answered. The price was never the reason.

You can't be instant all day. So do the two things that get you most of the benefit:

Set an away message with real hours in it, not a vague apology. "I answer between 8am and 9pm, usually within the hour. If it's after 9 I'll reply first thing." Specific hours make a slow reply into a kept promise instead of a red flag.

And when you can't answer properly, answer badly and fast. "Hi! Yes it's available, give me twenty minutes and I'll send you the details and the size chart." Twelve words, sent from a bus, holds the sale.

## Three: the price answer

"DM for price" is the most expensive four words in social selling. It costs you the buyers who won't ask, and it costs you trust with the ones who do, because a price that has to be requested reads as a price that changes depending on who's asking.

Answer in public, with the number, every time. Then answer again in the DM without irritation, because a person who asks a question you've already answered is a person who is about to buy.

The whole answer, in one message:

> Rp185.000. Sizes S to XL, the L fits like a UK 12. Delivery Rp20.000 within Java, Rp35.000 outside, or COD in Bandung. Here's the link with all the colours: [link]

Price, sizing in a form they can picture, delivery cost, payment options, link. One message. A buyer who has to ask four separate questions to assemble that has four separate opportunities to lose interest.

## Four: the proof

Everything a buyer sees at this stage should pass one test: could a fake shop produce this just as easily? If yes, it's decoration.

Fails the test: a logo, follower count, "100% ORI" in the bio, a highlight full of typed testimonial graphics, product photos taken from the supplier's catalogue that four other accounts are also using.

Passes the test:

- **A short video of you handling the actual item.** Your hands, your room, your voice. Ten seconds. This is the highest-trust thing you can produce and it costs nothing.
- **The parcel going out**, waybill number visible, being handed to a named courier.
- **A customer's own photo or video**, with the timestamp left in.
- **Old comment threads** where the same names appear more than once.
- **A location.** A district, a street, an offer to meet at a mall in Bandung if they're local. Even buyers 800km away find this reassuring, because it means you exist somewhere specific.

The best of these travels. A short dispatch video gets forwarded into a group chat by the buyer who is deciding, and the order that comes back is often from her friend rather than from her. That's the mechanism most sellers never see, and it's the reason it's worth making the video the same way every time so it's recognisable.

There's a longer version of this, aimed at markets where buyers have been burned repeatedly, in [proving you are a real seller](/en/blog/proving-you-are-a-real-seller).

## Five: the payment ask

Here's where the sequence either pays off or doesn't. Three rules.

**Say what the account name will be, before you send the number.** The buyer sees a name on screen before confirming a transfer. If they weren't expecting it, that surprise reads as danger, and it's also exactly how the copycat-account scam works.

> Before I send it: the account is under my own name, SARI WULANDARI. That's what you'll see. If any other name comes up, stop and screenshot it for me.

**Offer to carry some of the risk, without being asked.** This is the single most effective sentence available to a new seller.

> If you'd rather not transfer to someone you don't know yet, that's fine. COD if you're in Bandung, or Rp50.000 now and the rest when it arrives and you've opened it.

**Never send payment details from a different account, ever, and tell them so.** Then that becomes a rule they can use to detect an impostor.

A written refund policy belongs here too, and it does its work on the buyers who never ask about it. Two lines saying what happens if the thing arrives wrong is worth more at this moment than any amount of reassurance, because it's the only reassurance that survives you going quiet. How to write one you'll actually honour is in [writing a refund policy people trust](/en/blog/writing-a-refund-policy-people-trust).

Cash on delivery does the trust work more completely than anything you can say, which is a large part of why it's the default across Indonesia and isn't going anywhere. It also costs you real money in refusals, and pretending otherwise is how sellers get hurt. What a refusal actually costs is worked out in [cash on delivery for small sellers](/en/blog/cash-on-delivery-for-small-sellers), and the specific failure mode where orders arrive and then evaporate is in [dealing with fake orders and time wasters](/en/blog/dealing-with-fake-orders-and-time-wasters).

If a buyer suggests using a third-party escrow, a rekber, that's a request about the situation rather than an insult about you. Treat it that way. Agreeing to it costs you a small fee and buys you a customer who was otherwise gone, and refusing it in an offended tone confirms exactly the thing they were worried about.

## Six: the confirmation

The five minutes after someone pays are the loudest silence in the transaction. She's transferred Rp185.000 to a stranger and is now sitting there with nothing.

Fill it immediately, even if you've done nothing yet.

> Got it, thank you. Order 1042: tunic, navy, size L, Rp185.000 plus Rp20.000 delivery. I'll pack it tonight and it goes out tomorrow morning, and I'll send you the waybill number and a video of it leaving. Anything wrong, message me right here.

Then send the waybill when you said you would. That's it. The follow-through is the trust, not the message.

Note what it doesn't do: it doesn't confirm the money has arrived unless you've actually seen it in your account. On a manual rail nobody tells you a transfer has landed, so "got it" should mean "I've got your order" and a separate message should say "I've seen your payment". Blurring those two is how sellers end up shipping against a transfer that never came. The full routine for that is in [how to know a bank transfer actually arrived](/en/blog/how-to-know-a-bank-transfer-actually-arrived), and the anatomy of the confirmation itself is in [how to write an order confirmation](/en/blog/how-to-write-an-order-confirmation).

## Seven: the after

The moment almost everyone skips, and the one that produces the second order.

Message once, four or five days after it arrives. Not a marketing blast. One line, from a person:

> Hi Dewi, did the tunic fit OK? Just checking, no need to reply if it's all fine.

"No need to reply" is what makes people reply.

Three things come out of this. You catch the problem the buyer wasn't going to tell you about, which is the one that otherwise becomes a public complaint. You get a photo you can post, if you ask for it right there. And you convert a transaction into a relationship, which is worth more than any single order, because a returning customer needs none of moments one through five ever again.

When something has gone wrong, this is also where you find out early enough to fix it cheaply. The wording for that conversation is in [how to handle an angry customer](/en/blog/how-to-handle-an-angry-customer), and if it's already public, [handling a bad review](/en/blog/handling-a-bad-review) covers the reply that readers rather than reviewers are judging.

## Where it breaks, and what each break costs

| Moment | The common break | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Profile | No prices anywhere | Every buyer who won't ask, and that's most of them |
| First reply | Two hours | The order, to whoever answered in ten minutes |
| Price answer | "DM for price" | Trust, and the same question forty more times |
| Proof | Supplier catalogue photos | Nothing distinguishes you from a fake page |
| Payment ask | All the risk on the buyer | The nervous half of your market |
| Confirmation | Silence after payment | The chasing messages, and sometimes a dispute |
| After | Nothing at all | The second order, which was the profitable one |

## Worked example: Sari, modest wear, Bandung

Sari sells tunics and hijabs from her house in Bandung. Rp185.000 for a tunic, Rp65.000 for a hijab, delivery Rp20.000 within Java. She does somewhere between 80 and 120 orders a month, all through Instagram and WhatsApp, roughly half COD within Bandung and half transfer.

Her first six months, about a third of her conversations died the moment she sent her account number. She'd answer "amanah kok" and watch the typing indicator stop.

What she changed, and roughly what each one did:

She started answering price in the comments, publicly, with the number. Her DM volume went up, not down, and the DMs she got were from people who already knew the price and wanted to buy.

She recorded the same eight-second dispatch video for every order and sent it unprompted: the folded tunic going into the bag, the waybill with the number visible, the courier taking it. She now believes this is the single most effective thing she does, mostly because of the forwarding. Around one order in six is from someone who says "my friend showed me your video".

She added the account-name line before sending details.

And she started sending the four-day check-in. That one felt awkward for about a month. It now produces roughly a fifth of her repeat orders and it has caught eleven sizing problems that would otherwise have arrived as complaints, or worse, as nothing at all.

The thing she'd tell someone starting: the buyers who ask the most questions are not the time-wasters. The time-wasters ask one question and vanish. The buyer who wants to know about the fabric, the length and the delivery is a buyer who is trying to talk herself into it, and the seller's job is simply to keep answering until she has.

## What a shop link proves, and what it doesn't

Worth being straight about the limits here, because it's where sellers over-trust their tools.

A Sailo shop at sailo.store/yourname is live the moment you sign up. That means anyone can have one in five minutes, including the person copying your photos. There's no verification, no check that you exist, and no badge that means a human looked at you. On the free plan there's actually a Sailo badge on the page, which the Pro plan at $9.99 a month removes, and neither state says anything at all about whether you'll ship.

There's also no escrow. On bank transfer and cash on delivery, Sailo never touches the money and takes nothing from it, which is why those rails are free, and it's also why the platform cannot vouch for either side or hold anything in the middle. If you and a buyer want a third party holding the money, that's an arrangement the two of you make elsewhere.

Cards are the one rail where the money can come back out of your account weeks later on the buyer's say-so, and if you ever turn them on, that risk is worth understanding before rather than after: [what to do about a chargeback](/en/blog/what-to-do-about-a-chargeback) sets out which rails can be reversed and which cannot.

And there's no e-wallet rail. There's no OVO, DANA or GoPay option in the payment settings, and no mobile money rail of any kind. What sellers do in practice is put those details into the free-text instructions box under the bank transfer fields, which works and is worth knowing, but it's a workaround you're running rather than a feature doing anything for you. You'll be checking those payments by hand, in the wallet app, exactly as you do now.

What the page genuinely does is narrower: it holds your prices, sizes, delivery costs and terms in one place that doesn't change between conversations. Inconsistency is one of the loudest scam signals there is, and a seller whose price is Rp185.000 in the DM and Rp165.000 in the comments looks like someone making it up.

## Do this this week

Pick the two moments you're weakest at. For most sellers they're the first reply and the after, because those are the ones that feel like admin rather than selling.

Set an away message with real hours in it today. Record the dispatch video on your next order and send it without being asked. Then put a reminder in your phone for four days after your next delivery, with one line: "did it fit OK?"

That's three small habits, and between them they cover four of the seven moments. Everything else here is refinement.
