Enrolment opens, your messages triple in a week, and then it goes completely quiet for a month and you assume you've done something wrong.

You haven't. Back-to-school demand in the Philippines arrives in three separate waves, not one, and they're weeks apart. The first is enrolment, when parents buy the required list. The second is two to three weeks after classes start, when things get lost, broken or turn out to be the wrong size. The third is at the opening of the second semester. A seller doing ₱180 name-sticker sheets and ₱450 lunch bags will take roughly half their annual orders in the first wave, a quarter in the second, and the rest spread thin across the year.

Plan for three waves and the month of silence in between stops being alarming.

## Anchor to the DepEd opening date, not to a month

The Philippine school calendar has moved more than once in the last few years. It ran June to March for decades, shifted later during the pandemic, and has been walking back towards an earlier opening since. So don't build your plan around "June" or "August". Build it around the school opening date DepEd announces for that year, and count backwards from it.

The relative timings hold whatever the calendar does:

| When | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 8 to 10 weeks before opening | Enrolment period begins in most public schools. Parents start pricing things |
| 6 weeks before | Your stock and materials must be ordered. Suppliers in Divisoria and online get thin after this |
| 4 to 2 weeks before | The buying wave. Most of your first-wave revenue lands here |
| Opening week | Almost nothing. Everyone is at school |
| 2 to 3 weeks after opening | Wave two. Replacements, wrong sizes, the things the teacher asked for that weren't on the list |
| Second semester opening | Wave three, smaller, mostly consumables |

Write those six rows against real dates as soon as the opening date is announced. That's the whole planning exercise, and it takes twenty minutes.

## Payday is the demand signal

Nothing about back-to-school buying is smooth, and the reason isn't seasonal, it's payroll. Most Philippine employers pay on the 15th and the end of the month. Orders spike in the two or three days after each, and go quiet in between.

This matters more than it sounds. If you launch your back-to-school range on the 8th, you're launching into the flattest part of the month, and your post will be four days old and buried by the time anyone has money. Launch on the 15th or the 30th, or the day after.

The same rhythm shapes what you should offer:

- **Deposits work well mid-month.** A ₱200 deposit on a ₱1,200 uniform set lets a parent commit on the 8th and pay the balance on the 15th, which is exactly the conversation you'd otherwise be having in DMs anyway.
- **Instalments are already normal here.** "Half now, half on the 30th" is not exotic and nobody will find it strange if you offer it. Just write down who owes what.
- **Bundles beat singles right after payday**, when there's money available, and singles beat bundles at the end of the month.

The mechanics of taking a deposit without a payment gateway are in [taking a deposit before you start](/en/blog/taking-a-deposit-before-you-start), and it's the single most useful thing you can add to a back-to-school shop.

## Bundle by grade level, not by product

This is where most small back-to-school shops lose money, and it's not a pricing problem, it's a catalogue problem.

A parent with a Grade 3 child does not want to browse 40 items and work out which pencils are correct. They want somebody to have already done that. So build the bundle the school list describes, name it after the grade, and price it as one thing.

"**Grade 3 complete set — ₱1,450**. Includes: 12 notebooks with name labels, 1 pencil case, 2 sets of pencils, crayons, scissors, ruler, 60 name stickers, 1 lunch bag."

Three things happen at once. Your average order value goes up, because a bundle sells items a browsing parent would never have added. Your packing gets faster, because you're assembling the same set repeatedly rather than picking 40 unique baskets. And your questions in DMs collapse, because the bundle answers the question the parent was going to ask.

Keep four to six bundles, one per grade band, plus your five best-selling singles for the second wave. That's it. A back-to-school catalogue with 60 SKUs looks impressive and converts badly, because the parent buying at 10pm after work does not want a project.

## The three waves, and what to sell in each

**Wave one, enrolment.** Bundles, uniforms, shoes, bags, the required list. High value orders, planned buying, parents comparing prices across three sellers. Photography and a clear price matter enormously here because you're being compared. This is the wave to take deposits in.

**Wave two, three weeks in.** Replacements. A lost water bottle, a broken lunch bag, a notebook that ran out, the specific colour of art paper a teacher demanded on Tuesday. Low value, urgent, and almost nobody stocks for it because everyone sold out in wave one and moved on. Deliberately hold back stock of your five fastest-moving singles for this. Margins are better and there's no competition.

**Wave three, second semester.** Mostly consumables and a smaller repeat of wave two. Worth a single post to your existing buyers rather than a campaign.

Holding stock for wave two is the part sellers skip. You've just sold out, it feels like success, and the temptation is to bank the cash. Then a parent messages you on a Wednesday needing a ₱250 lunch bag today and you don't have one, and neither does anyone else, and that was a sale with no marketing cost attached to it.

## COD, and why your confirmation message matters more now

Cash on delivery is the default expectation, not a fallback. In the Philippines a shop with no COD option reads as suspicious rather than efficient, and during enrolment season, when parents are spending on five different things at once, it's how most of your orders will come in.

The problem is that back-to-school COD refusals run higher than normal, for a specific reason: the parent orders from three sellers for the same list and keeps whichever arrives first. That's not fraud, it's a rational response to not knowing who'll deliver in time.

So the confirmation message does more work in this season than in any other. Send it within an hour of the order, and make it require a reply:

> Hi Ms. Cruz, confirming: Grade 3 complete set, ₱1,450, COD to 22 Sanciangko St, Cebu City. Ships tomorrow, arrives Thursday or Friday. Please have ₱1,450 ready, the rider calls from an unknown number. Reply YES and I'll pack it today.

The arrival window is the part that stops the triple-ordering. A parent who knows it lands Thursday doesn't need a backup order.

The rest of the COD discipline, the refusal costs, the change problem, the courier remittance, is in [cash on delivery for small sellers](/en/blog/cash-on-delivery-for-small-sellers). It applies here with the volume turned up.

## Names, sizes and the errors that cost you the order

Personalisation is most of the value in this category and it's also where every mistake lives.

**Ask for the name in writing, then read it back.** Roughly one parent in fifteen gives a nickname when they wanted the full name, or the other way round, and you find out when the stickers arrive. Read it back in caps exactly as you'll print it: "printing as: MIGUEL A. SANTOS — confirm please?" It takes ten seconds and it eliminates the most expensive kind of remake.

**Print in batches by name, not by order.** If you're doing 60 sticker sheets, sort them alphabetically and print them in one run. You'll halve your setup time and you'll spot the two spellings that look wrong sitting next to each other.

**Get the size from a measurement, not a size label.** Uniform sizing varies by school and by supplier. Ask for chest and height in centimetres and keep a conversion chart. "Medium" means nothing and it will come back to you.

**Photograph the finished personalised item before it goes in the box.** One phone photo, saved against the order. When a parent says the name was wrong, you either have proof or you have an argument. The habit costs nothing until the day it saves ₱1,450.

Keep one written list of every variant you actually stock, in one place, and delete the ones you've run out of the same day. A parent ordering a size you no longer have is a refund and a message you didn't need.

## Shipping outside your city

If you're selling from Metro Manila or Cebu to the provinces, transit times roughly double, and back-to-school is exactly the season where that matters because there's a hard date at the end.

Two rules that prevent most of the pain:

**Publish separate cut-offs by region.** "Metro Manila and Cebu City: order by [date]. Visayas and Mindanao: order by [date minus five days]." Parents in the provinces are used to this and will respect it. What they won't respect is a single cut-off that turns out to be a Manila cut-off.

**Ask your courier for their provincial transit times in writing before the season, and add two days.** Enrolment period is high volume for them too, and the published transit time is a good week, not a busy one.

## Worked example: Marilou, name labels and lunch bags, Cebu City

Marilou prints personalised name stickers and sells imported lunch bags from a room in her house in Cebu City. She works alone, with one printer.

Her products:

- 60-sticker sheet, personalised, **₱180**. Costs her about ₱42 in vinyl and ink.
- Lunch bag with name embroidered, **₱450**. Bags cost ₱210 from her supplier.
- "Starter set", stickers plus bag plus a labelled water bottle, **₱780**.

Her constraint: the printer does about 25 sheets an hour, and she can't print and pack at the same time.

Her first back-to-school season she took 340 orders in ten days, and she was printing until 2am for a fortnight, and she still shipped 30 of them late. The second year:

1. **Capped orders at 40 a day**, published as "40 slots daily, resets at 8am". Sold out most days by noon.
2. **Bundled into three sets** by grade band instead of listing every item.
3. **Ordered bags in May**, before her supplier's price went up and before the stock got thin.
4. **Held 60 bags and 200 sticker sheets back** for wave two. Sold every one of them at full price in the three weeks after classes started, with zero advertising.
5. **Took a ₱200 deposit** by GCash on anything over ₱700, with the balance COD. Her refusal rate dropped by more than half.

Revenue went up about a third, and the late shipments went to two. The change that did most of the work was the daily cap, because it turned an unpredictable flood into a known quantity of work she could finish before bed.

## The honest limitation

Back-to-school catalogues get big fast, and Sailo's free plan caps you at **20 products**. Between three grade bundles, five singles, colour variants and the second-wave replacements, twenty is genuinely tight for this category. Pro is $9.99 a month for 250 products, which is the plan most sellers in this position end up on.

The other one worth knowing before you build a plan around it: **Sailo has no GCash rail.** There's no mobile money integration at all. The payment rails are card, bank transfer, cash on delivery, and orders via WhatsApp, Telegram, Instagram, email or phone. What sellers here actually do is put their GCash number and name in the bank transfer instructions field, which is free text, and confirm the payment themselves when it lands. That works, and plenty of people run their whole business on it, but be clear about what it is: a workaround using a text box, not a feature. Nothing checks the money for you.

If keeping track of who's paid across deposits, balances and COD is the part that worries you, [keeping track of who has paid](/en/blog/keeping-track-of-who-has-paid) is the routine.

## Do this eight weeks before opening

Write your six-row calendar against the announced opening date. Then order materials, because six weeks out is when your suppliers start running out, and the price of what's left goes up.

Build three bundles by grade band, price them as single items, and photograph each bundle laid out flat so a parent can count the contents in one look. Decide your daily order cap before you post anything.

Then, and this is the one people skip: write down which five singles you're holding back for wave two, and how many of each. Put them in a separate box and don't touch them.
