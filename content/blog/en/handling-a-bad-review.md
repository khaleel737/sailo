One star, posted at 4:30am. "Arrived leaking. Seller ignored my messages. Avoid." You did not ignore anything, you replied twice, and the bottle was fine when it left you.

Your reply is not for that person. It's for the next few hundred people who read the review while deciding whether to spend $14 with you, and they will read your reply more carefully than they read the complaint. Reply inside 24 hours, keep it shorter than the review, and use three parts: acknowledge the specific thing, say what you did about it, and give a way to reach you. Do not argue the facts. Ever.

That's the method. The rest is what to do in the six situations you'll actually meet, and the two occasions when the right move is silence.

## Who you're really writing to

A reader deciding whether to buy from you does something predictable. They sort to the worst reviews first, read two or three, and then look at what the seller said back.

What they're checking is not whether the complaint is fair. They already assume some complaints aren't. They're checking one thing: what is this seller like when something goes wrong? That's the only question a review section can actually answer, and it's the reason a one-star with a calm, specific reply underneath it is worth more to you than no one-star at all.

Which means the goal of a reply is never to win. A reader who watches you win an argument learns that you argue.

## Twenty-four hours, and shorter than theirs

Two rules that do most of the work.

**Reply within a day.** A month-old complaint with no response reads as abandonment, and readers date it by looking at the timestamps. Reply the same day where you can, and never later than the next one.

**Keep it shorter than the review.** This is the rule nobody follows. A three-line complaint answered with eleven lines of explanation makes the seller look rattled and makes the complaint look important. Match their length or come in under it.

There's a third rule that only applies once: do not reply twice. If they come back angrier under your reply, take it to a message. A public thread with four seller replies in it is the worst thing on your page, worse than the original review.

## The formula, with the parts named

**Acknowledge the specific thing.** Not "sorry you had a bad experience". The actual thing. "Sorry, a leaking bottle is genuinely annoying and it shouldn't happen."

**Say what you did.** Past tense, with a date if you have one. "I switched to a different cap and a seal in March." This is the sentence that converts readers, and almost nobody writes it. A competitor can copy your apology. They cannot copy a fix.

**Give a route.** "Message me on [number] and I'll replace it." Short. Not a discount code, not an offer, just a door.

Put together:

> Sorry, Jenna. A leaking bottle is a bad start and I hate that it happened. Two of these went out with a batch of caps that didn't seal properly in February, and I changed suppliers the week after. Message me on 267-555-0142 and I'll send a replacement, no need to send anything back.

Fifty-eight words. Nothing in it contradicts her. It doesn't mention that you replied twice, because that argument helps you and costs you every reader.

## Six reviews and what to say to each

**"Never arrived."** Facts you have, offered without contradiction.

> Sorry, that's the worst version of this. The tracking shows it was left in the porch on the 14th, but tracking is wrong often enough that I'd rather just sort it. Message me and I'll either resend or refund, your choice.

Notice the phrase "tracking is wrong often enough". It lets you state the fact you need on the record without calling them a liar.

**"Not what I expected."** Take it, and fix the listing in public.

> That's fair, and it's my description's fault rather than yours. I've added the measurements and a photo next to a mug so the size is obvious. Happy to refund it, message me any time.

Admitting the listing was unclear costs nothing and reads as competence. It also genuinely reduces the next ten complaints.

**"Took two weeks to arrive."** When it's the courier, say so once and then take responsibility anyway.

> Two weeks is too long, sorry. That one sat in a depot for nine days and I couldn't get an answer out of them either. I've moved to a different courier for anything going west of Ohio since May. If you want to try again I'll cover the postage.

**"Overpriced."** Don't defend the price. Explain the choice once, in a sentence, and let readers decide.

> Fair enough, it's not a cheap bottle. It's made in small batches with fresh peppers rather than mash, which costs about three times as much, and that's the whole difference. Thanks for trying it.

Never apologise for a price you've thought about. Never argue about it either.

**One star and no words.** These are common and they're mostly a slipped thumb or a delivery mood. Reply once, briefly, and move on.

> Sorry to see this. I've no idea what went wrong, but I'd like to fix it if you tell me. [number]

**The one that isn't a customer.** A competitor, an ex-employee, someone who has confused you with another business. This is the only situation where you don't reply with a fix.

> I can't find an order matching this name or address. If we have done business and I've got it wrong, message me and I'll sort it immediately. If this is meant for another shop, no hard feelings.

Then report it through the platform's process, with the reason "not a customer", and let it go. Do not accuse them in public. Readers who see a seller shouting "this is a fake review" assume it isn't.

## What a defensive reply costs, in numbers

Work out your own version of this once and you'll never write a defensive reply again.

Say a $14 bottle carries $6 of margin. A reader who bounces off your page because of one bad exchange costs you $6 once, and a repeat customer maybe $30 a year. If your worst review is read by even a few hundred people over a year, and it changes the mind of a handful, the reply you wrote at midnight to defend yourself is the most expensive sentence on the internet for you.

Meanwhile refunding the complaint costs $6.

That arithmetic is why the threshold for "just fix it" sits so low, and why almost every seller's instinct is wrong in the same direction. The same maths applies to the private version of this argument, which is in [how to handle an angry customer](/en/blog/how-to-handle-an-angry-customer).

## What can actually be removed

Very little, and only for the reasons the platform lists. Broadly, most review platforms will consider removing content that is abusive, contains personal information, is obviously spam, or comes from someone who was never a customer. None of them remove a review for being unfair, wrong, or one star.

So report the ones that genuinely break a rule, once, through the proper form, with a factual explanation and no adjectives. Then stop thinking about it. Sellers lose whole weeks to appeals that were never going to work.

The exception worth chasing: a review that's clearly meant for a different business. Those get removed reasonably often, because they're a factual error rather than an opinion.

## Asking for an update, once

Once you've fixed it properly, you're allowed to ask. Once. Weeks later. Never as a condition of the fix.

> Glad that sorted it. No obligation at all, but if you felt like updating your review at some point I'd be grateful. Either way, thanks for giving me the chance to fix it.

Never offer anything in exchange for changing a review. Most platforms ban it, and more importantly, a customer who has been paid to change a review will tell someone.

About a third of people update it. The rest don't, and the fixed customer is worth more than the star anyway.

## The real defence is a denominator

One one-star among nine five-stars is a 4.6, and it looks like a problem. One one-star among ninety is a 4.9, and it looks like the internet.

Nothing you write in a reply moves your rating. Volume moves your rating, and volume is a habit rather than a campaign: ask every satisfied customer, at the right moment, in one sentence, and keep asking. The mechanics of doing that without being annoying are in [how to collect reviews that convince](/en/blog/how-to-collect-reviews-that-convince).

A seller with 12 reviews is one bad week from a rating they can't recover. A seller with 200 is unbothered. That's the whole strategy, and it is much duller and much more effective than anything you can write at 4:30am.

## Reviews on your own shop page are worth less than you think

Sailo does have reviews. A buyer can leave a name, a rating out of five and a comment on any published product, and approved ones show on the product page with an average and a count. Every submission lands unapproved in your admin, and you either approve it or delete it.

Sit with that for a second, because it's the honest problem with reviews on any seller-controlled page, not just this one. You can delete the bad ones. Nothing stops you. A page showing eleven five-star reviews and nothing else is exactly what a seller who deletes everything would produce, and experienced buyers know it.

Two other things worth knowing about how it works here. There's no purchase check: a review isn't tied to an order, so anyone who can open the product page can leave one, including someone who has never bought from you and someone who is not your friend. That's precisely why the approve step exists. And there's no reply field, so the three-part formula above has nowhere to go on your own page. You'd have to answer in a message instead.

The practical way to use it: approve the honest four-stars along with the fives. A four-star saying "lovely, delivery took a while" is the single most credible thing that can appear on a seller's own page, because nobody fakes those. And treat the reviews you don't control, Google, your comments, the marketplace ratings, as the ones that actually persuade strangers, because a buyer knows you can't delete those.

## Worked example: Nate, hot sauce, Philadelphia

Nate makes small-batch hot sauce in a shared kitchen in Philadelphia. $14 a bottle, $38 for a three-pack, around 200 bottles a month, sold through a Sailo shop, two local shops and a farmers market.

Last February he got two one-stars in a week, both about leaking. He knew why: a box of caps from a new supplier that didn't seal on the wider bottles. He'd already fixed it by the time the reviews appeared, which is what made his replies work, because he could write a date.

His first instinct was to explain the whole cap situation in detail. He cut it to two lines. He replied to both within a day, said what had changed and when, and offered replacements with no return.

One reviewer updated to four stars three weeks later, unprompted. The other never replied. Nate reckons the second one was worth more, because it sat there for a year underneath a reply that said he'd changed suppliers, and three separate customers mentioned it to him at the market as the reason they'd trusted him.

The thing he'd tell anyone: the review that hurt his sales most wasn't either of those. It was a three-star from someone who said "fine but the box was crushed", which sat unanswered for five weeks because he didn't think a three-star needed a reply. Three-stars need a reply more than one-stars do, because readers take them seriously. He now answers everything below five within a day, and he changed his packing after that one, which is covered better than he could explain it in [packaging that survives the courier](/en/blog/packaging-that-survives-the-courier).

## Do this today

Go and find your oldest unanswered review, whatever the rating, and answer it now with the three parts. Old replies are still read.

Then write your two saved replies, the "sorry, here's what changed" one and the "I can't find this order" one, and keep them where you can reach them from your phone. Composing these while stung is what produces the paragraph you regret.
