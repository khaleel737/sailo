You typed your own shop name into Google and got a dormant Facebook page from 2019, a company in Ohio with a similar name, and nothing else. Meanwhile someone told you that you need to "do SEO", which as far as you can tell means writing 40 blog posts you don't have time for.

Three things actually work at your size, and they take about a day between them.

Be findable by your own name. Show up for your town plus the thing you sell, if you sell locally. And have two or three product pages written in the words a person would type, on a page a search engine can read. That's the realistic list. Ranking for "buy shoes online" is a two-year project with a budget, and you're not in that race, which is good news because you'd lose it.

The rest of this is how to do each one, roughly how long it takes, and the part where it's fair to say that search is not your best channel yet.

## What you can realistically rank for in year one

Search intent comes in rough tiers, and they're not equally winnable.

| What someone types | Can you win it | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Your shop's name | Yes, in weeks | Almost nobody else is competing for it |
| "cake delivery Nakuru" | Often, in months | Local results favour local businesses with a verified profile |
| "1kg red velvet cake price Nakuru" | Often | Long, specific, and most shops never write the words |
| "best cake shop Kenya" | No | Aggregators and directories own this |
| "buy cake online" | No | Not a real query with a real buyer behind it anyway |

Notice the pattern. The queries you can win are the ones where somebody has already decided most of the details. Those are also the ones that convert, because a person typing five words has done four decisions already.

The ones you can't win are broad, and they're full of listicles written by people who have never sold a cake.

I'm not going to give you a percentage for any of this. You'll read a lot of confident numbers about click-through rates by position and how many searches are local, and most of them trace back to a study nobody links, run on a sample that doesn't look like you. Measure your own instead. That's the only figure that describes your shop.

## Be findable by your own name first

This is the cheapest win and most sellers skip it because it feels too small.

Someone hears about you from a friend. They don't have the link. They type what they remember, which is your name, or your name plus "Instagram", or your name plus the city. If the first page of that search is confusing, that's an order gone, and it's an order you'd already earned.

What to do, in about 40 minutes:

1. **Search your own name in an incognito window.** Look at the whole first page. Note what's missing and what's wrong.
2. **Make sure the same name is on every profile.** Instagram, WhatsApp business profile, TikTok, your shop link. Not "Njeri's Cakes" in one place and "njeri_bakes_ke" in another and "N. Cakes Nakuru" in a third.
3. **Put the shop link in every bio**, spelled identically. Repetition of the exact URL across profiles is how search engines work out these are the same business.
4. **Put your name in your page title.** Not just "Shop" or "Home".

If someone else already ranks for a name close to yours and they're bigger, consider changing your name now rather than in three years. Nobody wants to hear that. It's cheaper today than it will ever be again.

## If you sell locally, the free listing is the highest-return hour

Google Business Profile is free, it's the thing that puts you in the map results, and for a seller with a physical presence or a delivery radius it's worth more than everything else in this article combined.

You need a real business name, a category, a service area or an address, hours, and a phone number. Verification varies by country and category, and it can be a postcard, a phone call, or a video of your premises. It's tedious and it's a one-off.

The parts people fill in badly:

- **Category.** Pick the most specific one that's true. "Bakery" beats "Food and drink". A wrong-but-broader category costs you the queries you could have won.
- **Photos.** Add real ones, and add more over time. A profile with three photos from eighteen months ago reads as closed.
- **The service area.** If you deliver to five neighbourhoods, list five, not the whole country.
- **Posts and questions.** People will ask questions on the profile. Answer them, because those answers show up in the results.

If you sell only online, with no location and no delivery area, skip this. You won't get verified and you'll waste a week trying.

## Make the page readable, which is mostly not a technical job

Search engines read text. That's the whole constraint, and small sellers break it constantly by putting the important words in images.

The flavour, the size, the price and the delivery area go in the text of the page. Not baked into the graphic. A beautiful tile that says "1kg Red Velvet, KSh 2,500, free delivery in Nakuru" is invisible to search, and it's also invisible to anyone using a screen reader.

Then, in rough order of value:

**One page per product.** Not one page with twelve products described in a paragraph. Each product gets its own URL, its own title, its own description. This is the single biggest structural thing, and it's automatic on most shop tools.

**Titles that read like a query.** "Red velvet cake 1kg, Nakuru delivery" is a title. "Our Signature Creation" is not. Put the thing, the size or variant, and the place if place matters.

**Descriptions with real sentences.** Not keyword lists. Write the answer to the three questions a buyer asks in the DMs anyway, and [writing product descriptions that sell](/en/blog/writing-product-descriptions-that-sell) is the same job done properly.

**Alt text on images.** One plain sentence describing the photo. It takes six seconds and it's the only thing standing between your images and being uncatalogued.

**A page that loads on a bad connection.** Search results are increasingly decided on mobile, and a lot of your buyers are on patchy data. [What a storefront should load like on 3G](/en/blog/what-a-storefront-should-load-like-on-3g) covers what actually makes a page fast, which is mostly image sizes rather than anything clever.

## Write the words your buyer types, not the words you'd use

Sellers write "artisanal", buyers type "cheap". Sellers write "bespoke celebration cake", buyers type "birthday cake price".

The way to find the real words costs nothing:

- Scroll your own DMs. Copy the exact phrases people use when they ask you things. That's a keyword list written by your actual market.
- Type the start of a query into Google and read what it suggests. Those suggestions come from real searches.
- Read the "People also ask" box on a search for your product. Those are questions with demand behind them.
- Look at what's in the comments under competitors' posts.

Then use those phrases in your titles and descriptions, in normal sentences. Not stuffed. Once each, in the right place, is enough.

## Njeri's cakes in Nakuru

Njeri bakes at home in Nakuru. A 1kg cake is KSh 2,500, delivery within town is KSh 300, and she takes orders on WhatsApp with M-Pesa on delivery or a till number for a deposit.

For a year, her shop page had six cakes, all with names like "Ruby" and "Midnight", each described in one line, and every price sat inside the photo. She got no search orders at all. Every customer came from Instagram or a friend.

She changed four things over two afternoons:

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Product name "Ruby" | "Red velvet cake 1kg, Nakuru delivery" |
| Price in the image | Price in the text, plus delivery cost |
| One page listing all six | Six separate product pages |
| No Google Business Profile | Verified profile, service area listed as Nakuru town and Lanet |

Her first search order arrived seven weeks later. It wasn't through the home page. Somebody searched something close to "red velvet cake price nakuru" and landed straight on that product page, read the price, and messaged.

The detail that mattered most is the one that felt least like SEO. The page only started showing up after she stopped putting the flavour and the price in the picture and put them in words. Everything else she did was tidying.

Over the following four months she got 11 orders she could trace to search. Not a flood. But 11 orders at KSh 2,500 is KSh 27,500 from work she did once, and the profile keeps working while she's asleep, which is not true of a story post. ## Why you probably shouldn't start a blog

The advice everyone gives a small seller is to publish articles. For most sellers it's the wrong advice, for a boring reason: it takes months and it competes against people who do it full time.

Writing is worth it when you sell something with a genuine information problem attached. If people search "how to keep a fondant cake in humid weather" before they search for a cake, an article can catch them. If they search "birthday cake Nakuru", an article catches nobody, because they don't want to read, they want to order.

So the test is: does a buyer need to learn something before they buy? If no, put the hours into product pages and the local profile instead.

## How long this takes, honestly

Your own name: two to six weeks, mostly waiting.

A verified local profile: a week or two to verify, then it starts appearing.

Product pages ranking for specific queries: two to four months before anything moves, longer if there's competition. Nothing in search is fast, and anyone promising fast is selling something.

That timeline is the honest argument for why search shouldn't be your first channel. If you have no orders yet, spending six weeks on titles while nothing sells is the wrong order of operations. [Getting your first ten orders](/en/blog/getting-your-first-ten-orders) comes from messaging people you know, and it works in a fortnight. Do search after that, as the thing that makes month six easier than month one.

## Two limits worth knowing before you invest

Your Sailo shop lives at `sailo.store/yourname`. That's a real page on a real domain, and it can rank, but it's a domain you don't own. Rankings you build accrue to a URL that isn't yours. For most small sellers that trade is fine, because the alternative is buying a domain and building authority on it from zero, which is slower. It's a genuine trade-off and it's worth knowing now rather than in year three.

The second limit is measurement. On the free plan, Sailo keeps 30 days of analytics. Search work pays off over months, so 30 days won't show you the shape of a trend that took four months to appear. Pro extends that to a year and Business to three years. Either way, connect a free search console account as well, because the search side of the picture lives there and not in your shop.

## The next hour

Open an incognito window and search your own shop name. Then search the thing you sell plus your town.

Whatever's wrong on that first page is your list, in priority order, and it's shorter than the article you just read. If you sell locally and you're not on the map, that's the hour to spend today. If you are, go and move the prices out of your images and into the text, one product at a time, starting with the one you sell most of.
