You wake up to fourteen messages. Four are price questions you've answered forty times, three are "hi", two are real orders, one is somebody asking whether you got their transfer at 1:06am, and one is a person who has messaged three times in nine hours and is now annoyed.

Publish a reply window and hit it. "I reply to messages twice a day, around 9am and 6pm" turns fourteen anxious people into fourteen people who know when they'll hear from you, and it costs you nothing but the discipline of actually doing it. A buyer who waits nine hours knowing it'll be nine hours is calm. A buyer who waits forty minutes not knowing is refreshing the chat.

Speed is not the thing you're selling. Predictability is.

## Why a stated window beats a fast reply

Fast replies train people to expect fast replies. That's the trap, and it closes about six weeks in.

If you answer within two minutes on a quiet Tuesday, you have told that buyer what normal is. When the same person messages during a Saturday rush and waits three hours, they don't experience three hours. They experience a two-minute service that has gone wrong, and some of them will read it as being ignored, or worse, as you having taken their money and gone quiet.

A stated window does the opposite. It sets a ceiling you can beat. Reply in twenty minutes and you look good. Reply in six hours on a bad day and you're still inside what you promised.

There's also the part nobody says out loud: you cannot answer messages at midnight forever. Sellers who try last about a year, and the failure mode isn't dramatic. They just start dreading their own notifications, and then they start replying to fewer of them, and then the shop feels abandoned to everyone messaging it.

> A reply window is a promise you can keep on your worst day, not your best one.

## Set the window you can actually hit

Pick the window from your worst realistic day, not your average one. Two rules make it stick.

**Name the times, not the duration.** "Within 24 hours" is a lawyer's answer and it tells the buyer nothing about tonight. "I check messages at 9am and 6pm" tells them exactly when to look at their phone.

**Leave a gap you can absorb.** If you can genuinely reply by 8am, say 9am. The extra hour costs you nothing and it's the difference between a normal Tuesday and one where the school run makes you late on a promise.

For most one-person shops the honest answer is two slots a day, five or six days a week, plus a named day off. Say the day off out loud. "Sundays I don't reply" is a completely acceptable sentence and it makes you look more like a business, not less.

If orders arrive in comments as well as DMs, the sorting problem is bigger than the timing problem, and the window only works once both feeds land in one queue you check at the same two times.

## The away message, word for word

Here's the WhatsApp Business away message. Set it once, under Business tools, and choose the "outside business hours" schedule rather than "always send".

> Thanks for messaging. I read everything, and I reply in two goes: mornings around 9am and evenings around 6pm, Monday to Saturday.
>
> If you're ordering, the shop link has every item, price and delivery time, and you can send the order straight through: sailo.store/yourname
>
> If you've sent a bank transfer, don't worry, I'll confirm it when I next check. Transfers sometimes take a few hours to show up on my side.
>
> If it's about a delivery happening today, put URGENT at the start and I'll see it sooner.

Four short paragraphs, and each one removes a category of anxious follow-up message. The transfer line alone will save you a dozen "did you get it?" messages a month.

The Instagram version has to be shorter, because people read it in a slim grey bubble:

> I reply mornings and evenings, Mon to Sat. Everything I've got, with prices and delivery times, is in the link in my bio. Sent a transfer? I'll confirm when I check, promise.

The email autoresponder version, if you use email at all:

> Thanks for writing. I'm a one-person shop, so I answer email once a day, usually before noon, Monday to Friday. If your message is about an order that's already on its way, reply with the order number in the subject line and I'll get to it first.

And the line that goes on the shop page itself, above or near the order button, because this is the one people read before they've messaged you at all:

> Questions? Message me any time. I reply at 9am and 6pm, Mon to Sat.

Four places, one promise, same numbers in all of them. Inconsistent hours across your bio, your away message and your shop page is worse than no hours at all.

## Answer the three questions before they're asked

Roughly three quarters of the messages waiting for you in the morning are the same three questions, and none of them need you.

1. **How much is it?**
2. **How long does delivery take, and how much?**
3. **Is it still available?**

Every one of those can be answered by the page you're already sending people to. Price on every item, a delivery line that names days rather than "fast shipping", and a sold-out state that's visible without asking. If a buyer has to message you to find out a price, you've converted a browsing customer into a message you have to answer at 6am.

This is dull work and it's the highest-value hour you'll spend on customer service all year. Write the delivery line once. Put the prices on. Then the only messages left are the ones that genuinely need a human, and there are far fewer of them than you think.

The wider version of this, everything you should nail down before someone buys, is in [setting expectations before the sale](/en/blog/setting-expectations-before-the-sale).

## The triage order for forty unread messages

When you open the phone at 9am and there are forty, don't start at the top. The top is the oldest, and the oldest is usually the least urgent.

Work in this order:

1. **Anything about a delivery happening today.** A buyer who is home now and needs a rider redirected can't be answered this evening.
2. **Money that's already moved.** Payment confirmations, double payments, short payments, refund requests. These get worse with silence, faster than anything else.
3. **Orders waiting on one answer from you.** "Do you have it in blue?" is a sale sitting still.
4. **New enquiries.**
5. **Everything else,** including the four "hi"s, which you can answer with one line each.

The "hi" messages deserve a word. Someone typing "hi" with no question is often a first-time buyer working up to something, and the reply that works is not "hi" back. It's: "Hi, morning. What are you after?" One line, ends the ping-pong, gets you to the actual question in one round instead of four.

## The messages that break the window

The window is a default, not a rule, and there are cases where waiting until 6pm is genuinely the wrong call.

- **A delivery going wrong today.** Rider at the wrong gate, buyer not home, address wrong.
- **A safety question about something you eat or put on skin.** Ingredients, allergens, whether it's safe for a child. Answer these immediately or say you'll check and come back with an hour attached.
- **A payment problem in either direction.** Charged twice, paid the wrong amount, paid the wrong person.
- **Anything already public and angry.** A comment thread doesn't hold until the evening.

That's a short list and it should stay short. Everything else can wait, including the ones that feel urgent because someone typed in capitals.

## Buyers in another time zone

If you're selling into the Gulf from South Asia, or to the US from Nigeria, or to anyone at all through a channel that doesn't care where you live, the gap between your window and their evening is where orders die.

Two things fix most of it. Put your time zone next to your hours, so "9am and 6pm" doesn't mean nine in the morning somewhere the buyer hasn't considered. And make the shop link do the selling in the hours you're asleep, because a page with prices and a working order button converts at 3am and a person doesn't.

That's the honest reason to have a proper page rather than a message-me-for-price account. Not because pages are lovely. Because a page works while you're unconscious, and your DMs don't. What that page has to prove to a stranger who found you at 3am is a separate job, and it's covered in [building trust before money moves](/en/blog/building-trust-before-money-moves).

## Worked example: Joy, skincare, Manila

Joy makes calamansi face oil and body butter in Quezon City, ₱450 a bottle, mostly bought through Instagram DMs, cash on delivery or GCash. She was doing around 70 orders a month and answering messages from 7am until she fell asleep.

She counted a week's messages once, properly. 143 messages, and 96 of them were price, delivery time or availability. She was spending, by her estimate, nine hours a week typing "₱450 po" and variations of it.

What she changed, in order:

1. Put prices and a delivery line on the shop page and stopped answering price questions in DMs at all. She sends the link instead.
2. Set an away message with two windows, 9am and 7pm, Monday to Saturday.
3. Added the transfer line, because GCash confirmations were a third of her anxious follow-ups.
4. Kept one exception: anything about a delivery happening that day.

Message volume dropped to about 60 a week. Orders didn't drop. They went up slightly, which she puts down to the page answering people at 11pm when she used to be asleep on the sofa.

The thing she noticed that isn't obvious: the complaints about slow replies stopped before the reply times improved. She was still answering at roughly the same hour. She'd just told people which hour it would be. Nobody has ever complained that she replied at 9am. They complained about not knowing whether a reply was coming at all.

One thing she'd warn about: the away message fires on the first message of a conversation, which means a returning customer who messages three days running only sees it once. If someone has been silent for a while and messages at midnight, they may get no auto-reply at all, so the hours have to live somewhere permanent too. That's why they're on the shop page as well.

## What Sailo does here, and what it doesn't

Sailo doesn't answer messages. There's no chatbot, no auto-reply, no inbox that replies for you. What it does is remove the reason for most of the messages: the catalogue, the prices, the options and the delivery information sit on a page at sailo.store/yourname, and a WhatsApp order arrives already written out with the item, the options, the address and the total, so you're reading an order rather than assembling one from six messages.

Two limits worth knowing before you build your evening around it. There's no native app, so notifications reach you the way they always did, through WhatsApp, Instagram, Telegram or email, and Sailo isn't going to wake you up. And on bank transfer, Sailo cannot tell you the money arrived. Only your bank can. Which is exactly why the away message has to say "I'll confirm when I check" rather than promising anything automatic. ## Set your away message before you go to bed

Open WhatsApp Business, go to Business tools, and set the away message with two real times in it. Copy the four-paragraph version above and change the hours to yours.

Then do the boring half tomorrow: put a price on everything and one honest delivery line on the shop page, so tomorrow night's messages are the ones that actually need you.

The first morning you open forty messages and only six of them need an answer is the morning this pays for itself. If several of them are new orders, [how to write an order confirmation](/en/blog/how-to-write-an-order-confirmation) is the next message to get right, and it's the most-read one you'll ever send.
