You've got 214 unread. You answered messages for two hours this morning and the number went up. Somewhere in there is a woman who's asked twice about a $34 candle and is now, reasonably, assuming you don't want her money.

You're not slow. You're doing work the buyer could have done for herself.

When there are too many DMs to manage, the fix is almost never in the inbox. It's upstream, in whatever is making people send the message in the first place. The diagnostic is one number: divide your total messages by your orders for a single week. Sellers who feel drowned usually land somewhere between eight and fourteen messages per order. Sellers who feel fine are at three or four. If you're at eleven, no amount of typing faster is going to save you, because you'd have to type three times as much as the person doing the same volume down the road.

## Count the messages per order before you change anything

Do this before you buy a tool, hire a VA, or start a colour-coded folder system.

Pick one normal week. Not the week of a launch. Count two numbers: how many message threads you touched, and how many orders you actually shipped. Divide.

| Messages per order | What it means | What to do about it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 to 4 | Healthy. The page is doing its job | Nothing. Go and make things |
| 5 to 7 | Something small is missing. Usually shipping cost or a size chart | One page edit, this afternoon |
| 8 to 14 | The buyer is doing discovery in your inbox | Structural. Read the rest of this |
| 15+ | You're running a helpdesk that occasionally sells | Stop taking orders in chat entirely for two weeks |

Most sellers guess low. They think they're at five and they're at ten, because the two-message exchanges are invisible in memory and the twelve-message ones are the only ones you remember.

The second number worth having is the split between people who bought and people who never did. If 70% of your messages come from people who never place an order, you don't have a customer service problem, you have a marketing problem, and the marketing is generating enquiries instead of sales.

## Four kinds of message, and only one of them needs you

Open your last fifty DMs and sort them. Every message a small seller gets is one of four things.

**"How much?"** This is not a question, it's a symptom. Every one of these means a price was not visible where the person was looking. It is the highest-volume category and the easiest to eliminate, and the fix is not a faster reply.

**"Do you have it in medium?"** Availability. Same category, same fix: it needs to be on a page, not in your head.

**"Is this real / do you actually ship / has anyone got this before?"** Trust. This one genuinely needs a human, but not necessarily you in real time. It needs your feed, your reviews, your delivery photos and your face on camera occasionally. Answered well in public, it stops being asked in private.

**"Where's my order?"** Post-purchase. Needs you, needs to be fast, and is entirely preventable with a confirmation message that says the shipping date out loud.

Only the third category deserves your Tuesday. The other three are structural, and the whole game is converting them from messages into things people can find without asking.

## Why replying faster stops working

There's a stage where the obvious answer, reply quicker, is correct. Then there's a stage where it makes things worse, and sellers don't notice they've crossed over.

The arithmetic is brutal once you look at it. Ninety messages a day at 40 seconds each is an hour of typing. But you're not typing for an hour, you're being interrupted ninety times, and every interruption costs you the thing you were doing before it. If you're also the person who makes the product, packs it, and photographs it, ninety interruptions is the whole day, and you'll finish it having produced nothing.

Then there's the trap on the other side. Fast replies train fast expectations. A buyer who gets an answer in ninety seconds on Monday sends a follow-up on Wednesday when you take forty minutes. Your responsiveness becomes the service, and the service is now unlimited and free.

So the goal isn't speed. It's fewer messages that need a reply at all.

One tactical thing before you start sorting: if your app lets you turn off read receipts, do it before you triage. The moment you open a thread just to categorise it, the buyer sees that you've seen it, and a clock starts that you never agreed to.

## The triage that buys you an hour today

This won't fix anything structurally. It clears the backlog so you can think.

Go through the unread in one pass, newest first, and put every thread in one of three piles.

1. **Will buy.** They've named a product, or asked about shipping to a specific address, or said "I'll take it". Reply to these first, individually, properly.
2. **Might buy.** Price questions, general interest, "is this still available". These get one saved reply with a link. One. If they come back with a real question they move to pile one.
3. **Never buying.** Compliments, questions about where you got your camera, people asking to collaborate, and anyone who opens with "quick question" about something that isn't your product. React with a heart and move on, or leave it. This is allowed.

Newest first, not oldest first, and this is the counterintuitive bit. The person who messaged four days ago has already bought something else or forgotten. The person who messaged eleven minutes ago is still holding their phone. Working oldest-first feels fairer and converts worse.

Then check your Requests folder. On Instagram, a DM from somebody who doesn't follow you doesn't land in your inbox at all, it lands in Requests, and for a lot of sellers that folder is full of the highest-intent strangers they've never replied to. People who saw a reel, didn't follow, and messaged anyway are buying-intent traffic. Check it weekly. Some sellers find their best month sitting in there unread.

## Saved replies, and the two worth actually writing

Instagram has quick replies. WhatsApp Business has them too, triggered by a short keyword. Both are free and both are underused, mostly because sellers write twelve of them and then can't remember the shortcuts.

Write two. Only two.

**The price reply.** Something like: "Hey! Prices, sizes and delivery are all here so you can see everything in one go: sailo.store/yourname. Shout if you want a recommendation." That last clause matters. It keeps the door open without inviting a full conversation.

**The order status reply.** "Just checked, yours goes out Friday and I'll send the tracking that evening." Fill in the specifics each time, but having the shape saved means you write it in eight seconds instead of forty.

That's it. Two replies handle most of the volume, and two is a number you'll remember under pressure.

Don't save a reply for the trust questions. Those are the ones where a real, slightly rambling human answer does the work, and a canned one reads exactly like what it is.

## The auto-reply that helps and the one that makes it worse

WhatsApp Business has an away message. Instagram has similar tools depending on your account type. Whether they help depends entirely on what's in them.

Makes it worse:

> "Thanks for your message! We'll get back to you as soon as possible."

This tells the person nothing, and it burns the one free message you get before they decide you're a dead account. Worse, it makes a real reply four hours later feel slower, because now there were two disappointments.

Actually helps:

> "Hi! I read everything and reply between 7 and 9pm. Prices, sizes and delivery are all at sailo.store/yourname if you want them right now, and you can order there any time."

Three things in that: a time, so they stop refreshing. A link, so the eager ones self-serve. And permission to order without you, which is the part sellers instinctively resist because it feels like losing the relationship.

It isn't. The people who buy without messaging you were never going to become friends. They were going to become a message you answered at 11pm.

## Turn off the tap, don't get a bigger bucket

Everything above is triage. This is the actual fix, and it happens in your posts, not your inbox.

Go and look at your last ten posts. Count how many of them contain a price. For most sellers the answer is zero or one, and every priceless post is a machine that manufactures "how much?" DMs at scale.

What to change, roughly in order of how much volume it removes:

- **Put the price in the caption.** Not "DM for price". The actual number. This is the single biggest lever and the one sellers resist hardest.
- **Put the shipping cost somewhere findable.** "Ships anywhere in the US, $6 flat" ends an entire category of message.
- **Say what's in stock and what isn't.** A pinned story or a highlight called "In stock" costs you four minutes a week.
- **Answer the same question publicly, once.** If three people ask whether the candle is soy wax, the answer belongs in the next caption, not in three DMs.
- **Make the bio link land on something with prices on it.** If it lands on a menu of buttons, every visitor has to make a decision before they see a number, and some of them will ask you instead.

Sellers hide prices because the conversation feels like a relationship, and it isn't, it's a queue. What to put on that page and in what order is worked through in [what to put in your bio link](/en/blog/what-to-put-in-your-bio-link), and the constraint that makes Instagram specifically hard, that it can't prefill a message the way WhatsApp can, is in [turning Instagram DMs into orders](/en/blog/turning-instagram-dms-into-orders).

## Marisol's week after a reel did 380,000 views

Marisol pours candles in San Diego. Two sizes, $34 and $52, flat-rate shipping at $7, and she'd been doing a steady 25 to 30 orders a month for about a year.

A reel of her pouring a batch did 380,000 views over four days. She got roughly 210 DMs in a week, up from her usual 40 or so.

Her first instinct was to answer all of them. She spent Saturday doing it, got through about 130, and shipped nothing. On Sunday the count was back up to 180 because half of Saturday's replies had generated a follow-up question.

By her own count, 140 of those 210 messages were some version of "how much" or "where can I buy". Her caption on the reel had no price and her bio link went to a link-list page with five buttons on it.

What she did on Monday:

1. Edited the reel's caption to lead with "$34 for the 8oz, $52 for the 12oz, $7 shipping, link in bio".
2. Pinned a comment on the reel with the same information and the plain link.
3. Changed the bio link to a page that opens on products with prices, no menu.
4. Wrote one quick reply with the link and used it on everything in the might-buy pile.
5. Left about 60 messages permanently unanswered, which she says was the hardest part and the right call.

The following week's DMs were around 70, on similar traffic. Orders went from her usual 7 a week to 31 over the two weeks of the spike. She's fairly sure she lost some sales in the chaos of the first weekend and she has no way to know how many.

The lesson she repeats: the reel didn't create the problem. The reel just found the hole that was always in her setup, and made 380,000 people walk past it.

## When to hire, and what you can actually hand over

At some volume a person is the answer. But hiring first, before the structural fix, reliably fails, and the reason is worth understanding.

The knowledge is in your head. Whether the lavender one is back in stock, whether you'd post to Canada, whether a wholesale enquiry is worth taking. Someone answering your DMs without that context either has to ask you every time, which costs you more than doing it yourself, or guesses, which costs you customers.

So the order is: fix the structure, write the answers down, then hire.

What genuinely hands over well:

- Order status replies, if your orders live somewhere they can look at.
- Shipping questions, given a written policy.
- Trust questions, given three or four real answers they can adapt.

What doesn't hand over: anything involving a price you'd bend, a custom order, or a complaint. Those stay yours for a lot longer than you'd like.

## What Sailo won't do about your inbox

Being direct, because this is the article where it matters most.

Sailo has no inbox. It does not connect to Instagram DMs, it does not read your WhatsApp, it does not auto-reply to anything, and there is no unified message view. If you were hoping for a tool that answers your DMs, this isn't it and no article here is going to pretend otherwise.

What it does is remove the reason for the message. A page with prices, options, delivery costs and stock on it turns four categories of DM into one, and the one that's left is the one that deserves a human. When someone does order, the order arrives written out rather than assembled from nine messages. That's the mechanism, and it's the same mechanism described in [selling on social media without a website](/en/blog/selling-on-social-media-without-a-website).

It's also worth knowing the manual side stays manual. On bank transfer or cash on delivery, you're still the one confirming the money arrived and marking the order paid. Sailo can't see your bank. So it reduces your message count, not your admin to zero.

And there's no app. Everything happens in a browser, which for a phone-first seller is a real thing to know before you build a workflow on it.

## Do the count first

Don't reorganise anything until you have the number.

1. This week, count every thread you touch and every order you ship. Divide.
2. Look at your last ten posts and count how many show a price.
3. Open your Requests folder. Reply to anything in there from the last two weeks.
4. Write two saved replies. The price one and the order-status one.
5. Put a price in your next caption. Just one caption, as an experiment.

Then count again the following week. If messages per order dropped and orders didn't, you've bought back hours for free. If orders dropped too, you've learned something real about your buyers and you can put the price back. Either way you'll know, which is more than you know right now.
