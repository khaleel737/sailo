"I'll send ₦20,000 now and give the rider the rest." You've had that message. If you sell anything over about ₦15,000 in Nigeria you get it every week, and the honest answer is usually yes.

The rule is one line: the part you take up front has to cover everything you can't get back if that parcel comes home. Materials, packaging, and the delivery fee you'll pay whether or not the buyer opens their door. Above that line, split it however the customer wants. Below it, you're funding a stranger's shopping trip.

The money side of this is easy. The part that actually breaks is the record. One order, two payments, two different rails, two different days, and by Thursday you genuinely cannot remember whether the ₦20,000 in your statement was Chioma's balance or Blessing's deposit.

## The four splits you'll actually be asked for

They're not the same problem and they don't get the same answer.

**Transfer now, cash to the rider.** The big one. The buyer is nervous about sending a stranger ₦42,000 and comfortable sending ₦20,000. Reasonable of them. This is the split worth saying yes to, because it converts an order you'd otherwise lose and it halves your exposure to a refused parcel.

**Two transfers, because their app won't send it all at once.** Nigerian banking apps carry a daily transfer limit, and a lot of people have never touched theirs since the day they opened the account. Your buyer isn't stalling. Their app has genuinely refused, and they'll send the balance tomorrow at 00:01. Recognising this one saves you an argument you were about to start.

**Someone else paying part of it.** A husband, a mother, an employer doing a staff order. Two names, two account numbers, one parcel. Harmless as long as you write down who's sending what before it happens, and a nightmare if you don't.

**Cash in hand plus a transfer to top it up.** In-person sales where the buyer has ₦15,000 in notes and the item is ₦18,500. Instant, fine, and the transfer half still needs a reference or it's invisible to you on Sunday.

The one to be careful with is a fifth shape that isn't really a split at all: "I'll pay ₦5,000 now to hold it and the rest next month." That's credit. You may choose to give it, to a customer you know, on something you'd otherwise not sell. Just be clear with yourself that you're lending money, not taking payment.

## How much goes up front

Work from the floor up, not from the percentage down.

Your floor is the money that's gone regardless of what the buyer does next. In Nigeria that's usually three things: what the goods cost you, what packaging costs, and the outbound delivery fee, because a dispatch rider who takes a parcel to Ikeja and brings it back charges you for both journeys.

If your ₦42,000 hair bundle set cost you ₦26,000, packaging ₦800, and delivery ₦3,500, then the absolute minimum up front is ₦30,300. Round it to ₦30,000 and that's your number for that product forever. Not 50%, not "whatever they can manage". A figure.

| Order shape | Sensible up front | The reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stock item, buyer within your city | 40–50% | Refusal risk is real but the goods resell |
| Stock item, buyer in another state | Cost price plus delivery, minimum | Two long-distance journeys if it's refused |
| Made to order or personalised | 100% | Nobody else will buy it |
| Repeat customer, third order or more | Whatever they ask | You've priced the risk already and they've earned it |
| First-time buyer, high value | Full payment, or don't ship | The largest orders from strangers are the ones that go wrong |

That last row is the one people argue with, and it's the one that's right. The ₦180,000 order from an account with eleven followers, who wants it sent to an address three states away and only wants to pay ₦20,000 up front, is not your big break.

Two exceptions worth naming. If the buyer is standing in front of you, none of this applies. And if you're sending to a customer you've delivered to four times, stop calculating and just say yes, because the goodwill is worth more than the arithmetic.

## The real problem is the record, not the money

This is the failure mode that costs actual parcels. You agree a split with a buyer in a DM on Monday. The ₦20,000 lands on Tuesday. On Wednesday your rider collects six parcels, and the one for Chioma is supposed to have ₦22,000 collected on delivery. But your order list says the order is `pending`, your DMs have scrolled, and you tell the rider to collect ₦42,000 because that's the price on the parcel.

Now the rider is at a gate in Bodija arguing with a customer who has already paid you half, and you're the one who looks dishonest.

The fix is a discipline, not a tool. Every split order gets three things written down in the same place, at the moment you agree it:

1. **The total.**
2. **The amount paid, and on which rail.**
3. **The amount outstanding, and how it's being collected.**

Written where you'll see it when you're packing, not where you agreed it. A DM is not a record. The single most useful habit is putting the balance on the parcel itself, in marker, on the day you pack it: `BAL ₦22,000`. Your rider reads it. Your buyer sees a number that matches what they expected. Nobody has to trust anybody's memory.

> The parcel is the only document that both you and your rider are guaranteed to look at. Write on it.

## Reference discipline for split payments

If you already give orders a reference like `SL1042`, extend it rather than inventing a new scheme.

- `SL1042A` for the first payment.
- `SL1042B` for the balance.

Two credits in your statement, two labels, and matching takes four seconds instead of four minutes. Tell the buyer the exact reference and the exact figure in the same message, because "send the balance" gets sent as a round number and "send ₦22,000 with reference SL1042B" gets sent correctly.

When the second half arrives short, and it will, don't improvise. Have a rule and apply it to everyone the same way. Short by less than a hundred naira is a fee or a rounding, ship it and move on. Short by a real amount is a conversation, and there's a full decision tree for that in [what to do about a partial payment](/en/blog/what-to-do-about-a-partial-payment).

## The wording that makes it stick

Agree the split in one message that contains all four numbers. Don't spread it across a conversation.

> Total is ₦42,000 including delivery to Bodija. You can send ₦20,000 now to book it and pay the rider ₦22,000 in cash when he arrives. Reference for the transfer is SL1042A. I'll write ₦22,000 on the parcel so there's no confusion at the gate. Rider comes Thursday between 10 and 2.

Five sentences, and it has removed every argument that this order could have produced. The buyer knows the balance. The rider will know the balance. The reference is stated. The delivery window is stated. You've said "in cash", which means nobody turns up expecting to transfer to a rider's personal account.

Then confirm the first payment only when you've seen it in your own banking app. Not when they say they've sent it, and not from a screenshot. The moment you make an exception to that is the moment somebody finds out you make exceptions.

## Where split payments actually go wrong

**The balance never comes.** The parcel arrives, the buyer isn't home, their sister collects it, and the ₦22,000 evaporates. Prevent it by making the rider's instruction unambiguous: collect the balance or bring it back. No partial handovers, no "she said she'd send it later". Riders will do what you tell them and will default to keeping the customer happy if you tell them nothing.

**Two orders collapse into one.** Two customers, same product, same amount, both paying half. Your statement shows two ₦20,000 credits and neither has a reference. This is why the reference scheme exists, and it's why odd totals are better than round ones.

**The rider remits late.** The cash reaches the rider on Thursday and reaches you the following Wednesday. That's a normal COD lag and not theft, but it means your split order isn't really finished until the remittance lands. Track the rider's payout as its own thing, which is a whole discipline covered in [rider handovers and cash reconciliation](/en/blog/rider-handovers-and-cash-reconciliation).

**The buyer treats the first payment as the price.** They sent ₦20,000, the parcel came, and in their memory that's what it cost. This is rare and it's almost always genuine confusion rather than fraud, and it's completely prevented by writing the balance on the parcel.

**You forget which half you're owed.** By far the most common one. It's not dramatic and it doesn't produce a story, it just quietly loses you ₦22,000 a month.

## What to do when the balance doesn't arrive

Give it 48 hours before you say anything, unless the parcel is about to move. Balances land late for the same boring reasons first payments do.

Then send the amount, not a feeling: "Hi Chioma, still showing ₦22,000 outstanding on SL1042. Same account, reference SL1042B." No question mark, no apology, no essay. A figure and a reference is easy to act on. "Just checking in about the balance" is easy to ignore.

If it's still outstanding at a week, decide what this customer is worth and act once. Either write it off and stop selling to them on split terms, or ask directly and accept whichever answer you get. What you must not do is chase it every three days for a month, because that costs you more in attention than ₦22,000 and it makes you feel like a debt collector rather than a seller.

Whatever you decide, put that customer on full-payment-only terms afterwards, quietly, without announcing it. "I'm taking full payment on this one" is a complete sentence.

## Worked example: Tunde, sneakers, Ibadan

Tunde resells sneakers from Ring Road. Popular pairs go for ₦42,000 to ₦65,000, he does maybe twenty-five orders a month, and almost every buyer finds him through Instagram and pays by transfer.

He used to offer one thing: full payment before dispatch. It cost him orders. Buyers in Abuja and Port Harcourt didn't want to send ₦52,000 to a person they'd never met, and he'd watch a conversation die at exactly that sentence.

He moved to a split with a fixed floor. Anything under ₦30,000, full payment. Anything above it, ₦25,000 up front and the balance to the rider, except for buyers outside Oyo State, where the up-front amount is his cost price plus the courier fee, usually around ₦38,000 on a ₦52,000 pair.

His conversion on out-of-state enquiries went up noticeably. Two other things happened that he hadn't planned for.

The first is that his refusals fell close to zero. A buyer who has sent ₦25,000 answers their phone. He hasn't had a parcel come back from a split order in eight months, against roughly one a month on the full-COD orders he used to accept.

The second is the trick worth stealing. He writes the balance on the parcel in marker, and he sends the buyer a photo of the packed, labelled parcel with `BAL ₦27,000` visible on it. It confirms the order is real, it confirms the balance without a second message, and it has ended the "I thought that was the full price" conversation permanently. One photo, sent while he's taping the box, costs him nothing.

He still won't split a first order over ₦100,000. He learned that one at a cost of ₦74,000 and a courier receipt to Kaduna.

## What Sailo does here, and what it doesn't

There's no split payment feature. No part-payment field, no "deposit plus balance" option, no way to record that ₦20,000 of ₦42,000 has landed. An order is `unpaid`, `pending`, `paid` or `refunded`, and there's nothing between those. That's the honest limitation and it's worth knowing before you build a process around a feature that doesn't exist.

What you can do:

- **Put the split rule in the bank transfer instructions box.** It's free text, the buyer reads it at the point of paying, and it's the right place for "send ₦25,000 now, balance to the rider in cash".
- **Leave the order `pending` until the whole thing is settled**, then mark it `paid` once. One order, one status change, no half-states to misread.
- **Use the delivery notes field on cash on delivery** for the same purpose if the balance is being collected at the door.

Sailo charges nothing on bank transfer and nothing on cash on delivery, because it never touches the money on either. Which is also precisely why it can't tell you the first ₦25,000 arrived. Only your bank can do that.

If most of your money is arriving at the door rather than before it, the wider set of trade-offs is in [cash on delivery for small sellers](/en/blog/cash-on-delivery-for-small-sellers), and if you're weighing this rail against cards and chat orders generally, start from [how to take payment as a small seller](/en/blog/how-to-take-payment-as-a-small-seller). If what you actually want is a booking fee rather than a split, [taking a deposit before you start](/en/blog/taking-a-deposit-before-you-start) is the closer fit.

## Set your floor today

Take your three best-selling items and work out, for each one, what you've spent by the time the parcel leaves your hands. Goods, packaging, delivery. That number is your minimum up-front payment, and it doesn't change based on how nice the customer sounds.

Write those three numbers somewhere you can see them while you're replying to DMs. Then put the split wording into your bank transfer instructions, with the reference format spelled out, so the next person who asks to pay half gets a straight answer in one message instead of a negotiation.

And buy a marker. Write the balance on the box.
