You don't make anything. You don't have stock, a workshop, a supplier or a garage full of boxes. Everyone selling online seems to have a thing, and you have opinions and a phone.

You can sell within a week, and the fastest thing to sell is an hour of your attention applied to somebody else's problem. Not a PDF. Not a course. An hour, at a real price, delivered on a video call, booked through a link. A 45-minute session at KSh 1,500 can be sold on Tuesday and delivered on Thursday. A KSh 600 ebook takes four months to sell the same money, and the ebook feels safer, which is precisely why most people choose it and then quit.

That's the whole answer. The rest of this is the list, in order, and the specific traps that eat the most months.

## You already own inventory, it just isn't in boxes

Stop thinking about products and start listing assets. You have more than you think.

**Your time**, applied to a specific problem. This is the biggest and most under-priced asset any small creator has.

**Your process.** The order you do things in, the settings you use, the shortcuts you found the hard way. You think it's obvious because you've done it 400 times.

**Your judgement.** Which of the nine options is worth buying. Which supplier is reliable. Which of these four photos to post. People pay for a decision that saves them a week.

**Your access.** Introductions, a group, a room where people can ask questions and get answers from someone competent.

**Your labour, made to order.** You don't need stock if you make each one after it's paid for.

**Somebody else's product**, sold with your recommendation attached.

Six categories. Five of them require nothing but a link and a way to be paid.

## Ranked by how fast each one can produce a first sale

| What you sell | Time to first sale | What it needs first | The catch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A one-to-one session | Days | A price and a calendar | Your time is capped. So is your income |
| An audit or review of their thing | Days | A clear scope and a turnaround promise | Scope creep. Write down what's excluded |
| A custom or made-to-order job | 1 to 2 weeks | Examples people can see | Quoting badly. Set one price, not a range |
| A paid group or Q&A | 2 to 4 weeks | Enough people to make it feel busy | It only dies once, and then loudly |
| A template, checklist or file | 1 to 3 months | The file itself, and traffic | Nobody wants a file from a stranger |
| Affiliate or reselling | 1 to 3 months | Trust, and buyers who already spend | You don't own the customer, or the terms |

Read the top two rows and the bottom two rows against each other. The fast ones require you to show up. The slow ones require an audience that already trusts you. If you had that audience, you'd know it, because people would already be asking you to do things.

## Sell your time before you sell your files

There's a reason every experienced person says this and every beginner ignores it.

Selling an hour requires no production. You already know the thing. You show up, you help, you get paid, and the entire cost of goods is one hour you were going to spend scrolling. If nobody buys, you've lost an afternoon writing a sentence, not six weekends building something.

Selling a file requires you to guess what people want before anyone has paid you to find out. You'll guess wrong, because everyone does, and you'll find out four weekends in.

And there's a second thing that only shows up after you've done it: the calls teach you what the file should contain. Six paid sessions and you'll have heard the same three questions eighteen times, in the buyer's own words, with their own examples. That's a product brief you could not have written on your own, and now you know that people will pay for it, because six people just did.

> The file you write after ten paid calls sells. The file you write instead of ten paid calls does not.

## Start from what people already ask you to do

Open your messages and go back three months. Look for requests, not compliments.

"Can you look at mine and tell me what's wrong." "Which one should I buy." "How do you do that bit." "Would you have a look at my page." "Do you do this for other people."

Each of those is a product with the price missing. The person has already decided they want your help. The only thing standing between that message and a payment is that you replied "sure, send it over" instead of "yes, I do that, it's ₹2,000 and takes two days."

If you've been doing free favours for a year, you have proof of demand and no revenue. That's the most common shape of a stuck creator account, and the fix is a sentence.

The method for spotting these signals systematically, including which kinds of engagement mean nothing, is in [turning free content into a paid offer](/en/blog/turning-free-content-into-a-paid-offer).

## What to sell if nobody has ever asked you anything

Then you're guessing, and the trick is to guess cheaply.

**Pick the narrowest possible problem.** Not "social media help". "I'll rewrite your Instagram bio and pin the right three posts, KSh 1,200, 24 hours." Narrow is easier to say yes to and easier to deliver well.

**Pick something you can do in under 90 minutes.** Your first paid job should not risk your weekend.

**Pick something with a visible before and after.** So the buyer can tell it worked, and so you have something to show the next person.

**Then announce it once, plainly, before you build anything.** If two people ask how to pay, you have a business. If nobody does, you've spent one post. Changing the offer is cheap. Changing a finished product is not.

That last step is the one people skip, and it's the cheapest hour in the whole process. An offer you've announced and nobody wanted has cost you a Tuesday. A product you've built and nobody wanted has cost you six weekends and most of your nerve.

## Reselling and affiliate links: what they're actually good for

You can sell things other people made. Three versions, with different economics.

**Affiliate.** You recommend, they buy elsewhere, you get a cut later. Good if your audience already spends money on things in your niche and you're genuinely opinionated. Bad as a first income, because you need real traffic and the cooling-off periods mean money lands months after the work.

**Buying and reselling.** You buy stock, mark it up, sell it. This is a real business with real inventory risk, which is exactly the thing you were trying to avoid. It's a fine business, it just isn't a no-product business.

**Made-to-order with a supplier.** Print on demand and similar. Nothing gets made until someone pays, so the risk is low, but so are the margins, and turnaround times are somebody else's promise to keep. Treat it as a side dish. It rarely carries a small account on its own.

None of these is a scam. All three are slower than selling an hour of your time, and all three hand the customer relationship to someone else.

## Worked example: Wanjiru, Nairobi

Wanjiru posts about growing vegetables in small spaces. Buckets on a balcony, sukuma wiki in old paint tins, that kind of thing. About 2,300 followers, mostly in Nairobi and Mombasa, mostly people in flats.

She had no product. She'd been asked to "come and look at my balcony" four separate times and had said yes twice, for free, and once spent a Saturday morning on it.

What she sells now, in the order she added it:

1. **A 45-minute video call, KSh 1,500.** The buyer shows her their balcony on camera, she tells them what will grow there, what won't, and what to buy this week. Sold four in the first fortnight.
2. **A written plan, KSh 2,500.** Same thing, but she sends a one-page plan afterwards. Added after three separate buyers asked her to "write that down".
3. **A starter pack, KSh 900.** Seeds and a printed page, posted or collected. Physical, but she buys the seeds after the order, so no stock.

First month, calls only: six sales, KSh 9,000. Third month, all three: KSh 21,400. Her costs are her time and about KSh 300 per starter pack.

She takes payment on M-Pesa. Sailo has no mobile money rail, so what she does is put her till number and the exact instructions in the bank transfer field, and confirm each payment herself against the SMS. Her buyers see the registered business name before they confirm, which is a trust signal a bank account number can't give her. The catalogue, the offer page and the order record sit in Sailo. The money sits in her phone. That split is worth understanding before you set anything up, and the full picture of which rails do what is in [how to take payment as a small seller](/en/blog/how-to-take-payment-as-a-small-seller).

The thing she'd tell you: the KSh 900 seed pack, which is the only item that looks like a real product, is her worst performer per hour worked. The KSh 2,500 written plan takes her 70 minutes and requires her to buy nothing.

## The four ideas that waste the most months

**Buying a "done for you" digital product bundle with resale rights.** These are sold hard to people in exactly your position. The maths is against you: hundreds of other people bought the identical files, they're competing on the same platforms, and the only person reliably making money is the one who sold you the bundle. If you want to sell a file, write one.

**Dropshipping trending gadgets.** Long shipping times, no control over quality, refund requests you can't service, and an audience that followed you for something else entirely. It's a paid-ads business in a costume.

**A course as your first product.** A course is the hardest possible first product: expensive to make, hard to price, needs proof you don't have yet, and takes months during which you learn nothing. If you eventually want one, run the live version six times first. There's a whole piece on doing that without buying a platform in [selling a course without a course platform](/en/blog/selling-a-course-without-a-course-platform), and the honest recommendation there is still to start small.

**An app.** Ask how much of your next six months you want to spend not selling.

## What "no product" costs you at the delivery end

Selling time and files instead of objects removes the warehouse and adds two problems.

**Your income has a ceiling made of hours.** Eight calls a week at KSh 1,500 is KSh 48,000 a month and you cannot get to KSh 96,000 by working harder, only by charging more or selling something that isn't your time. Plan for that around month four, not month twelve.

**Digital delivery on a manual payment rail isn't instant.** On Sailo, a digital file unlocks when the payment is confirmed. If the buyer paid by card, that's automatic. If they paid by bank transfer or M-Pesa or GCash, it unlocks when you mark the order paid, and you're asleep at 2am when they buy. That's not a bug, it's what "the platform never touches the money" means in practice, but it does mean a buyer can pay at midnight and wait until morning. Say so on the page. "Files are sent within 12 hours" is a promise you can keep. Silence is a complaint waiting to happen.

Card payments would close that gap, and they need the Business plan at $19.99 a month plus a Stripe account cleared for charges, with Sailo taking 0.5% of the goods. At six orders a month that's an expensive way to buy eight hours of sleep. At sixty it's obvious. Do the arithmetic before you upgrade.

## Turning the first sale into the second

The first sale is a fact. The second is a business.

After every paid job, do three things the same day. Ask what nearly stopped them from booking. Ask for one sentence you can quote. Ask if they want the next thing, whatever the next thing is, even if you have to invent it on the spot.

The answer to the first question is your marketing copy. The second is your proof. The third is your product roadmap, and it's free.

Keep a note of every question you get asked during delivery. After ten jobs, the three questions that came up every single time are your first file, and you'll be writing it for people you've already met.

## What to do this week

Write down the last three things somebody asked you to do for free. Pick the one that takes you under 90 minutes and that you didn't resent.

Give it a price, a turnaround and a one-sentence scope. Post it once, without a build-up, with a way to reply. Then go and set up somewhere for the money to land, using whatever your buyers already pay with.
