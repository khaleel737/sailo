You sent the price four days ago. They read it, replied "okay let me check", and vanished. You've typed a follow-up six times and deleted it six times, because every version reads like begging.

Send two follow-ups. One at 48 hours, one about a week later. Each has to carry something the buyer didn't already know, not just a reminder that you're waiting. Then send a third message that closes the conversation politely and lets them say no. Then stop.

That's the shape. Three messages, spread over roughly ten days, and the third one is the one that gets replies. Most sellers send four nudges in five days and then go silent forever, which is exactly backwards.

## Why a follow-up feels rude

It's not the follow-up. It's the emptiness.

"Just checking in!" contains no information. It's a request for the buyer to do emotional labour on your behalf: to explain themselves, or to feel bad, or to make a decision they weren't ready to make. That's why it feels rude to send, and it's why it feels irritating to receive.

Now compare: "The blue one's the last of that batch, I'll hold it till Friday if you want it." That's not a nudge. That's news. It gives the buyer a reason to reply that isn't guilt, and it gives them a deadline that came from the world rather than from your anxiety.

So the rule underneath everything else: **a follow-up without new information is a nudge, and nudges are what annoy people.**

New information can be small. Genuinely small. Any of these will do:

- Stock changed. One left, or a restock arrived.
- A price or delivery cost you didn't mention.
- A photo you didn't have before, especially a customer's.
- A related option. "There's a smaller one at ₹850 if the big one's too much."
- An answer to a question they didn't ask but probably had.
- A date. "I'm posting Thursday, so Wednesday's the cut-off."

If you cannot find one true new thing to say, you probably shouldn't send the message yet.

## The timings

**48 hours.** Long enough that they've had a life, short enough that they remember the conversation. Under 24 hours reads as pressure. Over four days and you're reintroducing yourself.

**Five to seven days after that.** One week is the sweet spot for the second. By now, if they were going to buy on impulse, they didn't, so this message should be lower pressure and carry something more substantial. A photo, a restock, a different option.

**Then the closing message, a few days later.** This is the one that works.

After that, nothing, unless something genuinely changes. A restock in two months is a legitimate reason to message. "Still thinking about it?" is not.

Do not send follow-ups between about 9pm and 8am. Not because it's rude. Because the message gets read in bed, mentally filed as handled, and then never actually handled. A message read at 11pm and not replied to is close to dead. Send between about 10am and 6pm and you get a reply rate you can live with.

## The closing message

This is the highest-return message in the whole sequence and almost nobody sends it.

> No worries at all if you've gone off the idea, honestly. I'll take it off my list so I'm not pestering you. If you change your mind later just message, it'll still be here.

It works because it asks for permission to stop rather than permission to continue. You've removed the obligation, which is the thing that was keeping them silent. A large share of the people who never replied to two follow-ups reply to this one, and a decent number of those replies are orders.

The ones that aren't orders are still useful. You get real reasons: too expensive, bought elsewhere, waiting on payday, forgot entirely. Payday is the most common one in a lot of markets and it's not a no, it's a date.

Write down whatever they tell you. Ten of those answers is a better explanation of your conversion problem than any dashboard, which is most of the argument in [finding out why people abandon](/en/blog/finding-out-why-people-abandon).

## Four kinds of silence, and what each one needs

They are not the same and they don't get the same message.

### They asked the price and went quiet

The most common. Usually the price was more than they hoped, or the delivery cost was a surprise, or they were browsing.

Follow-up at 48 hours, with a real option attached:

> Hi Meera, no rush on this. The 20cm one is ₹1,450 plus ₹120 delivery to Delhi. There's a 14cm at ₹850 if that suits better. Either's fine, just say which.

You've reframed the decision from yes/no to which, and you've named the delivery cost that they were probably doing arithmetic about.

### They said they'd pay and didn't

This is a different thing and it needs to be handled faster, because you may be holding stock.

Twelve to twenty-four hours, and it should reference the hold rather than the money:

> Hi Rohan, still holding the two lamps for you. Let me know either way by tomorrow evening and I'll release them if it's not happening. No problem at all if it isn't.

Naming the hold makes the message about your inventory rather than about their wallet, which is much easier to receive. And it has to be true. If you say you'll release it, release it.

If money is genuinely in dispute rather than late, that's a separate conversation with its own wording, and [what to say when a buyer says they paid](/en/blog/what-to-say-when-a-buyer-says-they-paid) covers the awkward version of it.

### They bought once, months ago

Not really a follow-up. A re-approach, and the rules are different because you have a relationship.

The trigger should be about them, not your calendar. If you sell something consumable, work out roughly how long it lasts and message a couple of weeks before it runs out. A lamp doesn't run out. Body oil does.

> Hi Meera, you got the sandalwood one back in April. Reckon you're about due. New batch just finished, want me to put one aside?

Notice there's no discount in it. A discount here trains people to wait for one, and the whole apparatus of getting people to come back a second time is covered properly in [how to get repeat buyers](/en/blog/how-to-get-repeat-buyers).

### They complained, you fixed it, silence

Follow up once, a week later, and do not sell anything.

> Hi Rohan, just wanted to check the replacement arrived okay and it's all sorted. Nothing needed from you, only checking.

The last four words are the whole message. A customer who has been through a problem is braced for a sales pitch, and not delivering one is worth more than any offer you could make.

## Anjali's lamps, in Jaipur

Anjali makes brass and glass table lamps in Jaipur. ₹1,450 for the 20cm, ₹850 for the 14cm, ₹120 delivery within Rajasthan and ₹250 elsewhere in India. She takes orders on Instagram and WhatsApp, mostly paid by UPI, some cash on delivery.

She used to send one follow-up, and it was always the same: "Hi, any update?" Her own count over one month was 31 people who asked the price and went quiet, 4 who eventually bought.

She changed to the three-message sequence in February. Same month the following year, roughly the same enquiry volume:

| Stage | People | Bought |
|---|---|---|
| Asked price, went quiet | 34 | 0 |
| After 48-hour message with the ₹850 option | 34 | 6 |
| After one-week message with a customer photo | 28 | 3 |
| After the closing message | 25 | 4 |
| Replied but didn't buy | | 9 |

Thirteen orders instead of four. Four of them came from the message that gave people permission to leave, which she nearly didn't send because it felt like giving up.

The nine who replied without buying told her things she'd never have found otherwise. Six said the ₹250 delivery outside Rajasthan was the problem, not the lamp. She now quotes delivery in the first message rather than the third, and the number of people who go quiet at all has dropped.

That's the ugly detail worth taking. Her follow-up sequence worked, and then it told her that half her follow-ups shouldn't have been necessary. The best follow-up is a first message that answers the question that causes the silence, and [what a good first message looks like](/en/blog/what-a-good-first-message-looks-like) is where to fix that.

## Channel manners

**WhatsApp** is personal space. Two follow-ups is the ceiling and voice notes are a bad idea for a follow-up, because they demand the recipient find a private moment. Type it.

**Instagram DMs** are noisier and more forgiving, but if the person doesn't follow you your message may be sitting in requests unread. Before you decide they're ignoring you, check whether the message was ever delivered.

**Email** tolerates more follow-ups than either, and gets read less. If someone gave you an email address for an order, use it for order things and not for chasing.

Don't chase across channels. Messaging on WhatsApp, then Instagram, then commenting on their post is three messages that feel like thirty. Pick the channel they used and stay in it.

And turn off your own read receipts anxiety. Whether they've read it is not information you can act on. Sellers lose hours to this.

## Chasing money is a different job

Everything above is about a sale that hasn't happened. Chasing a payment for a sale that has is a separate thing, and softening it the same way makes it worse.

Three situations, three tones.

**A deposit that hasn't landed.** You haven't started work. Say that, plainly, once: "I've got you in for the 14th, I'll start once the ₹700 deposit's through. No rush, but the slot's only held till Friday." The information doing the work is the slot, not the money.

**Cash on delivery that failed.** The rider went, nobody was in, and you've paid for a trip. This needs to be same-day and it needs a decision attached: "The rider couldn't reach anyone at 2pm. Happy to try again tomorrow, there's a ₹120 second-attempt charge from them. Or I can refund and cancel, whichever's easier."

**A bank transfer or UPI payment that's short or missing.** Ask once, without accusation, and give the exact number. "I've got ₹1,330 rather than ₹1,450, I think your bank might have taken a charge. Can you check your end?" Then wait a day before the second message.

The rule for all three: one message, one clear next step, a date. Not three vague reminders. Vagueness in a money conversation reads as either weakness or passive aggression, and both make it harder to collect.

## When not to follow up at all

Some silences should stay silent.

- They said no. A no is a complete answer and following up on it is the fastest way to lose a future customer.
- They asked a question, you answered, and the answer was clearly a dealbreaker. "Do you ship to the UK?" "No." There's nothing to follow up.
- You're following up because you need money this week. They can hear it, and it changes the tone of every message you write.
- It's a wholesale or corporate enquiry that went quiet in December. Wait for January.

## What no follow-up system will do for you

Sailo won't chase anybody on your behalf. There's no automatic sequence that messages a buyer who didn't pay, and on the manual rails, bank transfer, cash on delivery, WhatsApp and the rest, you are the one who checks whether the money arrived and you are the one who sends the reminder. That's a real cost in your week and it's worth being honest about before you build a process that assumes otherwise.

The related limit is memory. Sailo's free plan keeps 30 days of analytics, so an enquiry from six weeks ago isn't going to be sitting in a report waiting for you. Pro takes that to a year, Business to three. Whichever you're on, the follow-up list has to live somewhere you'll actually look, and for most sellers that's a note on the phone with four names in it, not a system.

## Set the two reminders now

Open the last conversation that went quiet. Work out one true new thing you can tell that person, and send it.

Then set two alarms for yourself on the next enquiry that goes quiet: one at 48 hours, one at a week. And write the closing message once, save it somewhere you can paste from, and commit to sending it on day ten every single time. It's the message you'll least want to send and the one that'll bring back the most orders.
