Your Tuesday afternoon post gets nothing. Same photo, same caption style, same hashtags that worked in February, and it sits there at eleven likes while you refresh it.

Nothing is broken. Ramadan doesn't reduce demand in the Gulf, it moves it, and if your posting and your packing still run on a nine-to-six day you're selling into the emptiest hours of the month. The buying happens after iftar. A home baker in Dubai selling an AED 120 kunafa tray will take more orders between 9pm and 1am during Ramadan than she does across an entire pre-Ramadan Saturday, and the same shift shows up whether you're selling perfume oils, abayas, gift boxes or dates.

So the plan is: move your hours, prepare everything you can before the month starts, and treat the last ten days as a completely different business from the first twenty.

## The month doesn't reduce demand, it moves it

Two things change at once. Working hours shorten, and social hours lengthen.

In the UAE, private-sector working hours are cut during Ramadan by law, and the exact reduction is announced each year, so check the current circular rather than assuming last year's. The effect is the same either way: people leave work earlier, go home, sleep, break the fast, and then the evening starts. That evening runs long. Shopping malls in Dubai and Riyadh stay open past midnight through the month, and phone screens are busiest in the same hours.

The practical consequence for a small seller is unglamorous. Your best posting window is not a fixed clock time. Iftar moves by a minute or two every day as the month progresses, so the hour your shop gets busy on day two is not the hour it gets busy on day twenty-six. If you schedule content once at the start of the month and never touch it, by the last week you're posting into the wrong hour.

## The four windows of a Ramadan day

There are four moments worth knowing, and only two of them are worth selling in.

**Late morning to mid afternoon.** Quiet. Low energy, low patience, and the worst possible time to push a food photo at someone who has been fasting for eight hours. Use it for admin: packing, replies, printing labels, updating the shop.

**The hour before iftar.** Traffic is terrible, everyone is heading home, and nobody is buying. Couriers effectively stop. Do not promise a delivery in this window and do not expect a rider to take a pickup.

**After iftar, roughly an hour to three hours later.** This is the window. People have eaten, they're on their phones, they're planning the next few days. Posts land, DMs get answered, and orders get placed. If you post once a day during Ramadan, post here.

**Late night, from around 10pm to 1am.** The second window, and for gifting it's often the stronger one. Orders placed at 12:40am are not a fluke or a mis-click. They're the most reliable hour of your month.

If you sell food, there's a fifth: the pre-dawn suhoor window. Small, but real, and almost nobody competes in it.

## The three phases of the month

Treat these as three separate operating plans, because the buyer's reason for buying changes completely.

| Phase | What people buy | What you should be doing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The two weeks before | Dates, decorations, tableware, prayer items, iftar catering deposits | Photograph everything, publish everything, take pre-orders |
| Days 1 to 20 | Daily food, iftar boxes, small treats, replenishment | Run a routine. Fewer products, faster turnaround |
| The last ten days and Eid | Gifts, gift boxes, perfume, clothes, sweets in presentation packaging | Cut-offs, caps, and packaging that survives being handed over |

The mistake is running the same catalogue and the same messaging across all three. A shop that sells iftar trays in week one and never repositions for Eid gifting has left the most profitable ten days of its year on the table.

The last ten days are also the charitable heart of the month, and a lot of buying in that period is for other people rather than for the buyer. Gift wrapping stops being an upsell and becomes the product. Presentation, a card, something that can be handed over in a majlis without you having to explain it.

## Delivery is the part that breaks

Delivery windows compress from both ends during Ramadan, and this is where most sellers get hurt.

Riders are fasting too. The hour before maghrib is unusable for last-mile delivery in most Gulf cities because roads are gridlocked and nobody wants a stranger at the door minutes before they break their fast. That removes a chunk of the afternoon. Meanwhile the early morning slot shrinks because everyone is sleeping later.

What you're left with is a usable delivery band roughly from mid-morning to mid-afternoon, and then a second one late in the evening. Two bands instead of one long day. Same number of orders, less time to move them.

Three things follow:

1. **Ask for a delivery window preference at order time**, not after. "Before 3pm" or "after 9pm" as a note on the order saves you two messages per delivery.
2. **Do not promise same-day in the last ten days.** You cannot control the traffic and you cannot control the rider.
3. **If you deliver yourself, batch by area and by band.** Three areas in the morning band, two in the evening band, and nothing at all between 5pm and 8pm.

Setting these expectations in the order flow rather than in a DM afterwards is most of the job. Write the two bands into the product description in plain words, so nobody has to ask, and so nobody is expecting a rider at half past five.

## The Eid cut-off you cannot pin down

One operational fact catches out sellers in their first Ramadan, and it's the reason a generic seasonal checklist won't save you.

Eid al-Fitr's date isn't fixed in advance. Ramadan runs either 29 or 30 days, and which one it is gets confirmed by the local moon-sighting announcement on the evening of the 29th. Plan for a 30-day Ramadan and get a 29-day one, and every order you promised for "the day before Eid" just lost a full day. Sellers who assume 30 spend that night driving.

So build your plan on 29 days and treat the thirtieth as a bonus. Concretely:

- Publish your gift order cut-off as **day 25 of Ramadan**, not "two days before Eid".
- Keep a private buffer: you can still take a handful of orders on days 26 and 27 for buyers you know, but nobody sees that offer on the shop.
- Ask your courier for their last collection date before the Eid holiday, and ask again a week later. In the Gulf they usually can't publish a firm date early for the same reason you can't, and offices close for several days over Eid, so the parcel that misses the last collection sits somewhere for the whole holiday.
- If you can offer collection in person, say so from day 20. It's the only thing that still works after the courier stops.

Write the cut-off into the shop itself, not just into a story that disappears in 24 hours. A line in the product description reading "Orders for Eid delivery close on the 25th of Ramadan" is doing work at 1am when you're asleep.

## What to sell, and what to stop selling

Ramadan rewards narrowing. You have fewer usable hours, and half of them are the hours you'd normally be resting.

Cut your catalogue for the month. If you normally list twenty things, list eight, and make sure the eight are the ones you can produce without thinking. Everything with a fiddly variant, everything that needs a conversation before it ships, everything you make three of a week: take it down until Eid.

Then add the two or three things that only make sense now. A gift box. A larger family size. Something in presentation packaging at a higher price.

The photography needs to happen before the month starts. This is the single piece of advice worth acting on today. You will not have the daylight, the appetite or the free hands to shoot a food photo at three in the afternoon on day twelve, and shooting food you can't taste is genuinely miserable. Shoot the whole Ramadan and Eid range in one session in the week before. If food is your product, [what to photograph when you sell food](/en/blog/what-to-photograph-when-you-sell-food) covers the shot list.

## Prices, discounts and the last ten days

You don't need to discount in the last ten days. Demand is doing the work.

What you might need is a reason for someone to order on day 12 instead of day 24, because day 24 is when your capacity runs out. That's a different tool: an early-order incentive, a free add-on for orders placed before the middle of the month, a slightly better price on a pre-order that lets you buy materials with the money.

If you do run something, run it early and stop it. A discount that's still live on day 26 is a discount on orders you'd have got anyway at full price.

Two pricing notes specific to the Gulf. First, gift buyers are not price-sensitive in the way daily buyers are, so a AED 250 presentation box and a AED 45 everyday item can live in the same shop without either damaging the other. Second, the buyer very often isn't the recipient, which means your product page has to explain what's inside the box to someone who will never open it.

## Worked example: Layla, home baker, Dubai

Layla makes kunafa and maamoul from a home kitchen in Al Barsha. Her regular year is quiet: maybe twelve orders a week, mostly friends of friends, taken through Instagram DMs and paid by bank transfer.

Ramadan is roughly a third of her annual revenue. Her numbers last year, roughly:

- Kunafa tray, AED 120. She can make eleven a day comfortably, fourteen if she starts at 6am.
- Maamoul, box of 20, AED 65. Made in batches of eight boxes.
- Eid gift box, dates and maamoul in a presentation tin, AED 185. Made to order.

Her first Ramadan went badly for one reason: she took Eid gift box orders right up to the last two days and then Eid landed a day earlier than she'd assumed. Nine boxes went out late. Three buyers asked for refunds.

The second year she changed four things:

1. **Published cut-off on day 25**, written into the product description and the shop, not just posted in a story.
2. **Capped the gift box at 40 units** and said so. "40 boxes only" sold them by day 18, which meant she knew her materials order by day 12.
3. **Moved her posting to 9:30pm and 11:30pm**, adjusted every few days as iftar moved.
4. **Stopped taking custom flavours from day 20.** Two products only in the last stretch.

Revenue went up because the cap sold the boxes faster, not because she raised the price. The refunds went to zero. And she slept, which was the actual goal.

## What Sailo does, and what it leaves to you

A Sailo shop gives you a `sailo.store/yourname` link that works as the storefront while Instagram does the discovery and WhatsApp does the conversation, which is how the Gulf already buys. The WhatsApp order arrives pre-written: item, options, address, total. That's genuinely useful at midnight when you're taking your fortieth order of the day and you don't want to type an address back and forth.

Now the honest limit. On bank transfer, cash on delivery and WhatsApp orders, Sailo never touches the money and takes nothing, which is the good news and the bad news at once. **You confirm every payment yourself.** Nobody does it for you. Sailo can't tell you an AED 185 transfer landed in your account, because it never sees it. Only your bank can. During the last ten days, when forty orders come in overnight, that reconciliation is a real job and it belongs to you. Budget twenty minutes a day for it, or you'll be doing it at 3am on the night before Eid with a spreadsheet and a bank app.

## Do this in the week before Ramadan

Block one day. Shoot every product you intend to sell during the month and over Eid, in daylight, in one session. Write the Eid cut-off as day 25 into every gift product description. Ask your courier, in writing, what their last collection is before the Eid holiday and what happens to a parcel that misses it.

Then take down everything you can't make without thinking about it, and put it back after Eid.
