Someone sent you a photo. Your candle on their windowsill, half burned down, next to a mug and a phone charger. The light's wrong and there's washing-up in the background.

That photo will sell more units than the one you spent Saturday morning taking.

So use it. But ask first, in writing, in the same message thread, and say exactly where it's going. Ten seconds of typing is the difference between a photo you can put on a product page for the next two years and a photo you'll have to take down in a hurry. The exact wording is further down, all 41 words of it.

The rest of this is why the worse photo works, what permission actually has to cover, and the four ways this goes wrong.

## The photo you take and the photo they take answer different questions

Your photo answers "what is it". Plain background, whole product in frame, colour accurate. That's a catalogue job and you should still do it, because [product photography for small sellers](/en/blog/how-to-photograph-what-you-sell) is the difference between a listing and a guess.

A customer's photo answers three questions your photo can't:

- **Is this real?** Somebody who isn't you has this thing in their house.
- **How big is it?** A pendant on white card is a shape. A pendant on a collarbone is a size.
- **Does it look like that at my place?** Under kitchen light, on a normal shelf, without a stylist.

That last one is why the mess helps. A buyer looking at a perfectly lit studio image is doing a translation in their head, discounting for the lighting and the props. A buyer looking at your product on someone's cluttered dresser doesn't have to translate anything. The discount's already been applied.

There's a second thing customer photos do that's harder to name. They prove somebody paid. A studio shot proves you own a camera.

## Ask properly, and ask in writing

Permission that lives in your head is not permission. Permission in a voice note is permission you can't find in eight months.

Ask in the thread you're already in. WhatsApp, Instagram DM, email, whatever carried the order. Then leave it there. The thread is the record.

Three rules that matter more than the wording:

1. **Ask before you post, not after.** "Hope you don't mind, I put your photo up" is not a question. It's an announcement with a question mark, and the people who do mind will say nothing and quietly stop buying from you.
2. **Name where it goes.** "On the shop page and Instagram" is permission. "Use it" is not, and when you later put it in a paid ad, you'll know you're outside what they agreed to.
3. **Make no the easy answer.** If declining costs them anything socially, you didn't get consent, you got politeness.

### The message to send

Send this within a day of them sending you the photo, while it's still a conversation and not an archaeology dig.

> Hi Sam, this is a lovely photo, thank you. Would you be okay with me using it on my shop page and Instagram? Happy to credit you or keep you anonymous, whichever you prefer. Totally fine to say no.

Forty-one words. It names the two places, it offers credit as a choice rather than a default, and the last sentence does the work. Without "totally fine to say no", a decent number of people say yes because saying no to a small seller feels rude.

If you want the photo for something bigger, ask for that specifically:

> Hi Sam, would you mind if I used this in a paid Instagram ad as well as on the shop page? It'd run for about a month. No problem at all if not.

And if it's an old photo, from a customer who bought months ago:

> Hi Sam, hope you're well. You sent me a photo of the lamp back in March. I'm redoing my shop page and that photo's better than anything I've taken. Okay to use it? Happy to credit you.

Notice what none of these do. They don't offer a discount in exchange. The moment a photo has a price attached, you've changed a favour into a transaction, and a transaction has terms you haven't written down. Ask plainly. Most people say yes, and the ones who say yes for free are the ones who meant it.

### What to do when they say no

Say thanks and move on in one line. "No problem at all, thanks anyway." Don't explain why it would have been good for you. Don't ask again in a different way three weeks later.

Then keep buying from that customer's goodwill in other ways. Somebody who won't have their kitchen on the internet will still tell three friends about the lamp, which is a different and often better channel, and [word of mouth](/en/blog/turning-one-customer-into-three) doesn't need a photo to work.

### What to do when they don't reply

Nothing. Silence is not yes. This is the most common way sellers end up with a photo they shouldn't have.

## What permission has to cover

Four things, and you can get all four in that one message if you're careful.

| The thing | Why it matters | How to cover it |
|---|---|---|
| Where | A shop page and a paid ad are different asks | Name every surface in the message |
| Faces | A person in shot has to agree, even if they aren't the buyer | Ask about the person, not the photo |
| Children | Almost nobody wants their kid on a business account | Crop, or don't use it |
| Home detail | House numbers, street signs, a visible address label | Look at the background before you post |

The fourth one catches people out. A customer sends a photo of a parcel on their doorstep, and the delivery label is legible, and now their full address is on your Instagram. Check corners. Check reflections in windows and in the product itself if it's glossy.

If there's a child in the photo, the answer is no unless the parent explicitly said yes to that photo, on that surface, in writing. Not "yes to photos generally". This one photo.

## Where a customer photo actually earns its place

Not the top of the product page. That's where the clear photo goes, because a buyer who can't tell what the thing is doesn't get as far as caring whether it's real.

Put customer photos here, in this order of usefulness:

**Second and third image on the product listing.** After the clean shot. This is where scale and reality get answered, and it's where the buyer already is.

**The reply to "is this legit".** You'll get this message. A screenshot of a customer photo plus a line saying "that's from a buyer in Coventry last week" ends the conversation faster than anything you can write about yourself. It's the same job as [looking trustworthy to a stranger who has never bought from you](/en/blog/proving-you-are-a-real-seller).

**A saved Instagram highlight, in date order.** Not a grid post. A highlight, because a hesitant buyer scrolls it in twenty seconds and sees eleven different people's houses. That's evidence in a shape people already know how to read.

**Stories, on the day it arrives.** Reshare it while it's warm, tag them if they said yes to credit, and it does double duty as a nudge to everyone watching. [Selling through Instagram stories](/en/blog/selling-through-instagram-stories) covers the mechanics of that.

Where it doesn't belong: as the only image, as the cover of a paid ad without written ad permission, or edited so heavily it's yours now.

## Ask for the right photo, not just any photo

Most customers won't send a photo unless you ask, and a vague ask gets a vague photo. "Send me a pic" gets you a parcel on a table.

Ask for the thing in use. Specifically:

- **In their space, not on white.** You already have white.
- **With a hand or a body in it** if the product goes on a person.
- **Unstyled.** Say the word. "Don't tidy up, that's the point."

The line that gets the best return rate, said at handover or written on the packing note:

> If you get a second, send me a photo of it at yours. Doesn't need to be tidy. I'm building up some real pictures and yours would be the first one from Leicester.

The "first one from" bit works because it gives them a small specific role. Generic requests get generic compliance.

Roughly half of people will send something. Some of those will be unusable, blurred or dark or with a child in shot. Budget for that.

## Priya's earrings, and the photo she nearly ruined

Priya sells hand-made silver earrings in Leicester. £22 a pair, £3.50 second-class delivery, about 25 to 40 orders a month, mostly through Instagram.

She'd been shooting on a white tile by the window and it looked fine. Then a customer sent a photo of the hammered hoops on, taken in a car, sun through the windscreen, half her face out of frame. Priya asked, got a yes, and put it up as the second image on that listing.

Here's the part worth stealing. She had a second photo from another buyer that she liked more, except it had a pile of school exercise books and a half-eaten toast plate in the background. She cropped both out, tightened in on the earrings, and used the clean version. It sat on the listing for nine days. She swapped in the uncropped one on a whim, and the tidy version has never gone back up.

Her working theory is that the toast was doing the same job as the label on a parcel. It said a real person in a real morning owns this.

Her rules now, written on a sticky note above the desk:

| Rule | Reason |
|---|---|
| Ask the day the photo arrives | The thread's still open |
| Never crop for tidiness, only for privacy | The mess is the evidence |
| Two customer photos maximum per listing | Beyond that they stop reading as individual people |
| Save the yes as a screenshot in the same folder as the photo | Findable in a year |

That last one takes four seconds and it's the one everybody skips.

## Keep a record, because you will not remember

One folder per photo is overkill. One folder for all of them, with files named `name-date`, plus a screenshot of the message where they agreed, is enough.

If you're keeping a spreadsheet of orders anyway, add three columns: photo yes/no, where they agreed to, credit or anonymous. It's less work than it sounds because you're only filling it in for the people who sent photos, which is a small fraction of your buyers.

Two years from now, someone will message asking you to take a photo down. It happens after breakups, job changes, and one memorable category of message that begins "my ex is in that picture". Take it down that day, without arguing, and reply once to say it's done. Being fast about this is worth more than being right about it.

## What this doesn't fix

Customer photos will not carry a product nobody wants. If you've got photos from your first four buyers and no fifth buyer, the photos aren't the constraint, and no amount of social proof will make them one.

They also don't replace a written review. A photo says it exists. A sentence says it was worth £22. You want both, and asking for both in the same message is more efficient than asking twice.

On the Sailo side, one thing worth saying plainly: there's no Sailo app. You'll be doing all of this in a phone browser, cropping in your camera roll first and uploading to the product from there. It works, and it's a couple more taps than a native app would be. If you're on the free plan you've also got 20 product slots, which is fine while you're photographing one listing properly and less fine once you've got four colourways of three things.

## Do this today

Open the thread with your last five customers. Find the ones who already sent a photo, unprompted, and never got asked. There's usually at least one, sometimes three.

Send them the 41-word message. Then, on the next order you pack, write the "doesn't need to be tidy" line on the note before you seal the box, because the ask that goes in the parcel costs nothing and the ask you plan to send later mostly doesn't get sent.
