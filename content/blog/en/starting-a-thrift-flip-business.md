You bought a jacket for $6, sold it for $48, and now you're doing arithmetic in your head about how many jackets that is a month.

The arithmetic is wrong, and it's wrong in a specific way. The $6 isn't your cost. Your cost is the two and a half hours you spent in three stores to find that jacket, plus the twenty minutes of laundry, plus the fifteen minutes of photographing and measuring and writing, plus the four other things you bought that day that haven't sold and might not. Thrift flipping is a sourcing business with a listing bottleneck. Price your time into it from day one or you'll spend a year working for about $11 an hour and calling it a shop.

That's not a reason not to do it. It's the number you need before you decide how.

## The $6 jacket is not your cost

Run one honest week and the picture changes.

Say you make one sourcing trip: 40 minutes of driving across three stores, an hour and forty in the aisles, and you come out with 10 pieces averaging $7.20 each. That's $72 of goods and roughly 2.5 hours gone.

Then the processing. Wash and dry the ten, press the six that need it: call it an hour, most of it waiting, some of it not. Then listing, and this is where people underestimate badly. Doing it properly means photographing front and back and the fabric and the label and the flaws, measuring pit to pit and length and sleeve, writing a description, and setting a price. Twelve to eighteen minutes an item once you're fast. For ten pieces, call it two and a half hours.

Six hours, $72 of stock, ten listings.

Now the sale side, and here's where the optimism usually lives. Suppose all ten sell at an average of $32. That's $320. Take off $72 of goods. Take off marketplace fees at around 13%, so $41. Take off packaging at maybe $0.80 a parcel. Net, roughly $199 for six hours. About $33 an hour, which is a real job.

But they don't all sell. Suppose six of the ten sell inside 90 days. Now you have $192 in, the same $72 of goods because you bought all ten, $25 of fees, $5 of packaging. That's $90 for six hours of work. About $15 an hour, and you still own four pieces that are taking up a rail.

Both of those calculations are honest. The difference between them is entirely sell-through, which means the skill you're actually building is not selling. It's buying.

## What a sourcing hour has to produce

Set a target before you go in, because a thrift store will happily absorb four hours and send you home with two things.

The number worth aiming at: **four sellable pieces per hour in the aisles.** Below that, the trip doesn't pay, no matter how good the two things you found were. Track it for a month, per store, and you'll discover that one of your three stores is quietly wasting your Saturdays.

Some ways to raise that number that cost nothing:

- **Go on restock days.** Every store has a rhythm. Ask an employee when the racks get filled. Most will tell you.
- **Work one section properly instead of the whole store badly.** Men's outerwear and women's blazers, every trip, all the way along the rail. Touch every hanger.
- **Feel, don't look.** You can identify wool, silk, linen and heavy cotton by hand at speed, and those four materials are most of what you're looking for. Reading every tag is four times slower and finds less.
- **Set a walk-out time and keep it.** Ninety minutes in one store, then leave, even if you're mid-rail.
- **Price the drive.** A store 25 minutes away needs to produce roughly twice what the one down the road does.

Bins, where a store's unsold stock goes into bulk containers priced by weight, change the maths again. Paying somewhere around $1.50 a pound means a shirt costs under a dollar, and the sourcing hour becomes brutal but the cost per piece collapses. It's also filthy, competitive and physically hard, and plenty of people who love flipping hate the bins. Try it twice before you decide.

## What to pick up and what to walk past

The habit that separates a profitable rail from a full one is walking past things.

Pick up, almost always:

- **Natural fibres in good condition.** 100% wool, silk, linen, leather, heavy cotton. Check the tag once your hand has already flagged it.
- **Made-in tags from countries that stopped making clothes.** USA, Italy, England, Japan, West Germany. Not proof of value, but it's a strong signal of age.
- **Single-stitch t-shirts**, where the hem and sleeve have one line of stitching rather than two. That's an age marker, and age is most of the value in a graphic tee.
- **Heavy hardware.** Metal zips with a brand stamped on the pull, real horn or shell buttons, a proper lining.
- **Anything outdoor and technical from a brand people trail-run in.** It sells all year and it doesn't need pressing.

Walk past, almost always:

- **Anything with a stain in the middle of the front.** You will not sell it, you will not fix it, and you will look at it for eight months.
- **Yellowed armpits.** Check them under the store's own fluorescent light before you check the label. Underarm yellowing that's invisible on the rack is the most common reason a piece you paid $8 for becomes a piece you can't sell at any price.
- **Smoke.** A musty thrift-store smell washes out. Cigarette smoke, in a wool coat, does not, and everyone learns this once.
- **Broken zips on cheap garments.** A replacement zip and a tailor cost more than the item is worth.
- **Anything you can't identify.** If you don't know what it is, you don't know what it's worth, and looking it up on your phone in the aisle costs four minutes you don't have. Occasionally you'll leave something good. You'll leave far more bad.

And the arithmetic rule that governs everything else: **if you can't list it above about $22, walk away.** Below that, shipping and fees eat the margin and you've done all the same work for six dollars. A $9 t-shirt is not a small win. It's a loss disguised as inventory.

## The flip itself, and when not to bother

"Thrift flipping" gets used two ways. One means buying low and selling as-is. The other means altering the garment. The second one is where beginners lose money, and the reason is easy to state.

Repairs pay. Redesigns rarely do.

| What you do | Time | What it typically adds | Worth it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wash, dry, press | 10 min | Enormous, it's the whole listing | Always |
| Replace a missing button with a matching one | 15 min | $10 to $30 on a blazer | Yes |
| Fix a seam or a small hole | 20 min | Turns unsellable into sellable | Yes |
| Take up a hem | 20 min | Very little, it narrows your buyers | Rarely |
| Crop a tee or a jacket | 10 min | Usually negative | Almost never |
| Tie-dye or overdye | 2 hours plus | Wildly variable, high failure rate | Only if it's your brand |
| Cut up a genuinely old piece | 30 min | Destroys the value | Never |

That last row deserves a paragraph. The single most expensive mistake in this trade is chopping up something collectible because it didn't fit you. A 1970s wool coat with a moth hole is worth more repaired than reimagined. If a piece is old, unusual, or from a name you recognise, sell it as it is and let the buyer decide.

The exception is if the alteration *is* your product. If you're building a brand around remade denim and people follow you for it, that's a different business with different economics, and the alteration time is your value rather than your cost. Just be clear about which business you're in, because the two need different prices and different photographs.

## Listing is the bottleneck, not sourcing

Every flipper has a rail of unlisted finds. Every single one.

> Sourcing feels like the business. Listing is the business. Forty unlisted pieces in the garage are the same as no inventory at all.

Fix it with a rule rather than willpower. **Nothing new comes in until last week's is listed.** It's an unpleasant rule and it doubles most people's revenue in two months, because the bottleneck was never finding things.

Batch it properly. One session of laundry for everything. One session of photographs, same spot, same light, same background, all ten pieces in a row. One session of writing at a table with a tape measure. Doing all three steps for one item at a time is roughly twice as slow as doing each step for ten items, and it feels faster, which is the trap.

Photograph near a window with the ceiling lights off, flat on a sheet or hung on a plain wall, and take the fabric close-up that almost nobody takes. The full method for shooting garments without a model or a mannequin is in [photographing clothes without a model](/en/blog/photographing-clothes-without-a-model), and the condition-photo discipline that stops arguments later is in [how to sell thrift clothes online](/en/blog/selling-thrift-and-vintage-clothes).

Measure everything in inches and put the numbers in the listing. Not the size on the tag. A 1980s large and a 2020s large are different garments, and half the tags are faded anyway.

## Pricing against comps, not against your feelings

You paid $6, so $30 feels like a win. It might also be $28 under what the piece sells for.

Look up sold listings, not active ones. Active listings tell you what people are asking, which is a fantasy. Sold listings tell you what people paid, which is a fact. Search the brand plus the garment type plus a distinguishing detail, filter to sold, and look at the middle of the range rather than the top.

Then price slightly above the middle and be willing to wait. One-of-one stock has no expiry date. The buyer for a 44-inch chest herringbone blazer exists, and they might turn up in October.

Two habits worth having. Price in tiers rather than agonising per item, because you'll otherwise spend your life deciding. And review anything that hasn't sold in 90 days: relist it with better photographs first, then drop the price, and only then bundle it out. Dropping the price first is the reflex and it's usually the wrong one, because the most common reason a good piece doesn't sell is that the first photograph is bad. The wider question of what price a made or found thing should carry is worked through in [how to price what you make](/en/blog/how-to-price-what-you-make).

## Where to sell, and when your own link starts to matter

Start on the marketplaces. That's where the buyers are, the search works, and you don't have to bring anyone.

You pay for it. Roughly 13% or so in fees on the big resale platforms, plus payment processing, and the fee comes off the shipping too on some of them. On $320 of sales that's $40 a month you're renting from the platform.

Your own link is the other half, and it's not a replacement, it's an addition. The people who already follow you, who saw the piece on a story before it was listed, who want to buy the thing you posted this morning: they're the ones you should not be paying 13% to reach. Post the piece, put the link in your bio, take the order directly, keep the fee.

The honest way to think about it is that marketplaces sell to strangers and your own link sells to people who already know you. You want both. The circumstances where one genuinely beats the other are laid out in [when a marketplace beats your own shop](/en/blog/when-a-marketplace-beats-your-own-shop).

Here's the limitation that matters for this specific trade. Sailo's free plan caps you at 20 products. Thrift stock is one-of-one, so 20 products is 20 garments, and if you're listing ten a week you've filled it in a fortnight. The Pro plan at $9.99 a month takes you to 250, which is roughly what an active solo flipper carries. That's a real cost and you should put it in the spreadsheet next to the fees, not discover it in week three.

The other limitation, and it's a good one: if all your selling happens on a marketplace and you have no audience anywhere, your own shop link will get about four visitors this month. A shop is not traffic. Build the first, then the second, and don't be surprised by the order.

## Worked example: Devon, Columbus, Ohio

Devon flips menswear part time, around a full-time job. Two sourcing trips a week, Tuesday evening and Saturday morning.

A typical month: eight trips, about 18 hours in stores and driving, 74 pieces bought at an average of $6.80, so $503 of goods. Laundry and processing about 6 hours. Listing about 16 hours, which is the number that shocked him when he first counted it.

Of the 74, he lists 68 and bins or donates 6 after finding flaws at home. In the same month he sells 51 pieces, some from this month's buys and some from the standing rail, at an average of $34. That's $1,734.

Fees at 13% take $225. Shipping is paid by buyers but he eats about $60 in the difference between charged and actual. Packaging is $45. Against the $503 of goods, he clears roughly $900 for the month.

Forty hours of work. About $22 an hour, on top of a job, with a rail of 90 unsold pieces in the basement that represents about $600 of buried cash.

Two things he changed in year two. He stopped buying anything he couldn't list above $25, which cut his purchases by a third and raised his average sale price by $9. And he introduced the rule that nothing new comes home until the previous trip is listed, which felt like restraint and was actually just capacity. His monthly revenue went up while his hours went down, which is the only kind of improvement worth chasing. Working out whether any of this is actually profitable, rather than just busy, has its own method in [working out if you are actually profitable](/en/blog/working-out-if-you-are-actually-profitable).

## What to do next

Before your next sourcing trip, start a note on your phone with four columns: date, store, hours, sellable pieces found. Fill it in for one month. Don't change anything else.

At the end of the month you'll be able to see which store pays and which one is a hobby. Drop the hobby.

Then set your floor price, write it down, and hold it in the aisle. If it can't list above $22, it goes back on the rail, no matter how good it feels in your hand.
