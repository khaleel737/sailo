"I bought a white backdrop off the internet and my photos still look wrong."

They do, and the backdrop is the reason. The best background for a product photo is a plain, matte, mid-tone surface that contrasts with the product, curves up behind it with no visible edge where the table meets the wall, and has nothing on it worth looking at. That's usually a sheet of grey or off-white card costing about KSh 200, a painted board, or a plain wall. White is the default everyone reaches for and it's wrong most of the time, for a specific technical reason worth understanding.

## Why white goes wrong

Your phone doesn't know what colour the background is meant to be. It measures the light in the frame and exposes so the average comes out mid-grey.

Point it at a white sheet of paper filling most of the frame and it will darken everything until that paper is grey. Then you look at the result, decide the photo is dull, and brighten it in editing. Which works, except that as you brighten the background towards white, every pale edge of your product dissolves into it. A cream candle on white paper stops having a left side. A silver ring loses its outline. You end up with a photo that looks like a hole.

That's the mechanical problem. There are three more.

**White shows everything.** Dust, a hair, a crease in the paper, the shadow of your own hand, a scuff from where you put a mug down last Tuesday. Mid-grey hides all of it. White broadcasts it.

**White needs a lot of light.** A properly white background wants more light on the background than on the product, which means two light sources. One window is one light source. This is why professional white-background work is done in a studio and why home attempts at it look grey and sad.

**A cut-out white background reads as a reseller.** This is the part nobody says. Products floating on pure white look like marketplace listings, because that's where you see them. If you're selling something handmade, that aesthetic actively works against you. It says "imported and resold", not "made by a person in a room".

White is genuinely right in three situations: a marketplace that requires it in its listing rules, technical or industrial products where the shape is the whole message, and a shop where every product is very dark and very simple. Otherwise, reach for grey.

## Contrast is the actual rule

Forget colour theory. The rule is one line: the background should be a different tone from the product.

| Product | Background that works | Background that fails |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pale, cream, white, silver | Mid-grey, warm stone, deep green, charcoal | White, pale wood, cream linen |
| Dark, black, navy, dark leather | Off-white, light grey, pale plaster, sand | Black, dark wood, slate |
| Bright and saturated | Neutral grey, always | Any other saturated colour |
| Transparent or glass | Mid-tone with a bright edge behind it | White, black, anything busy |
| Mixed range, many colours | One mid-grey for everything | A different surface per product |

If you sell one thing in fifteen colours, mid-grey is the only answer, because it's the only surface that works for all fifteen. Choosing a background per product is how a catalogue ends up looking like fifteen different shops.

## Getting rid of the horizon line matters more than the colour does

The single biggest visual tell of an amateur product photo isn't the colour of the surface. It's the horizon line.

If your photo has a visible edge where the table meets the wall, the eye reads a room. Rooms have context, and context competes with the product. Curve the surface instead: take a sheet of card, lay the bottom half flat on the table, and lean the top half up against the wall or a couple of books so it bends rather than folds. The transition becomes a soft gradient with no line in it and the product looks like it's floating in tone.

That curve is the whole difference between a photo taken at home and a photo that could have been taken anywhere. It costs nothing and takes fifteen seconds.

If you can't curve it, get the edge out of the frame entirely by shooting from above. A top-down shot has no horizon by definition, which is why flat lays are so forgiving.

## Five backgrounds that work, cheapest first

**A sheet of matte card.** A1 or A2, in mid-grey or a warm off-white. Roughly KSh 200, ₹90, ₦2,000, ₱120, £2.50 or $3. Buy three, not one, because the first one gets a footprint on it in week three and you'll keep using it anyway. Matte, not glossy. Gloss reflects the window straight back at the camera.

**A painted board.** An offcut of MDF or plywood, one tester pot of matte emulsion, two coats. Grey, deep green, terracotta, or a soft off-black. Costs about £6 and lasts years, wipes clean, doesn't crease, and gives you a slightly textured surface that reads as expensive on camera.

**A plain painted wall in your house.** Free. Find a wall near a window that isn't brilliant white and isn't textured. Push a table against it and you have a permanent setup. The tape mark on the floor is the only thing you need to remember.

**Unbleached linen or heavy cotton, ironed.** Good for food, soap, textiles, anything that wants softness. It must be ironed. A crease in fabric reads as scruffy in a way a scuff on card doesn't, because fabric creases look like laundry.

**A plain floor tile, paving slab or terrazzo offcut.** Under KSh 400 from any hardware yard. Heavy, permanent, and the slight texture is genuinely attractive for jewellery, ceramics and cosmetics. Get one with no grout line in it.

## Surfaces that always look cheap

- **Patterned tablecloth or oilcloth.** The pattern wins. It always wins.
- **Granite or speckled worktop.** Visual noise at exactly the scale of a small product.
- **Stainless steel.** Reflects you, the ceiling, and every fingerprint from the last week.
- **Glossy tiles with grout lines.** The grid pulls the eye and the gloss doubles your light source.
- **Carpet.** Fibres at product scale read as fluff.
- **A bedsheet thrown over something.** Creases and slack. If you use fabric, it's flat and it's ironed.
- **Printed vinyl "marble" or "wood" backdrops.** They photograph as printed paper, because that's what they are, and the repeat becomes obvious the moment you shoot two products on the same sheet.
- **Grass, gravel and garden furniture.** Fine for one lifestyle shot. Terrible as your standard, because you can't reproduce the light in February.

## Colour backgrounds, and the risk they carry

A colour behind a product can be excellent. It can also date your entire catalogue in a single season, and it can shift how buyers perceive your product's colour, which turns into refunds.

Two rules if you go colour.

**Desaturate it.** Whatever colour you pick, use the muted version. A dusty sage, a chalky terracotta, a muted blue. Saturated colours fight the product and bounce coloured light onto it, so your white soap picks up a green cast on one side and the buyer receives something that doesn't match.

**Pick one and keep it for a year.** The value of a coloured background is entirely in consistency. One colour across every product makes a grid look designed. Three colours makes it look indecisive.

If your product's own colour is the main thing you're selling, the safest background is always neutral, and the colour goes in the words underneath. Words survive a badly calibrated screen. Photographs don't, and colour drift is one of the more expensive mistakes a small shop makes: it comes back as a return you pay postage on twice.

## Props, and the ten per cent rule

A prop is allowed to occupy about a tenth of the frame and it has to be doing a job.

Jobs a prop can legitimately do: show scale, show what the product is for, show a material, or show that a human being is involved. A hand qualifies. A coin qualifies. A cup of coffee next to a notebook does not, unless you sell notebooks to people who drink coffee, which is everyone, which is the problem.

The test: cover the prop with your thumb. If the photo is worse without it, keep it. If it's the same, it was decoration and it's stealing attention from the product.

Dried flowers, in particular, have been in every third handmade product photo for years. They no longer read as styling. They read as a stock photo.

## Shadows are part of the background

The shadow the product casts is doing more work than the surface it falls on.

A soft shadow running away from the window grounds the object, gives it weight, and tells the eye how the light works. No shadow at all makes a product look pasted on. A hard black shadow with a sharp edge looks like a flash went off.

To soften a shadow, move the product further from the window, or put a piece of white card on the shadow side about 30cm away to bounce light back in. That white card is the cheapest piece of studio equipment in existence and it fixes about half of all product photos.

To keep a shadow but stop it going black, shoot on an overcast day. Grey sky is a giant softbox, and the shadow it produces is exactly the one you want.

## Two backgrounds, maximum, for the whole shop

Pick one light and one dark. Photograph pale products on the dark one and dark products on the light one. Never introduce a third without a reason you could say out loud.

A catalogue photographed on two surfaces looks like a shop. A catalogue photographed on nine surfaces looks like a folder, and the inconsistency is the thing buyers notice without being able to name it. This is the same argument as shooting everything in a single session, and both come down to the same point: consistency across the grid is invisible until it's absent. ## The four-minute test

Stop reading and do this, because opinions about backgrounds are worthless next to four photographs.

Take one product. Photograph it, in the same light, from the same distance, on four surfaces you already own: a sheet of white paper, a mid-grey or brown surface, your kitchen table, and whatever wall is nearest the window.

Now look at the four images as thumbnails, not full size. Small, in a row, the way a buyer sees them.

One of them will be obviously better and it usually isn't the one you expected. In most homes it's the wall.

Do it once, decide, and mark the spot. That's your background settled for the next two years, and you never have to think about it again.

## Worked example: leather sandals, Mombasa, KSh 2,800

Halima makes leather sandals in Mombasa and sells them at KSh 2,800, around 35 pairs a month, mostly through Instagram with payment to an M-Pesa till number, which is how her buyers expect to pay and needs no gateway at all.

Her sandals are tan and dark brown leather. Her original photos were shot on the concrete floor of her workshop, which sounds like it should work and didn't: the concrete was almost exactly the same tone as the tan leather, so the sandal's outline disappeared and every photo looked flat.

She changed one thing. A sheet of pale grey card, 100 bob from a stationers near the market, laid on a bench by the door where the light comes in sideways.

The tan sandals now had an edge. The dark brown ones, which had always photographed fine on the concrete, looked slightly worse on the grey, so she kept a scrap of the old concrete floor idea in the form of a paving slab for those. Two surfaces, chosen by tone, decided in one afternoon.

What she didn't expect: the grey card also fixed the colour. On concrete, her tan leather photographed with a faint blue-green cast picked up from the floor, and buyers had been asking whether the tan was "more brown or more orange". After the change, that question stopped, and so did the two returns a month she'd been quietly absorbing at KSh 300 of courier cost each.

That's KSh 600 a month recovered by a sheet of card that cost KSh 100 once.

## The honest limitation

A background fixes a photograph that was nearly right. It does nothing for a photo taken at night under a ceiling bulb, and it can't rescue a product that isn't ready to be sold.

On the Sailo side, there's no native app, so every one of these images gets uploaded through a mobile browser. Twenty products, six photos each, on a patchy connection, is a genuinely slow evening. Do it sitting down, on wifi, in one session, and resize your photos before you start rather than pushing 4MB files up one at a time.

## What to do next

Run the four-minute test today with one product and four surfaces. Look at the thumbnails, not the full-size images, and pick the winner.

Then buy three sheets of card in that tone, mark the spot on the floor where you stood, and write down which window and roughly what time. In November you will not remember, and reproducing a July setup from memory is how a consistent catalogue quietly falls apart.
